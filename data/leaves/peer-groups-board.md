**Short answer.** For a new owner the practical options are an informal advisory board or a peer group of other owners, not a formal board of directors. A peer group gives you people facing the same problems and a place to be candid; an advisory board gives you specific expertise and accountability. A formal board with legal authority makes sense mainly when you have outside investors who expect one or a structure that requires it.

### Three different things

- **Board of directors.** The legal governing body of a corporation (an LLC may have managers or a similar body instead). Directors have legal duties and authority set by your state's law and your company documents, and investors may have the right to appoint some of them. Changing who controls decisions is a legal question; ask your lawyer.
- **Advisory board.** A few people you invite to advise you, with no legal authority. You can choose them for specific expertise (your industry, finance, sales, operations) and meet quarterly or so. You can pay them, give them a small equity grant, or simply ask.
- **Peer group.** Owners of other, non-competing businesses who meet regularly to share problems, numbers and advice in confidence. There are paid organized groups, industry associations' owner groups and informal groups of friends who own businesses.

### What each is good for

**A peer group** is useful when you want:

- People who understand the job of owning a business, which employees, family and the seller can't fully share. See [isolation and burnout](/q/owner-wellbeing).
- Honest feedback on decisions such as hiring, pricing or letting someone go.
- Accountability for doing what you said you'd do.
- Ideas from owners in different industries.

**An advisory board** is useful when you want:

- Specific expertise you lack, such as an experienced operator in your industry or someone who has bought and integrated businesses.
- Regular, structured review of your results and plans.
- Credibility with lenders or investors, in some cases.

**A formal board** is mainly for when you have outside investors (as in a search fund or a deal with equity partners), when the company documents or an investor agreement require one, or when you plan to bring in outside capital later.

### Setting up an advisory board

1. Decide what you need help with, and choose two to four people with that experience who will be candid with you.
2. Write down the arrangement: meeting frequency, time commitment, compensation, confidentiality, and that they have no authority to act for the company.
3. Send a short package before each meeting: financial results, scorecard, key decisions and questions.
4. Review whether it's working after a year, and change members as needs change.

If you grant equity or options to advisors, get tax and legal advice first.

### Choosing a peer group

Ask who's in it (size of business, industry, stage), how often it meets, what it costs, how confidentiality works, and whether there are competitors you'd need to avoid. Visit a meeting if you can. Fit matters: a group of owners of much larger companies, or of very different businesses, may not help as much.

### Common mistakes

- Filling an advisory board with friends who won't disagree with you.
- Giving advisors equity without a written agreement or vesting.
- Joining a group, then not preparing or showing up.
