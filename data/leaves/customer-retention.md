**Short answer.** Keep the customer's experience the same while you earn their trust: same people, same service, same prices and terms at first. Have the seller introduce you to the important customers in person or by phone, in an order you plan ahead, and give each one a reason to believe service will hold or improve. Watch early signs of drift (fewer orders, slower payment, unanswered calls) and act on them quickly.

### Start with a list, not an announcement

Before closing, or in the first days after, sort customers into groups:

- **Relationship customers.** The ones who buy because of the seller or a particular employee. These need a personal introduction.
- **Contract customers.** Check whether their contracts let them leave, renegotiate or refuse consent when the owner changes. In an asset purchase the contract itself may need to be assigned to you. See [customer concentration](/q/customer-concentration) for how much rides on the biggest names.
- **Everyone else.** A short, plain message may be enough, if any is needed at all.

Rank the first two groups by revenue and by how exposed they are (a single contact, a competitor courting them, a contract coming up for renewal). That ranking sets the order of calls.

### The introduction

The seller's endorsement is one of the most useful things you can borrow in the first months. Agree with the seller, in writing if possible, which customers they'll introduce you to and by when. This belongs in the [transition plan](/q/seller-transition-period) or the [consulting agreement](/q/seller-consulting-agreement).

In each conversation:

- Let the seller say why they chose you and why the customer is in good hands.
- Say what is not changing: the people they deal with, pricing, terms, delivery.
- Ask what they value, what annoys them and what they wish the business did. Write it down and follow up on one thing quickly.
- Give them your direct contact.

### Protect the people customers know

Some customers' relationship is with an employee (an account manager, a technician, an estimator), not only with the owner. Losing that person can lose the account. Identify these people early and treat keeping them as part of keeping customers. See [keeping employees](/q/employee-retention).

### Don't give customers a reason to shop around

The change of owner is a natural moment for a customer to look at alternatives. Avoid adding reasons in the first months:

- No surprise price increases or new fees. See [raising prices](/q/raise-prices).
- No change of invoices, bank details, phone numbers or ordering process without warning and an explanation. A sudden change of payment instructions can also look like fraud to a customer.
- No drop in response times while you learn the business.

If you plan a rebrand, wait until relationships are secure. See [integration and rebranding](/q/integration).

### Measure it

Track, by customer, monthly revenue, order frequency and days to pay, starting with the months before closing so you have a baseline. Flag any large customer whose orders fall or whose payments slow, and call them. A quarterly check-in call with the top customers costs little and gives early warning.

### Common mistakes

- Assuming the customer list transfers with the business. It transfers only if customers keep choosing you.
- Leaving introductions to the seller's goodwill with no list or deadline.
- Announcing big changes in the first conversation.
- Treating a quiet customer as a happy one.

See also [what not to change right away](/q/dont-change-too-fast) and [the first 100 days](/q/first-100-days-plan).
