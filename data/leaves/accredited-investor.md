**Short answer.** Under the SEC's Regulation D, an individual is an accredited investor with net worth over $1 million (alone or with a spouse or spousal equivalent), not counting their primary residence,[^ecfr-17-cfr-230-501-accredited-net-worth][^ecfr-17-cfr-230-501-primary-residence] or income over $200,000 in each of the last two years ($300,000 joint) with a reasonable expectation of the same this year.[^ecfr-17-cfr-230-501-accredited-income] Certain professional licenses, insiders of the company, and entities above set asset levels also qualify. It matters because it decides who you can raise from and how.

### Individuals

- **Net worth.** Over $1,000,000, alone or with a spouse or spousal equivalent.[^ecfr-17-cfr-230-501-accredited-net-worth] The primary residence isn't counted as an asset.[^ecfr-17-cfr-230-501-primary-residence]
- **Income.** Over $200,000 individually, or $300,000 jointly with a spouse or spousal equivalent, in each of the two most recent years, with a reasonable expectation of the same in the current year.[^ecfr-17-cfr-230-501-accredited-income]
- **Professional certifications.** Anyone holding, in good standing, a certification or credential the SEC has designated. The SEC posts the designated list on its website, so check it there.[^ecfr-17-cfr-230-501-professional-certifications]
- **Insiders.** Directors, executive officers and general partners of the company selling the securities count for that offering.[^ecfr-17-cfr-230-501-insiders]

### Entities

- A corporation, partnership, LLC or similar entity with total assets over $5,000,000, not formed to buy the securities.[^ecfr-17-cfr-230-501-entity-assets]
- Any entity whose equity owners are all accredited investors.[^ecfr-17-cfr-230-501-all-owners-accredited]
- A family office with more than $5 million under management that meets the rule's other conditions.[^ecfr-17-cfr-230-501-family-office]

The rule lists other categories too, such as banks, registered advisers and certain trusts and plans; read the full definition when an investor is an institution or trust.

### Why it matters for your raise

- **Rule 506(c)** lets you advertise, but every purchaser must be accredited[^ecfr-17-cfr-230-506-c-accredited] and you must take reasonable steps to verify it, not just accept a checkbox.[^ecfr-17-cfr-230-506-c-verification]
- **Rule 506(b)** bars advertising but allows up to 35 non-accredited purchasers in a 90-day period;[^ecfr-17-cfr-230-506-b-purchasers] accredited investors aren't counted toward that limit.[^ecfr-17-cfr-230-501-accredited-excluded] If you sell to anyone non-accredited, you must give them specified disclosure information before the sale,[^ecfr-17-cfr-230-502-information] which adds cost.

See [Rule 506(b) or 506(c)](/q/506b-506c-securities).

### Practical points

- Use a written investor questionnaire for every investor.
- For 506(c), the rule lists ways to verify, such as reviewing IRS forms showing two years of income, or getting written confirmation dated within three months from a registered broker-dealer, registered investment adviser, lawyer or CPA.[^ecfr-17-cfr-230-506-c-methods] Use a process your counsel approves.
- Re-check status for each new offering. Net worth and income change.
- Watch the residence math: the home isn't an asset,[^ecfr-17-cfr-230-501-primary-residence] and a mortgage on it up to the home's value isn't a liability, except that any increase in that debt in the 60 days before the sale counts against net worth.[^ecfr-17-cfr-230-501-residence-debt]

### A change under consideration

In October 2026 the SEC asked for public comment, through December 4, 2026, on whether to designate a CPA license as a qualifying credential for accredited investor status.[^sec-notice-cpa-accredited-2026] That's a notice, not a final order. Check the SEC's current list of designated credentials before you rely on it.
