**Short answer.** Only if you know exactly why it's struggling, you're confident you can fix that specific problem, you're paying a price that reflects the trouble, and you have the cash to survive while you fix it. Turnarounds can be bought cheaply, but they're hard to finance with an SBA loan, can take more time and money than planned, and leave little room for error. For a first acquisition, a stable business is the lower-risk path.

**Why financing is harder**

Under SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026):

- An SBA lender tests debt coverage on historical results: the last fiscal year or the average of the last two, at 1.25:1 for an initial acquisition.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-dsc-ratio] A business losing money or barely breaking even may not support much debt.
- The lender must look at your turnaround plan but can't rely on projections to meet coverage (aside from a narrow special purpose property exception).[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-projections]
- The loan must be repaid from cash flow; the lender must decline a loan the cash flow can't support, whatever the collateral.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-cash-flow-repayment]
- If you pay more than the independent valuation supports, the difference must come from equity.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-valuation-gap-equity]

So a turnaround may need more of the buyer's own cash, seller financing, asset-based loans, or a price low enough that the assets carry it. If you guarantee whatever debt there is, the risk is personal.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-personal-guaranty]

**Diagnose before you price**

Separate fixable causes from structural ones:

- **Causes a new owner may be able to fix:** an owner who's checked out, poor pricing, weak sales effort, bad bookkeeping, a bad manager, a lost customer that can be replaced.
- **Causes that are harder to fix:** a shrinking market, a technology shift, a lost license or franchise, a bad location with a long lease, a damaged reputation, legal or tax problems.

Ask what the owner has already tried, and why it didn't work. Talk to employees, customers and suppliers. See [customer calls](/q/customer-dd-calls) and [AI disruption](/q/ai-disruption).

**What to watch for**

- **Unpaid liabilities.** Back taxes, payroll taxes, unpaid suppliers, liens, deferred maintenance, customer deposits for work not done.
- **Staff and customers already leaving.**
- **A seller under pressure**, who may disclose less.

An asset purchase can leave many of the company's past liabilities with the seller, while buying the company itself brings them along. See [asset vs. stock purchase](/q/asset-vs-stock), and have a lawyer review the structure.

**Pricing a turnaround**

- Start from the assets and the current earnings, not what the business earned at its peak.
- Subtract the cost of the fix: new hires, equipment, marketing, catching up on maintenance and payables.
- Add your own time, and the losses you'll fund while fixing it.
- Keep a cash reserve sized for a fix that takes twice as long as planned.

**Questions to answer honestly**

- Have you fixed this kind of problem before, in this kind of business?
- How many months can you fund losses without new money?
- What will you do if the fix doesn't work?
