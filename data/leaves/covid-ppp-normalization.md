**Short answer.** Strip out anything that won't happen again, in both directions. Forgiven loans, grants and tax credits are not earnings from operations, so remove them from income. A year that was unusually bad or unusually good because of a one-time event should be explained and, if needed, set aside when judging what the business normally earns. Then check that any pandemic-era credits the business claimed were proper, because problems can follow the company.

**Government money is not operating income**

The Paycheck Protection Program (PPP) ended May 31, 2021, and SBA forgave loans that met its employee retention and eligible-expense criteria.[^sba-ppp-ended] Forgiveness was a one-time event tied to those years. However the seller's books recorded it, take it out when measuring what the business earns from customers. The same goes for other grants, emergency loans that were forgiven, and refundable tax credits.

Under SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), SBA lenders may adjust cash flow for non-recurring income,[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-adjustment-items] and a required quality of earnings report must document non-recurring revenue and expenses.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-qoe-revenue-quality]

**The Employee Retention Credit**

The Employee Retention Credit (ERC) was a pandemic payroll tax credit. The IRS is reviewing ERC claims closely, and an incorrect claim must be repaid, possibly with penalties and interest.[^irs-erc-improper-claims] Ask whether the business claimed it, how much, who prepared the claim, and whether the IRS has paid, questioned or disallowed it. If you buy the company itself rather than its assets, an improper claim can become your problem. Your CPA and lawyer should look at this; see [tax due diligence](/q/tax-dd).

**Unusual years in the trend**

Some businesses had a surge in demand during the pandemic and others shut down for months. Neither kind of year shows normal earnings. When reviewing:

1. Line up each year's revenue, gross margin and owner earnings, and mark what was unusual about each.
2. Ask what changed: closures, demand surges, supply shortages, price increases, staff changes.
3. Look for whether a surge has faded since. A strong year followed by steady declines is a warning, not a base.
4. Use the years that look most like the business you'll actually run.

**How the lender sees it**

An SBA lender reviews the three most recent year-ends[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-three-years] and tests coverage on the last fiscal year or the average of the last two.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-dsc-ratio] As years pass, pandemic years drop out of that window, but an unusual recent year can still affect what the lender will lend.

**Other one-offs to normalize**

The same method applies to a lawsuit settlement, insurance payout, a one-time large contract, a move, or a fire. See [add-backs](/q/add-backs) for expenses and [TTM and trends](/q/trends-ttm) for weighing years.
