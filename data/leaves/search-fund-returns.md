**Short answer.** Stanford GSB publishes a periodic search fund study that reports on the financial returns of search funds formed in the United States and Canada since 1984; the 2026 edition covers data through December 31, 2025.[^stanford-gsb-2026-search-fund-study] Read the figures there rather than secondhand. What matters as much as the headline number is how search fund returns are built: investors lose the search capital on funds that never buy, and an aggregate figure can hide a wide spread between funds. We don't repeat return figures here because the full report isn't openly accessible for us to quote.

### How returns are built

A search fund is an investment vehicle in which investors back an entrepreneur to find, buy, run and grow a private company.[^stanford-gsb-search-fund-definition] Investor returns come in stages:

1. **Search capital.** Investors fund the searcher's salary and costs while they look. If no deal closes, that money is gone.
2. **Acquisition capital.** If the searcher finds a company, investors decide one by one whether to fund the purchase. The search capital may convert into the acquisition with a premium (a "step-up").
3. **Operating years.** The company pays down debt and, ideally, grows earnings.
4. **Exit or recapitalization.** Much of the investors' return arrives when the company is sold or refinanced. The searcher's performance-based equity comes out of the same pot. See [searcher equity](/q/searcher-economics).

### Reading any return number

- **Portfolio or single fund?** Aggregate returns across all funds can differ sharply from the median fund. A few large winners can lift the average far above what a typical investor saw.
- **Including failed searches?** Some figures count only funds that made an acquisition.
- **IRR or multiple?** An internal rate of return (IRR) is time-weighted; a multiple of invested capital (MOIC) shows total cash back per dollar. A quick small win can have a high IRR and a low multiple.
- **Gross or net?** Before or after fees, the searcher's carry and taxes.
- **Which years?** Returns vary with entry prices and interest rates, so older cohorts may not predict newer ones.
- **Who's reporting?** Self-reported data from participants may miss funds that stopped responding.

### What this means for you

- **As an investor:** one search fund is a concentrated bet. Decide how you'd size it and whether you'd have money to follow on if the searcher finds a good company.
- **As a searcher raising money:** don't quote headline returns as a promise. Show investors why your deal should do well. See [what investors look for](/q/what-investors-want).
- **As a self-funded buyer:** these studies describe investor-backed search funds; your own risk and return depend on your leverage, personal guarantee and how much equity you keep. See [self-funded search](/q/self-funded-search).

The Stanford study covers funds formed in the United States and Canada,[^stanford-gsb-2026-search-fund-study] so check a study's scope before comparing figures.
