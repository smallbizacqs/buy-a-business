**Short answer.** Representations and warranties insurance (RWI) is a policy, generally bought by the buyer, that pays for losses caused by a breach of the seller's representations in the purchase agreement. It exists for smaller deals, but its fixed costs (a minimum premium, underwriting fees and a retention you bear first) and its diligence requirements make it hard to justify at the small end of the market. In small deals, the practical alternatives are an escrow or holdback, a seller note you can offset against, and a well-drafted indemnity. Ask an insurance broker who places RWI whether your deal is large enough to quote before you plan around it.

### How it works

- **What it covers.** Losses from breaches of the seller's representations, such as financial statements that were wrong or taxes that weren't paid, that you didn't know about at signing. See [reps and warranties](/q/reps-warranties).
- **Who buys it.** A buy-side policy is bought by the buyer, who claims against the insurer instead of, or before, the seller. That can matter when the seller is someone you'll keep working with, or has little money left after closing.
- **The retention.** Like a deductible, it's the amount of loss you absorb before the policy pays. Some deals split it with the seller through a small escrow.
- **Limits.** The policy pays up to a set amount chosen when you buy it.
- **Underwriting.** The insurer reviews your diligence (financial, tax and legal reports) and asks questions on a call. Areas you didn't examine are likely to be excluded.

### What it may not cover

- Anything you knew about before signing.
- Problems found between signing and closing, which policies can exclude.
- Purchase price and working capital adjustments.
- Forward-looking statements and projections.
- Specific exclusions the insurer adds for the deal or the industry, such as areas of known risk or areas your diligence didn't cover.

### Why it's hard to justify in small deals

- **Fixed costs.** Minimum premiums, underwriting fees and broker fees don't shrink in proportion to the price, so on a small deal they can be a large share of the price.
- **Diligence it expects.** Underwriters look for a quality of earnings report, legal diligence and tax review. A small deal may not have all three, which can lead to exclusions or no quote.
- **The retention.** On a small deal, the retention can be close to the size of the claims you're most worried about, leaving little the policy would actually pay.

Get a quote rather than assuming either way; the market changes, and what's available depends on the deal, the industry and the diligence you've done.

### Alternatives in small deals

- **Escrow or holdback.** Part of the price held back for a period to cover claims. See [escrows and holdbacks](/q/escrow-holdback).
- **Offset against a seller note.** The right to reduce payments on the seller's note by the amount of a valid claim. Check how this interacts with your lender's rules if the note is on standby. See [seller notes on standby](/q/seller-note-standby).
- **A specific indemnity** for a known issue, such as an open tax question, with its own cap and duration. See [indemnification](/q/indemnification).
- **Better diligence.** Finding a problem before closing is cheaper than insuring it.

### Questions for a broker

- What's the smallest deal size you can place, and what's the minimum premium?
- What retention and limit would be offered for this deal?
- What diligence would the insurer expect to see?
- What exclusions are likely for this industry?
- How long does underwriting take, and can it fit my closing timeline?

Insurance for the business itself (liability, property, life insurance for the lender) is a separate subject; see [insurance](/q/insurance-review).
