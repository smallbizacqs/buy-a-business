**Short answer.** Yes, with your own funds, investors or conventional financing: you buy a majority stake now, and an agreement sets when and at what price you buy the rest. With an SBA 7(a) loan, it's harder. An outside buyer becoming the majority owner is an "initial acquisition," and in that case the seller can't keep a stake. Ask your lender how they'd classify the deal before you propose it.

**How a staged purchase works (outside SBA financing)**

You buy, say, a majority of the shares or units now. An operating agreement or shareholders' agreement then sets:

- **Call option**: your right to buy the rest, after a date or on an event.
- **Put option**: the seller's right to make you buy the rest.
- **Price formula**: a fixed price, a multiple of trailing earnings, or an appraisal, and which earnings definition applies. Spell out how adjustments, debt and cash are treated.
- **Timing and payment**: when the second purchase can happen, and whether it's paid in cash or over time.
- **Governance meanwhile**: what you decide alone and what needs the seller's consent, board seats, information rights, distributions (including tax distributions).
- **Events**: death, disability, divorce, the seller leaving the business, a sale to a third party (drag-along and tag-along rights).
- **The seller's role and non-compete** while they're still an owner.

**Why buyers and sellers like it**

- Less cash needed up front.
- The seller stays invested in how the business does.
- The seller can ease out over time.

**What can go wrong**

- A price formula based on future earnings gives the seller a reason to push for short-term profit and gives you a reason to show lower profit. Choose the formula carefully.
- Disagreements between a majority and a minority owner who used to run everything.
- A put option you can't fund when it's exercised.

**With an SBA 7(a) loan**

Under SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026):

- A deal that makes a newcomer (or someone employed by the business for under 24 months) the majority or largest owner is an initial acquisition.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-initial-acquisition-definition] In an initial acquisition the seller can't remain as an officer, director, stockholder or employee; they may consult for up to 24 months in total.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-seller-consultant]
- SBA's partial change of ownership category (an owner buyout) does let a seller stay on as an owner,[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-partial-seller-stays] but a person not employed by the business for at least 24 months can buy less than 50% and can't become the largest shareholder.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-owner-buyout-outsider-limit]
- In a partial change, a selling owner who keeps less than 20% must guarantee the full loan for at least two years after final disbursement,[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-partial-change-guaranty] and anyone owning 20% or more must give an unlimited guarantee.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-personal-guaranty]

So for an outside buyer, "majority now with SBA, rest later" generally doesn't fit. Some buyers instead buy 100% with SBA financing and use a seller note for part of the price. See [partial changes of ownership](/q/partial-change-of-ownership), [rollover equity](/q/rollover-equity) and [seller financing](/q/seller-financing-basics). SBA rules change; confirm with your lender and the current SOP.

The agreements behind a staged purchase are complex; your lawyer and CPA should draft and review them.
