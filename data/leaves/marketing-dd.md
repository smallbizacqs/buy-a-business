**Short answer.** Find out, with numbers, where each year's new customers came from and what each source cost: referrals, repeat business, search, paid ads, a marketplace, a contract, a salesperson, or the owner's own network. Then ask which of those sources survive the sale. A business whose new customers come from channels you can see, measure and keep is easier to own than one whose pipeline lives in the seller's phone.

### Questions to ask

- **Where did last year's new customers come from?** Ask for a breakdown by source and how it's tracked (a customer relationship management system, a "how did you hear about us" field, call tracking, or memory).
- **What does each source cost?** Ad spend by channel, agency and software fees, commissions, referral fees, trade shows and sponsorships, for the same years as the financial statements.
- **What does a new customer cost?** Divide each channel's spend by the customers it produced. That's customer acquisition cost (CAC). Compare it with how much a customer is worth over the time they stay; see [revenue quality and churn](/q/revenue-quality-churn).
- **Who does the selling?** If the owner closes most sales, see [owner dependence](/q/owner-dependence). If a salesperson does, check their contract, pay plan and non-solicitation terms.
- **What's been tried and stopped?** Channels that stopped working tell you as much as the ones that work.
- **Has anything changed recently?** A cut in ad spend before a sale raises reported profit without changing anything else. A search ranking drop or an expired marketplace listing can cut leads before it shows up in revenue.

### What to look at yourself

- **Ad accounts and analytics.** Ask for read access, or screen-share, to the ad platforms, web analytics and search console for the last two or three years. Match spend to the general ledger.
- **Lead and sales records.** Leads by month and source, and the share that became customers.
- **Reviews and reputation.** Ratings and recent reviews on the sites customers use, and how the business responds. FTC rules bar paying for or offering incentives for reviews conditioned on whether they're positive or negative,[^ecfr-16-cfr-465-4-paid-reviews] so ask how reviews were collected.
- **The website and search position.** Which pages and searches bring traffic, and who controls the domain and site. See [IP, code and domains](/q/ip-tech).
- **Referral relationships.** Who sends business, under what arrangement, and whether it's tied to the seller personally.

### Compliance in the marketing itself

How the business contacts people can create liability that comes with it:

- **Texts and calls.** Under the FCC's rules, commercial robotexts need written consent.[^fcc-robotexts-commercial-consent] Ask how text and call lists were built and whether consent records exist.
- **Email.** CAN-SPAM covers email whose primary purpose is commercial promotion.[^ftc-can-spam-commercial-message] Check that the lists were collected properly and that opt-outs are honored.
- **Purchased lists and lead vendors.** Ask where the leads came from and what the vendor promised about consent.

### What it means for the deal

If marketing spend was cut to raise profit, the earnings need adjusting back up for spend you'll have to restore. If the main channel depends on the seller, the [transition plan](/q/seller-transition-period) should include handing it over. If a single platform drives most leads, a change to that platform is a risk you're buying. For growing marketing after you own the business, see [sales and marketing growth](/q/sales-marketing-growth).
