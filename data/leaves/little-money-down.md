**Short answer.** Sometimes, but "no money down" usually means someone else's money or more risk, not no money. With an SBA 7(a) loan, an initial acquisition needs at least 10% equity that the lender can't waive,[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-equity-minimum] and a seller note can cover at most half of it, and only if it's on full standby.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-equity-limited-sources][^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-seller-debt-equity] Outside SBA, deals with very little buyer cash happen when the seller finances most of the price or investors put up the equity, and each of those has costs.

### Where the down payment can come from

Under SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), the 10% is measured against total project cost, not just the price.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-equity-project-cost]

- **Your cash, a gift, or a personal loan repaid from outside the business** can supply all of it.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-equity-unlimited-sources] A loan repaid from your salary at the business doesn't qualify.
- **A seller note on full standby**, with no payments for the life of the SBA loan, can supply up to half.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-seller-debt-equity][^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-equity-limited-sources]
- **A passive minority investor** (under 20%, no control) can also count, within that same half.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-minority-investor][^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-equity-limited-sources]

So on an SBA deal, the floor is roughly 5% of project cost from unlimited sources such as your own cash or a gift, with the rest of the 10% from a standby seller note or minority investor. The lender may ask for more.

### The structures behind "no money down" claims

- **Heavy seller financing without a bank.** The seller carries most of the price. This depends on a seller who trusts you and doesn't need cash now, and it puts their payments ahead of your income in lean months. See [why sellers finance](/q/seller-financing-basics).
- **Investors put up the equity.** You find the deal and run it; investors fund it and own most of it. You give up ownership and answer to them. See [independent sponsors](/q/independent-sponsor) and [search funds](/q/traditional-search-fund).
- **Borrowing the down payment.** A home equity line or a personal loan. The money isn't free, and it adds a payment on top of the business's debt. See [ROBS, HELOC or savings](/q/equity-sources-robs-heloc).
- **Earnouts and deferred payments.** Part of the price is paid later if the business performs. SBA deals can't use seller earnouts.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-earnouts] See [the SBA earnout ban](/q/earnout-ban-forgivable-note).

### The risks that come with it

- **Thin coverage.** Less equity means more debt and bigger payments. SBA requires 1.25:1 coverage on historical cash flow for an initial acquisition;[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-dsc-ratio] other lenders set their own tests. A business that barely clears the test leaves no room for a bad year.
- **Price can't exceed value on borrowed money.** In an SBA deal, total debt is capped at the business valuation,[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-total-debt-cap] and anything paid above the valuation must come from equity.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-valuation-gap-equity]
- **Personal guarantees.** On an SBA loan, every 20% owner guarantees it personally.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-personal-guaranty] Little money down doesn't mean little at risk. See [personal guarantees](/q/personal-guarantee).
- **Sales pitches.** The FTC warns that business offers promising guaranteed income, large returns or a "proven system" are likely scams.[^ftc-coaching-scams-red-flags] Check any "no money down" structure a course teaches against the current SBA rules and a lender's term sheet.
