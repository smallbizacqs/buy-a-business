**Short answer.** Help with repetitive sourcing work (building lists, finding owner contacts, logging activity, making first-touch calls or emails) can multiply what you get done. Keep the conversations that build trust with owners, and every negotiation, for yourself. Before you bring anyone on, settle how they're classified and paid, what they can say in your name, and how you'll check their work, because the rules on wages and outreach still apply to you.

**What to hand off, and what not to**

Good to delegate:

- Researching businesses and building target lists. See [data sources and lists](/q/data-and-lists).
- Finding and verifying owner names and business contacts.
- Keeping your CRM (customer relationship management system, or a spreadsheet) up to date.
- First-touch outreach from scripts you've written and approved.
- Summarizing listings and teasers against your [buy box](/q/define-buy-box).

Keep for yourself:

- The first real conversation with an interested owner. See [the first call](/q/first-conversation).
- Anything involving price, terms or confidential information.
- Relationships with brokers and referral sources.

**Interns**

The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) requires for-profit employers to pay employees for their work.[^dol-fact-sheet-71-for-profit-pay] Whether an unpaid intern is actually an employee depends on the "primary beneficiary test," which looks at who really benefits from the arrangement, including how tied it is to the intern's education.[^dol-fact-sheet-71-primary-beneficiary] An intern doing your sourcing for your benefit may well need to be paid. If you're unsure, pay them, or ask an employment lawyer.

**Contractors and assistants**

Whether someone is an employee or an independent contractor depends on facts the IRS groups as behavioral control, financial control and the type of relationship.[^irs-worker-classification] Calling someone a contractor doesn't make them one. Write down the scope, the hours or deliverables, and the pay, and check classification with your CPA.

**Outsourced callers and emailers**

- **You stay responsible.** For email, the FTC says you can't contract away your legal responsibility to comply with CAN-SPAM.[^ftc-can-spam-vendor-responsibility]
- **Calls and texts have rules.** FCC rules require consent before an autodialed or prerecorded call or text to a wireless number,[^fcc-robocalls-wireless-consent] and the number listed for a small business may be the owner's cell phone. Ask any calling service what equipment it uses and how it handles do-not-call lists. See [outreach rules](/q/outreach-rules).
- **They speak for you.** Approve the script, make sure callers say truthfully who they're calling for, and listen to calls or review logs early on.

**Making it work**

1. **Write the playbook first.** Target criteria, scripts, what counts as a qualified lead, how to log contacts, and when to hand off to you.
2. **Start small.** A trial week or a small batch, reviewed closely, before scaling.
3. **Measure output and quality.** Contacts made, conversations booked, and whether the leads actually fit.
4. **Protect information.** A confidentiality agreement, access only to what they need, and nothing from a seller's data room unless your NDA with that seller allows it.

**Is it worth it?**

Compare the cost (pay, tools, data and your time managing) with the time it frees for conversations only you can have. If you haven't yet found a message that works yourself, more volume through someone else mostly multiplies what isn't working. See also [using AI to find and screen deals](/q/ai-sourcing).
