**Short answer.** Raise prices once you understand your costs, your contracts and what customers value, and once key relationships are secure, not in the first weeks unless costs force it. Then do it deliberately: start with the work or customers where prices are clearly below value, test with a segment, give notice, explain the reason, and keep service steady. Decide your prices on your own; agreeing prices with competitors is illegal.

### Before you raise anything, know three things

1. **Your real costs by product, service or customer.** Job costing or gross margin by line shows where you're losing money or earning too little. A price increase on unprofitable work and one on profitable work are different decisions.
2. **What your contracts allow.** Check each customer contract for fixed pricing, price escalation clauses, notice periods and renewal dates. Some prices can only change at renewal.
3. **Why customers buy from you.** If they value speed, reliability or a relationship, price may matter less than you fear. If they buy on price, an increase carries more risk. Talking with customers in the first months tells you which.

### When

Waiting while you build relationships is safer than a surprise increase right after the sale, when customers may already be wondering whether anything will change. Reasons to move sooner: costs have risen and margins are shrinking, prices haven't changed in years and are clearly below the market, or a contract renewal date forces a decision. A natural moment is a regular cycle the business already uses (an annual update, a new season's rate sheet), so the increase doesn't look tied to the new owner.

### How

- **Segment.** Different increases for different customers or services, based on margin and on how likely each is to leave. New customers can get new prices right away.
- **Test.** Try the change on a slice of quotes or customers and watch acceptance and win rates before rolling it out.
- **Give notice.** Tell customers ahead of time, in writing, with the effective date. Respect any notice period in their contracts.
- **Explain briefly.** Cost increases, investment in service, or simply that prices haven't changed in a long time. Don't apologize at length.
- **Hold the line on service.** An increase alongside slower service invites customers to shop around.
- **Prepare your team.** The people who talk to customers need the reason and an answer for pushback, including what discretion they have.

### Other ways to raise price

- Charge for things you currently give away: trip charges, rush fees, after-hours work, small orders.
- Add tiers or service plans that let customers choose more service at a higher price.
- Tighten discounts, which can drift upward over the years.
- Update payment terms, such as deposits on large jobs.

### Watch the results

Track gross margin, win rate on quotes, volume and customer losses in the months after an increase. Losing a few low-margin customers can be acceptable if margin improves; losing a key account isn't. Call any large customer who goes quiet.

### Don't coordinate with competitors

Under the Sherman Act, agreements among competitors to fix prices, rig bids or divide customers or markets are criminal violations.[^doj-sherman-act-competitor-agreements] Set your prices from your own costs and customer data. Don't discuss planned price changes with competitors, including at trade association events or through a shared contact.
