**Short answer.** Taxes affect how a deal is structured (asset or stock purchase) and, in an asset purchase, how the price is split among the assets. That split changes both your taxes and the seller's, so buyer and seller often want different allocations. A CPA should model it for you before you sign the letter of intent; this page only explains the basic federal reporting.

**Asset purchases and Form 8594.** When a group of assets that makes up a business is sold and goodwill could attach to it, both the buyer and the seller report the sale on IRS Form 8594, the Asset Acquisition Statement.[^irs-form-8594-who-files] The price is allocated across the assets using the residual method, which determines the buyer's basis in each asset and the seller's gain or loss on each.[^irs-form-8594-residual-method] The IRS sorts assets into seven classes; the last, Class VII, is goodwill and going concern value.[^irs-form-8594-class-vii] Each side generally attaches the form to its income tax return for the year of the sale.[^irs-form-8594-when-to-file]

**Why the allocation is negotiated.** The amount assigned to equipment, inventory, a non-compete, consulting payments or goodwill can be taxed differently for each side, and can affect when you get deductions. Purchase agreements often include an agreed allocation, or a process for agreeing one, so that buyer and seller report consistently.

**Stock purchases** follow different tax rules, which depend on the kind of entity being bought. That's a question for your CPA.

**State and local taxes** can also matter. Ask your CPA and lawyer what applies where the business operates.
