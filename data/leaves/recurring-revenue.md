**Short answer.** Recurring revenue is income that comes back without the business having to win it again each time: subscriptions, service contracts, maintenance plans, monitoring fees. It makes next year easier to predict, makes the loan easier to carry, and makes the business less dependent on the seller's selling. Many kinds of business can have it; what matters is what the contracts actually say and how many customers stay.

**Three kinds to tell apart**

- **Contracted recurring revenue.** The customer has signed up to pay on a schedule: a service agreement, a subscription, a monitoring contract. Strongest when the contract has a term and isn't cancellable at will.
- **Repeat revenue.** No contract, but customers come back because they need to: consumables, scheduled service, regular repairs, route-based work. Predictable if the history shows it.
- **One-time revenue.** Each job is won separately: projects, installations, one-off sales. It can still be a good business, but next year's revenue depends on next year's selling.

**Why buyers look for it**

- **Predictability.** You're buying future cash flow, and recurring revenue is the part you can see in advance.
- **Financing.** An SBA lender tests coverage of the loan payments on historical results (the last fiscal year-end or the average of the last two),[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-dsc-ratio] looking at three years of the acquired business's numbers.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-three-years] Steady recurring income makes that history steadier. When an SBA-required quality of earnings report applies, it must assess the quality and sustainability of revenue, including customer concentration and contract continuity.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-qoe-revenue-quality]
- **Transition.** Customers on a contract or schedule are less likely to drift away when the owner leaves than customers who bought because of a personal relationship.

**Where to look for it**

Businesses that can sell on contract or subscription include commercial cleaning and facility services, pest control, HVAC and other equipment maintenance plans, landscape maintenance, alarm and fire-system monitoring and inspection, managed IT services, bookkeeping and payroll services, waste and route services, and software sold by subscription. Treat this as a list of places to check, not a promise: two businesses in the same industry can have very different revenue mixes.

**What to check before you count on it**

1. **The contracts themselves.** Term, renewal, price-increase rights, cancellation rights, and whether the contract can be assigned to a new owner or ends on a change of control. See [contract assignment](/q/contract-assignment).
2. **Churn.** How many customers and how much revenue left each year, and why. See [revenue quality and churn](/q/revenue-quality-churn).
3. **Concentration.** Whether a few accounts make up most of the recurring base. See [customer concentration](/q/customer-concentration).
4. **Price.** Whether recurring customers are on old prices well below current rates, and what happens if you raise them.
5. **Who holds the relationship.** If the contracts renew because customers like the owner, they're less recurring than they look. See [owner dependence](/q/owner-dependence).
6. **Margins.** Recurring work at a thin or negative margin is a commitment, not an asset.

**Using it in your search**

Put a target share of recurring or repeat revenue in your [buy box](/q/define-buy-box), and ask for a revenue breakdown by type early. How recurring revenue affects price is covered in [what earns a higher multiple](/q/value-drivers).
