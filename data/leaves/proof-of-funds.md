**Short answer.** Show enough to make it believable that you can close, and no more: a recent bank or brokerage statement (or a letter from your bank) showing the cash you'll put in, with account numbers blacked out, plus a letter from a lender saying it has reviewed your profile and is interested in financing a deal like this. Share it after an NDA, with the broker or owner who needs it, and remember a lender's letter isn't a loan approval.

Not to be confused with a "proof of cash," which is a test of the seller's bank deposits against reported revenue; see [proof of cash](/q/proof-of-cash).

**Why brokers and owners ask**

A broker is paid when a deal closes and an owner can't afford months with a buyer who can't pay. Proof of funds is how they decide whom to spend time with and whose offer is real. See [looking credible to brokers](/q/broker-credibility).

**What you can provide**

- **Liquid funds.** A recent statement from a bank or brokerage account showing the balance, with account numbers and unrelated transactions redacted. Or ask your bank for a short letter confirming funds.
- **A lender letter.** A letter from a lender (called a prequalification or letter of interest, among other names), saying it has reviewed your finances and experience and would consider financing an acquisition of a certain size. Ask the lender exactly what it will say, since wording varies.
- **A personal financial statement.** A one-page summary of assets and liabilities. SBA lenders use one in underwriting: under SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), owners of 20% or more and proposed guarantors provide a personal financial statement (SBA Form 413 or the lender's equivalent) dated within 90 days.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-owner-financial-statement] A broker may accept a summary; keep the detail for the lender.
- **Investor commitments.** If partners or investors are funding part of the deal, a letter from each confirming their intended commitment.

**How much to show**

Enough to cover your equity in the deal, plus closing costs and some reserve. For an SBA 7(a) initial acquisition the buyer must inject at least 10% equity,[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-equity-minimum] and cash that isn't borrowed, including a gift, or a personal loan repaid from outside the business can supply all of it.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-equity-unlimited-sources] If your equity will come from a gift, a sale of stock or another source, say so and be ready to document it for the lender.

**What a lender letter does and doesn't mean**

It shows a lender has looked at you and is interested. It doesn't approve a loan for this business: the lender still has to underwrite the business's financials, valuation and your application, and for applications the lender sends to SBA for processing, SBA itself decides and approves or declines.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-lgpc-decision] Don't describe a prequalification as approved financing. See [how underwriting works](/q/underwriting) and [choosing an SBA lender](/q/lender-selection).

**Protecting yourself**

- Share after you've signed the broker's NDA (nondisclosure agreement), and only with the people who need it.
- Redact account numbers and anything not needed to show the balance.
- Send a PDF rather than giving access to accounts.
- Keep statements current; a balance from months ago carries less weight.
- Don't give a broker or seller authority to contact your bank or lender without asking you first.

**Timing**

Have the package ready before you start contacting brokers, so you can send it the same day it's asked for. Being quick here is one of the easiest ways to stand out. See [winning without the highest offer](/q/buyer-of-choice).
