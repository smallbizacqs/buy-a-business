**Short answer.** A roll-up (or buy-and-build) buys several companies in one industry and combines them into a larger one. The thesis is that the combined company can be worth more than the parts: through shared overhead and purchasing, better systems and management, cross-selling, wider reach, and a higher valuation multiple because larger, more professional companies attract more buyers. Each of those gains has to be earned through integration; buying the companies only creates the opportunity.

### How it's supposed to create value

1. **Cost savings.** One back office, one software system, combined purchasing, shared equipment, fewer locations.
2. **Revenue gains.** Selling each company's services to the others' customers, covering a wider territory, bidding on larger contracts.
3. **Better management.** Professional finance, hiring, pricing and operations that small companies couldn't afford alone.
4. **A higher multiple.** The hope is that buyers will pay more per dollar of earnings for a company that is larger, less dependent on one owner and better run. Whether this holds for a given deal is uncertain; see [multiple arbitrage](/q/multiple-arbitrage).

### The structure

- **The platform.** The first acquisition, ideally one with the management team and systems to absorb others.
- **Add-ons.** Smaller companies merged into the platform. See [add-on acquisitions](/q/add-on-acquisitions).
- **Integration.** Bringing add-ons onto common systems, brands and processes, or deliberately keeping some separate. See [integration](/q/integration).
- **Capital.** Debt and equity to fund repeated acquisitions, and a plan for when and how investors are paid back.

### What it takes

- **A fragmented industry** with many owner-run companies, steady demand and work that benefits from scale.
- **A team** that can find, diligence and close deals while others run operations. One person can't do both well for long.
- **Systems** (accounting, payroll, scheduling, reporting) that can take on new companies quickly.
- **Discipline on price** and on which companies to buy. Overpaying or buying weak companies erodes the thesis.
- **Capital** with enough patience for integration to work.

### Where it goes wrong

- Buying faster than you can integrate, so you own several separate businesses with more overhead, not less. See [buying too much too fast](/q/too-many-too-fast).
- Savings that never arrive because each company keeps its own way of doing things.
- Losing owners' relationships and key employees in each acquisition.
- Too much debt for the combined cash flow.
- Counting on a higher exit multiple that doesn't materialize.

### A legal limit to know

Under the Clayton Act, a merger is illegal when it may substantially lessen competition or tend to create a monopoly in a relevant market.[^doj-clayton-act-mergers] A roll-up that buys most competitors in a local market can raise that question. Ask your lawyer whether a planned acquisition needs antitrust review.

### Schools of thought

Practitioners disagree on how fast to buy, whether to integrate tightly or let acquired companies keep their names and teams, and whether the value comes mainly from operating improvements or from selling the larger company at a higher multiple. The view that operating improvements should justify the deal on their own, with any higher multiple a bonus, is the more cautious one.
