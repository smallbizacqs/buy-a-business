**Short answer.** Walk away when the deal you'd close is no longer the deal you decided to buy, and fixing it on price or terms isn't possible. Decide that by looking forward: what the business is worth to you now, what you'll pay, and whether you still want to run it. Not by looking back at the money and months already spent. Writing your walk-away conditions down before diligence starts makes the call much easier when you're tired and invested.

### Set your conditions before you're invested

Before or right after the LOI, write a short list of what would make you walk:

- A floor on verified earnings (for example, if verified cash flow comes in below the level your financing needs).
- Specific risks you won't take: a key customer who won't stay, a license you can't get, a lease you can't secure.
- Limits on terms: the most you'll pay in cash at closing, the least seller financing you'll accept, the personal guarantee you're prepared to give.
- A personal limit: the timeline or cost beyond which you'd rather start over.

Share the list with someone who will hold you to it, such as an advisor, a partner or an investor.

### Sunk costs

By late diligence you've spent money on lawyers, accountants and reports, and months of your time. None of that comes back whether you close or not, so it shouldn't push you toward closing. The right question is: knowing what I know now, would I sign this deal today if I were starting fresh? If the answer is no, the money already spent is a reason to stop, not to continue.

The same goes for the relationship with the seller. Walking away can feel like breaking a promise. If your LOI's main terms are non-binding, that was the point: diligence exists to give both sides that choice. See [what goes in an LOI](/q/loi-contents).

### Deal fatigue

Long deals wear everyone down. Signs it's affecting your judgment:

- You're explaining away findings you'd have flagged in week one.
- You're agreeing to terms just to be done.
- You've stopped asking questions because you don't want the answers.
- You feel relief, not concern, when an advisor says something is "probably fine."

If you see these, take a day off from the deal, reread your original thesis and walk-away list, and ask an advisor to argue the case against closing.

### Separate "reprice" from "walk"

Some problems are fixable: a lower price, more seller financing, an escrow, a longer transition, a specific indemnity. See [retrading](/q/retrade) and [findings that kill or reprice a deal](/q/deal-killers). Walk when:

- The problem can't be priced, because you can't tell how big it is.
- The seller won't negotiate a fix you need.
- What you learned changes your view of the seller's honesty. See [fraud and misstatements](/q/fraud-misstatement).
- You no longer want to own and run this business.

### How to walk away well

- Check the LOI for what you owe: confidentiality, return or destruction of documents, a break fee if any.
- Tell the seller directly, in writing, with a short and honest reason. If a fix would change your mind, say what it is.
- Pay your advisors and close out their work.
- Keep notes on what you learned for the next deal; your [buy box](/q/define-buy-box) may need updating.

Walking away from a bad deal is part of a search that works, not a failure of one.
