**Short answer.** Earn credibility by listening before you change things, being visible, keeping every promise, and being honest about what you don't know yet. Learn the work well enough to respect it, explain your decisions and their reasons, and set a regular rhythm of communication. The culture you inherit came from the seller's habits; you change it mostly through what you do, reward and tolerate, not through announcements.

### The first weeks: listen and show up

- **Meet everyone one-on-one.** Ask what they do, what works, what gets in their way, and what they'd change if it were their business. Take notes and refer back to them.
- **Do the work.** Spend time on job sites, in the shop, on the phones or at the counter. Learn the vocabulary of the trade. People judge a new owner by whether they understand the work.
- **Be honest about being new.** Saying "I don't know yet, I'll find out" builds more trust than pretending.
- **Keep small promises.** If you say you'll look into a broken tool or a scheduling headache, report back by when you said.

### Understand the culture you bought

Before deciding what to keep or change, notice:

- How decisions actually get made, and who people go to when the owner isn't around.
- What the seller praised, ignored and punished. Employees will expect the same from you until you show otherwise.
- The unwritten rules: start times, overtime, who gets the good jobs, how mistakes are handled.
- Who the informal leaders are. Their view of you spreads.

### Set a rhythm

A predictable schedule of communication helps people trust a new owner:

- A short regular team meeting (weekly, for example) to share what's happening and what's coming.
- Regular one-on-ones with your direct reports.
- A clear way to raise problems with you directly.

If you later adopt a management framework, the rhythm can grow into it. See [EOS and other management systems](/q/operating-system-eos).

### Making decisions as the new owner

- **Explain the why.** People accept decisions they disagree with more readily when they understand the reasons.
- **Involve the people affected.** Ask for input on changes to their work before deciding.
- **Be consistent.** Apply rules the same way to everyone, including long-time employees and anyone who was close to the seller.
- **Address problems directly.** Tolerating poor performance or bad behavior tells everyone else what the new standard is.

### Your style versus theirs

You may come from a large company, a different industry or the military, with a different style from the seller's. Adjust where it helps and say openly what will be different about how you work. Avoid corporate jargon, heavy reporting requirements or new processes that make the business feel like someone else's company overnight.

### Common mistakes

- Arriving with a long list of changes before learning why things are done as they are.
- Spending time mostly in the office or on spreadsheets.
- Talking down the seller. Employees may have been loyal to them for years.
- Promising raises, promotions or job security you can't yet guarantee.
