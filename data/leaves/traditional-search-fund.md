**Short answer.** In a traditional search fund, a searcher (often one person or a pair) raises money from a group of investors to cover a salary and search costs for a set period. When the searcher finds a business, those investors get the first right to fund the purchase. If the deal closes, the searcher usually runs the company and earns a share of ownership over time, while investors own the rest.

**The two raises**

1. **The search capital.** A first, smaller raise pays the searcher's salary and expenses during the search. Investors in this round typically receive a right to invest in the acquisition and some extra benefit for having funded the search.
2. **The acquisition capital.** When a deal is found, the searcher offers the investment to the search investors first; others may fill any gap. Debt (bank or seller) usually funds part of the price.

**How the searcher is paid**

A salary during the search and, after an acquisition, an equity stake that is commonly earned in parts: some at closing, some over time, and some tied to investor returns. Exact terms vary by fund. See [searcher economics](/q/searcher-economics).

**Governance**

Investors usually take board seats and stay involved after closing. The searcher becomes the chief executive and reports to that board.

**Securities rules apply to both raises**

Each raise sells securities. Under Regulation D, outside Rule 506(c) the raise can't use general solicitation or advertising,[^ecfr-17-cfr-230-502-general-solicitation] and under Rule 506(c) every purchaser must be accredited.[^ecfr-17-cfr-230-506-c-accredited] See [Rule 506(b) or 506(c)](/q/506b-506c-securities).

**Compared with a self-funded search**

The searcher gets a salary, investor support and the ability to pursue larger deals, and gives up most of the ownership and some control. See [choose your path](/q/choose-path).
