**Short answer.** They're two different letters from the landlord, sometimes combined in one document. A consent is the landlord's written permission to assign the lease to you (or, in a stock deal, to the change in ownership, if the lease requires it). An estoppel certificate is the landlord's signed statement of the lease's key facts: that the lease is in effect, what the rent and term are, that the tenant isn't in default and that no side deals exist. You need the consent whenever the lease requires it, and an estoppel is worth asking for whenever the lease matters to the business.

### What a consent does

Check whether the lease restricts assignment. If it requires the landlord's consent and you take over without it, the landlord may be able to treat that as a default. The consent letter says the landlord agrees to the assignment, and may set conditions: your financial information, a personal guarantee, a security deposit, or an assignment fee. It should also say whether the seller stays liable under the lease after the assignment. The seller will want a release; the landlord may refuse one.

In a stock or membership interest purchase, the tenant doesn't change, but some leases treat a change of control as an assignment. Read the clause.

### What an estoppel does

"Estoppel" means the landlord is held to what it certifies. Once it signs, it generally can't later claim facts that contradict the certificate against someone who relied on it, such as you or your lender. An estoppel can confirm:

- The lease documents, listed by date, are the whole agreement, with no unwritten amendments.
- The lease is in full force and effect.
- The start and end dates, renewal options and how to exercise them.
- Current rent, additional charges (taxes, insurance, common area costs) and the date rent is paid through.
- The security deposit held.
- No defaults by either side, and no events that would become defaults with notice or time.
- No pending disputes, audits or claims for back charges.
- Any rights the tenant has: options to renew, rights of first refusal, exclusivity in the building.

It protects you from surprises the seller may not mention: a rent increase already in motion, a disputed reconciliation of common area charges, an unexercised option that's about to lapse, or a default notice.

### When it matters most

- The location is central to the business's value (a restaurant, a clinic, a retail store).
- The lease has years left and renewal options you're relying on.
- Your lender needs it. An SBA 7(a) application file includes a copy of the lease,[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-application-lease-copy] and under SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), where the lease rule applies, the lease term including your renewal options should equal or exceed the loan term.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-lease-term] The lender may require the landlord's documents as a condition of closing.

### Getting it signed

- Ask the seller to request it, and send the landlord a draft rather than a blank request. A short form the landlord can check against its records is easier to sign.
- Allow time. The landlord has no deadline unless the lease sets one, so check whether the lease obliges the landlord to sign an estoppel on request.
- Make it a closing condition. See [closing conditions](/q/closing-conditions).
- Compare every statement with the lease and the rent ledger before you accept it.

For negotiating the lease itself (term, a new lease versus an assignment, guarantees), see [the landlord and the lease](/q/lease-assignment).
