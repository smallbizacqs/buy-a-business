**Short answer.** Move the work you do into roles, written processes and numbers, one area at a time. List everything you do, decide who should own each task, document how it's done, train that person, and check results through a scorecard instead of doing the work yourself. The key step is a capable manager for daily operations, with clear authority, and a weekly rhythm that keeps you informed without being involved in every decision.

### Start with an inventory of your own job

For two to four weeks, write down everything you do, including decisions, approvals, customer calls and fixes. Then sort each item:

- **Only the owner should do it:** strategy, banking relationships, hiring and firing senior people, big contracts.
- **Someone else could do it now** with a little instruction.
- **Someone else could do it with training or a new hire.**
- **It shouldn't be done at all.**

That list is your plan for getting out of daily work.

### Roles before processes

Write down each important function (sales, operations or service delivery, finance and administration, hiring) and who owns it. One person per function, even if one person owns several. A chart like this, sometimes called an accountability chart or organization chart, shows the gaps.

If you plan to step back, the key hire is a general manager or operations lead who can take over daily decisions. Give that person:

- A written description of their authority: what they can decide alone, what they decide with you, what they bring to you.
- Clear results to deliver and the numbers you'll watch.
- Time to learn before you step back, and support when they make mistakes.

See [hiring and strengthening the team](/q/hiring-team).

### Document how work gets done

Write down core processes in plain, short form: a checklist, a short video or a one-page procedure. Start with the work that's done often, is done differently by different people, or would stop if one person left:

- Quoting and pricing.
- Taking and scheduling jobs.
- Delivering the service, with quality checks.
- Invoicing and collections.
- Hiring and onboarding.

Have the people who do the work write or record it, and review it with them. Keep documents where everyone can find them, and update them when the process changes.

### Manage with numbers

Replace watching the work with watching results. A weekly scorecard of a handful of numbers (sales, jobs completed, margin, cash, receivables) and a monthly financial review let you see problems without doing the work. See [reporting and key numbers](/q/financial-controls-kpis).

### Set a rhythm

A weekly meeting with your managers to review the numbers and solve problems, and quarterly priorities, keep the business moving without you directing each day. A named management framework is one option for this. See [EOS and other management systems](/q/operating-system-eos).

### Step back gradually

1. Hand off tasks area by area, and stay available for questions.
2. Stop answering questions that the owner of an area should answer; send them back.
3. Take time away (a few days, then a week) and see what breaks. Fix those gaps.

### Common mistakes

- Delegating responsibility without the authority to act.
- Writing long manuals no one reads.
- Hiring a manager, then overriding their decisions.
- Keeping all customer relationships yourself.

A business that operates without its owner is also easier to sell and can be worth more. See [increasing the value of the business](/q/value-creation) and [owner dependence](/q/owner-dependence).
