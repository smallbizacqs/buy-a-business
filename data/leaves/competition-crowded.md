**Short answer.** There's no official count of individual buyers chasing small businesses, so claims that the field is "crowded" or "wide open" are impressions, not data. What you can control is how you look to owners and brokers compared with whoever else calls them: specific about what you want, able to show how you'd pay, knowledgeable about the industry, quick, and easy to trust. Buyers also stand out by looking where fewer others look.

**What competition looks like in practice**

You'll feel it as listings that get several offers, brokers who won't call back buyers they don't know, and owners who've already had letters or calls from other buyers. None of that means good deals are gone; it means a generic approach gets lost.

**How to stand out in general**

- **Be specific.** A clear [buy box](/q/define-buy-box) (industry, size, location) tells owners and brokers you know what you want, and lets them match you quickly.
- **Show you can pay.** Know your equity and your financing before you approach anyone. For an SBA 7(a) initial acquisition, under SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), the buyer must inject at least 10% equity.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-equity-minimum] Being able to show that cash and a lender relationship sets you apart from buyers who can't. See [proof of funds](/q/proof-of-funds).
- **Know the industry.** Owners notice a buyer who understands their work, customers and problems. Lenders do too: the credit write-up covers your industry experience and who will manage day to day.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-management-experience] See [how to pick an industry](/q/industry-selection).
- **Be local and present.** Meeting in person and living near the business answer questions owners will have about whether you'll stay and run it. See [buying locally or moving](/q/geography-relocation).
- **Be fast and reliable.** Reply quickly, sign NDAs (nondisclosure agreements) promptly, read what you're sent, and do what you say when you say.
- **Tell a true story.** Why you want to own this kind of business, and what you'd do for its employees and customers. See [what sellers care about besides price](/q/seller-psychology).

**Look where fewer buyers look**

- **Off-market owners.** Owners who haven't listed haven't heard from every buyer. See [finding owners whose businesses aren't listed](/q/off-market-sourcing).
- **Smaller towns and less visible industries.** Businesses that don't fit the popular lists can draw fewer bidders.
- **Businesses with fixable problems.** Messy books, an owner who never hired a manager, or no online presence put some buyers off. If you can fix them and the price reflects them, they can be your edge. See [hidden upside](/q/hidden-upside).
- **Sizes between categories.** Deals that are too big for most individuals and too small for institutional buyers can have fewer serious bidders; check whether that's true in your market.

**What doesn't work**

Overpaying to win, waiving diligence, or making promises you can't keep. Winning a deal at the wrong price or with hidden problems is worse than losing it. For winning a particular deal against higher offers, see [winning without the highest offer](/q/buyer-of-choice); for whether retiring owners are creating more supply, see [the wave of retiring owners](/q/silver-tsunami).
