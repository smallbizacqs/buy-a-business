**Short answer.** Buying gets you customers, employees and cash flow on the first day, in exchange for a higher price up front, usually a loan you personally guarantee, and whatever problems come with the business. Starting costs less at the outset but takes longer to pay you, and the idea may never find enough customers. The right choice depends on how much capital you have, how you feel about debt, and whether you'd rather build something new or take over and improve something that already works.

### What you get when you buy

The SBA describes the main advantage of buying as an existing blueprint: an established customer base, known operating expenses and trained employees.[^sba-buy-business-blueprint] That history also makes an acquisition easier to finance. For an SBA 7(a) loan (a bank loan partly guaranteed by the U.S. Small Business Administration), under SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), the lender measures debt coverage on the business's past results,[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-dsc-ratio] and the business's cash flow must be the main source of repayment.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-cash-flow-repayment] A startup has no past results to lend against, so new ventures usually rely more on the founder's own money, outside investors or collateral.

### What buying costs you

- **The price.** You pay for the profit the seller already built, usually as a multiple of earnings. See [what multiple to pay](/q/what-multiple).
- **Your equity and your guarantee.** Under the same SOP, an initial acquisition needs at least 10% equity from the buyer,[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-equity-minimum] and every individual owning 20% or more gives an unlimited personal guarantee.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-personal-guaranty] See [personal guarantees](/q/personal-guarantee).
- **Debt payments first.** Loan payments come out of cash flow before you're paid much. See [take-home pay](/q/owner-take-home) and [the pay dip after closing](/q/j-curve).
- **What you inherit.** Habits, staff, customer expectations, worn equipment and sometimes liabilities. How much depends on the deal structure; see [asset or stock purchase](/q/asset-vs-stock).

### What starting costs you

Starting lets you choose everything: the market, the name, the first hires, the pace. You can often begin small without borrowing. The costs are time without a salary, the work of finding customers who will pay, and the uncertainty of not knowing when, or whether, it will cover your living expenses.

### Questions that decide it

- How much can you put at risk, and are you willing to sign a personal guarantee?
- How long can your household go without your paycheck?
- Do you enjoy creating something from nothing, or improving and running something?
- Do you have a specific idea or edge that a new business would exploit? If not, buying lets you skip the search for a product people want.
- Do you want to manage people from day one? A bought business arrives with a team.

### Common mistakes

- Comparing a best-case startup plan with a worst-case acquisition, or the reverse.
- Treating a bought business as passive. Someone still has to run it; see [running it yourself or hiring a manager](/q/operator-vs-absentee).
- Forgetting the third path. See [franchises](/q/buy-vs-franchise) and [entrepreneurship through acquisition](/q/what-is-eta).
