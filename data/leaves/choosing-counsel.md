**Short answer.** No law requires a lawyer to buy a business, but the purchase agreement decides who carries which risks after closing, and a lender's closing has its own documents to review. Some buyers handle small deals with templates and guides (see the [letter of intent](/q/loi-contents) and [purchase agreement](/q/apa-key-terms) pages). Buyers who hire counsel can look for AI-using law firms with fixed fees that are lean and run by an attorney with big-law experience.

**What deal counsel usually does:** reviews or drafts the letter of intent, runs legal diligence (contracts, leases, licenses, litigation, employees), drafts and negotiates the purchase agreement and closing documents, reviews the loan documents, and coordinates closing.

**Questions to ask a lawyer before hiring:**

- How many small-business purchases like this one have you closed, in this state and industry?
- Is the fee fixed? What exactly does it cover, and what costs extra (real estate, licensing, a second round of negotiation)?
- Who does the work, and how quickly do you turn drafts around?
- If you're using an SBA loan (a bank loan partly guaranteed by the U.S. Small Business Administration): have you closed deals with SBA lenders?
- Do you have any conflict with the seller, the broker or the lender?
- How do you use AI and templates, and how does that show up in the fee?

**Who represents whom.** The seller's lawyer works for the seller, and the lender's lawyer works for the lender. A broker usually works for the seller. None of them is your advisor.

See also [who should be on your team](/q/deal-team) and [what advisors cost](/q/advisor-costs).
