**Short answer.** If the seller owns the building the business runs from, you can buy it with the business, lease it from the seller, or (sometimes) buy it later. Buying gives you control and keeps the location secure, but it adds cost, debt and a second kind of risk. Leasing keeps more cash for the business, but then the lease terms matter a great deal.

**Things to weigh:**

- **How much the location matters.** A business tied to its site (a restaurant, a car wash, a shop with special equipment) is exposed if it can't stay.
- **Cash and debt.** Buying the property raises the amount you need at closing.
- **The seller's wishes.** Some sellers want rent as retirement income; others want a clean exit.
- **Lease terms if you rent**: length, renewal options, rent increases, who pays repairs and taxes, and whether you get a right of first refusal (the right to match any offer if the seller later sells the building).

**With an SBA 7(a) loan.** An SBA 7(a) loan is a bank loan partly guaranteed by the U.S. Small Business Administration (SBA). Under SBA SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026):

- The SBA's "Business Purchase Price," which drives several diligence rules, excludes owner-occupied commercial real estate bought in the deal.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-business-purchase-price]
- When one loan covers both the business and the real estate, only the real estate portion may amortize over more than 10 years, up to 25; everything else, including working capital, gets 10 years.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-real-estate-structure]
- If you lease and a large share of the loan or collateral relates to leasehold improvements or attached equipment, the lender must get a copy of the lease, and the lease term including your renewal options should equal or exceed the loan term.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-lease-term]

Ask your lender how it would structure each option for your deal. See also [the landlord and the lease](/q/lease-assignment).
