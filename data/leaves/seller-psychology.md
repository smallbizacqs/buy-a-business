**Short answer.** Besides price, owners care about what happens to their employees, customers and name; whether the deal will actually close; how the sale is timed and kept quiet; what role they'll have afterward; and what they'll do with themselves once the business is gone. Ask about these early, listen, and offer only commitments you'll keep and can put in writing.

**What owners weigh**

- **Employees.** An owner may have worked with the same staff for years, and want to know whether people keep their jobs, pay and roles, and how and when staff will be told. See [announcing the sale to employees](/q/announce-to-employees).
- **Customers and reputation.** Whether customers will be served the way they're used to, and whether the business name will survive.
- **Legacy.** The business may carry the family name or represent decades of work. A buyer who respects that history is easier to sell to.
- **Certainty.** A failed deal after months of diligence is costly and painful; an owner may prefer a buyer who's clearly able to close over a slightly higher offer. See [winning without the highest offer](/q/buyer-of-choice).
- **Timing.** Retirement dates, tax years, a spouse's plans, health. The closing date and the length of the transition matter.
- **Confidentiality.** Fear that employees, customers or competitors will find out before the deal is done.
- **Their role afterward.** Some want a clean exit; others want to stay involved. In an SBA 7(a) initial acquisition, under SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), the seller can't stay as an officer, director, owner or employee, but the business may hire the seller as a consultant for up to 24 months in total.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-seller-consultant] Explain that early so it doesn't surprise them. See [the seller's transition period](/q/seller-transition-period).
- **Identity and what's next.** For some owners the business is who they are. Selling can feel like retiring, grieving and being judged at once.
- **Family.** Children who didn't want the business, or a spouse who works in it.
- **Fairness and respect.** How they're treated in the process, especially when you raise problems or ask for a lower price.
- **Net proceeds.** Not just the headline price but what they keep after taxes and debts, and when they get it. See [price versus terms](/q/price-vs-terms).

**How to learn what matters to this owner**

Ask open questions and let them talk:

- "What would a great outcome look like for you?"
- "What matters most to you about who takes over?"
- "What worries you about selling?"
- "What do you want to be doing a year after the sale?"

Listen for what they mention first and come back to. See [the first call](/q/first-conversation) and [earning an owner's trust](/q/earn-trust).

**How to respond**

- **Make commitments you can keep.** If you plan to keep the staff and the name, say so; if you can't promise it, don't.
- **Put the important ones in writing,** in the letter of intent or purchase agreement where it makes sense, such as the transition role or employee offers.
- **Be honest about problems.** Raising an issue respectfully, with your reasoning, keeps trust even when it costs the seller money.
- **Respect the emotion.** An owner who seems irrational about price may be pricing their life's work, not a set of cash flows. Patience and a clear explanation do more than pressure.

**A caution**

Understanding what a seller cares about is about finding a deal that works for both of you, not about manipulating them. Owners talk to each other, to brokers and to their advisers, and your reputation follows you. See also [why the owner is really selling](/q/why-owner-selling) and [a seller who won't let go](/q/seller-wont-let-go).
