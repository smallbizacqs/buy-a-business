**Short answer.** Do the cheap work that is most likely to kill the deal first, and commission the expensive reports only once that work comes back clean. In practice that means your own review of the financials, bank statements and the biggest customers before you pay for a quality of earnings report, legal documents or an appraisal. Agree the order with your lender early, because some reports are on its schedule, not yours.

### A sensible order

**Phase 1: your own work (low cost, high kill rate).** In the first weeks after the LOI (letter of intent), check what you can do yourself or with a few hours of a CPA's time:

- Tie the tax returns, profit and loss statements and bank deposits together. See [proof of cash](/q/proof-of-cash).
- Look at revenue by customer and by month for concentration and decline. See [revenue quality and churn](/q/revenue-quality-churn).
- Read the lease and the largest contracts for assignment and change-of-control terms.
- Run the public-record searches for liens and lawsuits. See [hidden liabilities](/q/litigation-liabilities).
- Ask the questions the seller is least likely to want to answer: why they're selling, what's changed this year, who would leave.

If any of these turns up a problem you can't price, stop here. You've spent mostly time.

**Phase 2: lender and third-party reports.** Once the basics hold up, the lender runs its process and the paid reports start: the business valuation, a quality of earnings (QoE) report if required or wanted, and any environmental or equipment appraisal. Under SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), a lender with delegated (PLP) authority may finish the valuation and QoE after the SBA loan number is issued, as long as they're under way and done before closing,[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-plp-valuation-timing] so ask your lender when it plans to order them.

**Phase 3: legal documents and closing.** Drafting the purchase agreement, disclosure schedules, transition agreements and lease documents can be the largest legal spend. One approach is to hold full drafting until the financial work and the lender's approval are in sight, while keeping the key legal questions (entity, liens, consents) in phase 1.

### Avoid paying twice

- The SBA business valuation must be ordered by and prepared for the lender; one prepared for the buyer or seller can't be used.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-valuation-for-lender] Don't commission your own valuation expecting the lender to rely on it.
- A lender may have a QoE you commissioned reviewed by one of its approved vendors instead of ordering a new one.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-qoe-buyer-commissioned] Ask before you hire anyone whether the lender will accept their work.
- The cost of the valuation and QoE may be passed to you, and what you spend on them can count toward your equity injection.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-diligence-costs]

### What drives the order for your deal

- **Where the risk is.** A business with one big customer should call that customer early; one with old equipment should get it inspected early.
- **Exclusivity.** If your exclusive period is short, you may have to run phases in parallel. See [exclusivity](/q/exclusivity).
- **The lender's timeline.** Reports the lender orders run on its schedule. See [the approval timeline](/q/approval-timeline).
- **What's refundable.** Ask each advisor for a scope that can stop at a checkpoint, with a fee for the work done so far.

This page covers spending after the LOI. For what to check before you sign one, see [pre-LOI diligence](/q/pre-loi-diligence).
