**Short answer.** Build a simple model of the business after closing, with your salary and every loan payment in it, then change one thing at a time: revenue down, gross margin down, a key cost up, interest rates up, a big customer gone. For each case, see whether you can still make the loan payments and pay yourself, and how many months of cash you have. The goal is to know, before you sign, how much can go wrong before you can't pay.

**Why it matters**

An SBA loan is repaid from the business's cash flow, and a lender must decline a loan the cash flow can't support, whatever the collateral.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-cash-flow-repayment] Anyone owning 20% or more must give an unlimited personal guarantee,[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-personal-guaranty] so a downturn the business can't absorb lands on you. See [personal guarantees](/q/personal-guarantee).

**Build the base case**

Start from verified historical earnings, then subtract: a market salary for you (or your manager), all loan payments including any seller note, maintenance capital spending, and taxes. Under SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), the lender requires 1.25:1 coverage on historical results for an initial acquisition[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-dsc-ratio] and a global cash flow analysis (including your personal finances) at 1:1,[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-global-cash-flow] and it analyzes whether working capital is adequate for at least the next 12 months.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-working-capital-adequacy] Meeting those tests is the floor, not proof the deal is safe.

**Cases to run**

- **Revenue drop.** Lower revenue and keep fixed costs fixed. Find the decline at which coverage hits 1.0 (you can just make payments) and the point where you can't pay yourself.
- **Margin squeeze.** Raise materials, labor or subcontractor costs without raising prices.
- **Rate increase.** 7(a) loans can be variable, set as a base rate plus a fixed spread,[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-variable-rate] so payments can rise. Rerun with a higher rate.
- **Lost customer or key employee.** Remove your largest customer, or add the cost of replacing a key person.
- **Transition dip.** Assume a few soft months after closing while customers and staff adjust.
- **Big repair.** A major piece of equipment fails in year one.
- **Combined case.** A moderate version of several at once.

Look at your own history for guidance on size: how far did revenue fall in the business's worst year, or in its industry in the last recession?

**What to do with the results**

- **Cushion.** Hold cash or an available credit line large enough to cover payments through your downside case. See [working capital lines](/q/wc-credit-line).
- **Price and structure.** If a modest drop breaks the deal, pay less, use more equity, or put more of the price in a seller note with flexible terms. See [price vs. terms](/q/price-vs-terms).
- **Cost plan.** Know in advance which costs you would cut and in what order.
- **Walk away** if the deal only works when nothing goes wrong.

Related: [macro exposure](/q/macro-exposure) helps you pick which shocks matter for this business; [how much you can afford](/q/affordability) sets the starting point.
