**Short answer.** There's no single official cutoff. Customer concentration means a large share of revenue comes from a few customers; it becomes "too much" when losing one of them would leave the business unable to pay its loan and its people. Measure it, find out why those customers buy, and check whether the relationships belong to the business or to the owner.

**How to measure it**

- Revenue from each of the top customers as a share of total revenue, for each of the last few years and the last 12 months.
- The same for gross profit, since a big customer may also be a low-margin one.
- Contract terms: length, renewal dates, termination rights, and whether a contract can be assigned to a new owner.

**Questions that matter more than the percentage**

- **Who holds the relationship?** If the customer buys because of the owner personally, the risk rises when the owner leaves. See [owner dependence](/q/owner-dependence).
- **How long have they bought, and why?** Long history, switching costs and a contract are reassuring; a single project is not.
- **Could you replace them?** How long would it take to win equivalent revenue?
- **What does the stress test show?** Recalculate coverage without the largest customer. See [stress-testing a deal](/q/stress-test).

**What lenders look at**

When an SBA lender requires a quality of earnings (QoE) report, under SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026) that report must assess the quality and sustainability of revenue, including customer concentration risk and contract continuity.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-qoe-revenue-quality]

**Ways buyers handle it in a deal**

A lower price, more of the price paid later through a seller note, a seller transition plan focused on introducing you to the top customers, or customer conversations before closing (with the seller's agreement).
