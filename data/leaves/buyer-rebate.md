**Short answer.** A buyer rebate is money the seller pays back to the buyer after closing if the business underperforms. An earnout is the reverse: the buyer pays the seller more if the business hits targets. Under SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), seller earnouts are prohibited in an SBA 7(a) acquisition, but buyer rebates tied to business performance are allowed, and any rebate must be used to pay down the 7(a) loan.

**The SBA rules**

- **Earnouts out, rebates in.** SBA prohibits seller earnouts in a change of ownership but allows buyer rebates based on business performance because they benefit the borrower.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-earnouts]
- **Rebates go to the loan.** If the borrower receives a rebate from the seller, the money must be applied to the principal of the 7(a) loan that funded the purchase.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-seller-rebate-principal] Doing so does not trigger a subsidy recoupment fee.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-rebate-no-recoupment]
- **A working capital true-up isn't a rebate.** Cash paid to the buyer under a working capital adjustment in the purchase agreement can stay in the business.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-working-capital-true-up] See [post-closing true-ups](/q/post-closing-true-up).

**How a rebate differs from an earnout in practice**

- **Who holds the money.** In an earnout, the buyer keeps the contingent part of the price until it's earned. In a rebate, the seller has already been paid and must pay some back. Collecting from a seller who has spent or invested the money is harder.
- **What it does to the price.** An earnout lets the seller argue for a higher headline price that's only paid if the business performs. A rebate starts from the full price and reduces it if the business doesn't.
- **Where the money goes.** Under SBA rules, a rebate pays down your loan; it doesn't come back to the business as cash for operations.

**Making a rebate collectible**

Because the seller holds the cash, think about how you would actually get paid:

- **An escrow or holdback** of part of the price for the rebate period. See [escrow and holdbacks](/q/escrow-holdback).
- **Set-off against a seller note**, if there is one, subject to the lender's standby and subordination terms. Ask your lender how they'd treat it.
- **A personal guarantee** from the seller of the rebate obligation.

**Drafting points**

The same issues that cause earnout disputes apply here: a precise metric, fixed accounting rules, a defined measurement period, reporting, and a way to resolve disagreements. See [how earnouts go wrong](/q/earnout-disputes). A seller has every reason to argue that the buyer's decisions caused the shortfall, so expect that argument and address it in the terms.

**When to use one**

A rebate can bridge a gap when the seller is confident about something you can't verify yet, such as a large customer renewing or a new contract ramping up. If the seller won't accept a rebate on a claim they're making, that tells you something. See [the SBA earnout ban](/q/earnout-ban-forgivable-note) and [earnouts](/q/earnouts). Your lawyer, CPA and lender decide how it's written.
