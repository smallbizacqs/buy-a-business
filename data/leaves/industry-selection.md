**Short answer.** Narrow to one to three industries where your skills give you an edge, where there are enough businesses of the right size to find one, and where you'd be glad to work for years. Then rule out anything you can't license, can't finance or don't want to do. A short written reason for each industry (an "industry thesis") keeps your search focused and helps owners and brokers take you seriously.

**Filters to run, in order**

1. **Your edge.** What do you already know: an operating skill (sales, field service management, finance), a customer type, a trade? The SBA's guide tells buyers to be honest about their skills and experience and use them to rule out unrealistic businesses.[^sba-buy-business-skills] A lender will also write up your industry experience and who will run the business day to day.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-management-experience] See [buying without industry experience](/q/no-industry-experience).
2. **Can you legally run it?** Licenses depend on the business's activities and location, at the state, county and city level.[^sba-licenses-permits] If the business runs under a professional license you don't hold and can't get, you'll depend on a licensed employee. See [license dependence](/q/license-dependence).
3. **Can it be financed the way you plan?** Some types of business are ineligible for SBA loans, and the lender must check.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-ineligible-businesses] If you need an SBA 7(a) loan, take those off the list now.
4. **Are there enough targets?** You need many businesses in your size range to find one that's for sale and good. The Census Bureau's County Business Patterns gives yearly counts of establishments with paid employees by industry and employment size, at state and county level.[^census-cbp-establishments] It's a quick way to see whether your area has dozens or thousands of candidates.
5. **Will the demand last?** Ask what customers would do if the business disappeared, whether the service is required (by law, safety or breakdown) or optional, and how exposed it is to recessions, technology and AI. See [resilient businesses](/q/resilient-businesses) and [AI disruption](/q/ai-disruption).
6. **Can you hire for it?** Check whether skilled workers in the trade are hard to find locally. See [labor availability](/q/labor-availability).
7. **Do you want the life?** Hours, physical work, emergencies, seasonality, and the customers you'd deal with daily. See [hours and lifestyle](/q/hours-lifestyle).

**Writing an industry thesis**

For each industry on your shortlist, write a paragraph: why demand is durable, why owners in it may sell, what a good one looks like in numbers (size, margins, customer mix), what tends to go wrong, and why you'd be a good owner. Keep it a working document; update it as you learn.

**Testing the shortlist**

- Read listings in each industry and note prices, earnings and common problems.
- Talk to a few owners, employees, suppliers or lenders who know the industry. Ask what separates good businesses from bad ones and what they'd check before buying.
- Look at trade association materials and industry publications to learn the vocabulary and the key numbers people in the industry track.

**One industry or several?**

Fewer industries let you learn faster, sound more credible to owners, and compare deals well. More industries give you more deal flow. One middle path is a primary industry with one or two adjacent ones that use similar skills and customers. Whatever you choose, write it into your [buy box](/q/define-buy-box) along with industries you'll avoid; see [businesses to avoid](/q/businesses-to-avoid) and [why buy a boring business](/q/boring-business-thesis).
