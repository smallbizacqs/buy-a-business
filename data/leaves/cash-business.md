**Short answer.** Treat it as zero. Income that isn't on the tax returns can't be verified, an SBA lender won't count it, and paying for it means paying for a tax problem. Price the business on reported, documented earnings. If the seller insists the business earns more, that's their reason to accept a lower price, not yours to pay a higher one.

**Why it can't be counted**

- **It's a tax issue.** A business must report all of its income on its tax return, including cash, unless the law excludes it.[^irs-pub-334-report-all-income] A seller who says there is unreported income is telling you the returns are wrong.
- **The lender works from IRS records.** Under SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), the lender must verify the seller's financial data,[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-seller-tax-verification] get tax transcripts and reconcile discrepancies,[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-transcript-reconcile] and check the figures behind the business valuation against the seller's IRS transcripts.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-valuation-verified] Adjustments the lender can't analyze and justify don't count toward debt coverage.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-unsupported-adjustments]
- **Price above the valuation costs you cash.** If you agree to pay more than the independent business valuation supports, the difference has to come from your equity, not the loan.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-valuation-gap-equity]
- **You can't confirm it.** By definition there is no record. You'd be relying on the word of someone who is admitting they didn't report it.

**What it tells you about the seller**

A seller who underreported income may also have cut other corners: payroll paid in cash, sales tax not collected, workers misclassified. Look harder at those areas, and at whether reported numbers are reliable at all. A required quality of earnings report includes a cash proof that reconciles bank deposits to the income statement and tax returns,[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-qoe-cash-proof] which is where gaps show up.

**If you buy the company itself**

In a stock or membership-interest purchase, the company's past tax filings stay with the company you now own. That's a reason to weigh an asset purchase and strong protections in the purchase agreement. See [asset vs. stock purchase](/q/asset-vs-stock) and [fraud and misstated financials](/q/fraud-misstatement); your lawyer and CPA decide how to protect you.

**After you own it**

Plan to run every sale through the books. If reported revenue goes up after closing because you're now recording everything, the extra tax comes with it. Budget for that rather than for the cash the seller described.

**How to respond to the seller**

Keep it neutral: "I can only pay for earnings I can verify and the bank can lend against." Don't help a seller document or restate unreported income for the sale; that's for the seller and their own tax adviser.
