**Short answer.** Only if each contract allows it, or the other party consents. Read every contract that matters to the business for two clauses: an anti-assignment clause (the contract can't be transferred without consent) and a change-of-control clause (the other side can end or renegotiate the contract if the business's ownership changes). Which one bites depends on how you buy: an asset purchase transfers contracts by assignment, while a stock purchase keeps the same company in place but can trigger change-of-control terms.

### Which contracts to read

Start with the ones that would hurt most to lose:

- The largest customer contracts and any master service agreements.
- Supply agreements, especially exclusive or discounted ones.
- Franchise, dealer, distribution or licensing agreements (see [franchise resales](/q/franchise-transfer)).
- Software and data subscriptions the business runs on.
- Equipment leases and financing.
- Government contracts and any contract with a public entity.
- Agreements that give the business a right it depends on: a referral arrangement, a territory, a certification.

The premises lease is its own question; see [the landlord and the lease](/q/lease-assignment).

### What to look for in each

- **Assignment.** Can it be assigned at all? With consent? With consent "not unreasonably withheld"? Does a sale of substantially all assets count as an assignment?
- **Change of control.** Is a sale of the owner's shares treated as an assignment? Does it let the other side terminate?
- **Termination for convenience.** If the customer can end the contract on short notice anyway, its transfer matters less than the relationship.
- **Term and renewal.** When does it end, and does it renew on its own?
- **Personal terms.** Contracts that name the owner, depend on the owner's license, or were signed by the owner personally rather than the business.
- **Unwritten arrangements.** Some of a small business's most important relationships have no contract at all, so ask the seller which ones run on a handshake.

An SBA-required quality of earnings report must assess revenue quality, including customer concentration and contract continuity,[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-qoe-revenue-quality] so lenders look at this too.

### How the structure changes the work

- **Asset purchase.** Each contract you want is assigned to your company. Those that need consent need it before closing, or you risk the counterparty treating the transfer as a breach. See [asset or stock purchase](/q/asset-vs-stock).
- **Stock purchase.** The company stays the party to its contracts, so a pure anti-assignment clause may not be triggered, but a change-of-control clause can be.

### Getting consents

- List every contract needing consent, who decides at the other party, and the deadline.
- Agree with the seller who asks and when. Approaching customers before the deal is certain can unsettle them, so consider waiting until financing is nearly done and having the seller make the first call.
- Get consents in writing, ideally confirming the contract is in good standing.
- Decide which consents are closing conditions and which you'll accept the risk on. See [closing conditions](/q/closing-conditions).

If a key counterparty won't consent, or uses the request to renegotiate, that's information about the relationship; it may justify a price change or a new contract signed at closing. Reading these clauses closely is legal work: lawyers review them as part of [legal diligence](/q/legal-dd-scope).
