**Short answer.** Spend most of the first 100 days learning how the business actually works, keeping customers and employees, and taking control of cash. Make only the changes that are urgent or clearly safe. Big changes to prices, people, systems or the brand usually go better once you understand why things are done the way they are.

**First weeks: take control and listen**

- Control the money: bank access, who can approve payments, a weekly cash report.
- Meet every employee one-on-one. Ask what works, what's broken, and what they'd change.
- Call or visit the key customers with the seller.
- Get the seller's handover started against a written list. See [the seller's transition](/q/seller-transition-period).

**Next: understand the numbers**

- Set up monthly financial statements you trust, and a short list of key numbers (sales, gross margin, cash, receivables, backlog).
- Check that pricing, job costing and collections match what you saw in diligence.
- Track any post-closing items in the purchase agreement: the working-capital true-up, escrow release dates, the seller's obligations.

**Then: pick a few improvements**

- Fix what employees and customers raised most often.
- Plan bigger changes, such as pricing, new hires or new systems, with what you've learned. See [what not to change right away](/q/dont-change-too-fast) and [raising prices](/q/raise-prices).

**Compliance dates worth putting on the calendar:**

- For anyone you hire, Section 2 of Form I-9 must be completed within 3 business days of their first day of work for pay.[^uscis-i9-section-2-timing]
- If you bought assets, Form 8594 generally goes with your income tax return for the year of the sale; your CPA prepares it.[^irs-form-8594-when-to-file]
- License renewals, insurance renewals, and loan reporting your lender requires.

See [reporting and financial controls](/q/financial-controls-kpis) and [why acquisitions fail after closing](/q/post-close-failure).
