**Short answer.** When you sign the purchase agreement on one day and close on a later one, the agreement binds both sides in the gap, but you don't own the business yet. The agreement controls what happens in between: the seller promises to keep running the business normally (interim covenants), each side has things that must be true or done before it has to close (closing conditions), and the agreement says what happens if something goes wrong. The gap lets you finish things that need a signed deal, such as consents, licenses or a franchisor's approval, at the cost of exposure to what happens in the meantime.

### Why there might be a gap

- **Third-party approvals** that won't be given until there's a signed agreement: a landlord, a franchisor, key customers, a licensing agency.
- **Financing** that needs a signed purchase agreement before final approval. For an SBA loan, the lender's file must include the purchase agreement with all schedules and amendments.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-purchase-agreement-in-file]
- **Locking in the deal** while employees, customers or regulators are told.
- **Timing**: closing at a month-end or after a busy season.

If nothing needs a signed agreement first, signing and closing on the same day avoids the risks below. See [what happens on closing day](/q/closing-day).

### What interim covenants do

Interim covenants are the seller's promises about how the business will be run between signing and closing. They can cover:

- **Ordinary course.** Run the business as it has been run: keep customers served, staff paid, equipment maintained and bills current.
- **Things the seller can't do without your consent**: sell assets, take on debt, sign or end major contracts, change pay or hire key people, change prices or credit terms sharply, pull cash or inventory out beyond normal levels, or settle lawsuits.
- **Access and information.** You keep access to the books, the premises and key people, and get regular updates.
- **Help with closing.** The seller cooperates on consents, license transfers and the lender's requirements.
- **No shopping the deal.** The seller doesn't talk to other buyers.

You get approval rights, not control. You can't direct operations before closing, and you should avoid acting as though you can.

### Closing conditions in the gap

The agreement lists what must be true at closing for each side to be obliged to close: financing funded, consents received, the seller's representations still true, the seller's covenants performed, and no material adverse change. With an SBA loan, the lender must have its collateral and every condition in its credit memo met before or at disbursement.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-conditions-before-disbursement] See [closing conditions](/q/closing-conditions).

### What can still go wrong

- **The business changes.** A key customer leaves, a key employee quits, revenue drops or something breaks.
- **A representation stops being true.** The agreement should say whether the seller must update its disclosures and what that means for your right to walk away or claim later.
- **A consent or approval is refused or delayed.**
- **Financing falls through**, or the lender adds new conditions.
- **The seller gets cold feet** or tests whether you'll accept less. See [seller surprises](/q/seller-surprises).
- **Working capital moves.** Cash, receivables and inventory change between signing and closing; a working capital adjustment at closing handles that. See [the peg](/q/peg).

### Terms to settle in the agreement

- An outside date after which either side can end the agreement if closing hasn't happened.
- What happens to any deposit if a condition fails, and whose fault it was.
- Whether you can walk away for a material adverse change, and how it's defined.
- Whether you can recover from the seller for a breach of the interim covenants.

Your lawyer drafts these terms; the business points (which actions need your consent, which conditions matter to you) come from your diligence. See [key purchase agreement terms](/q/apa-key-terms).
