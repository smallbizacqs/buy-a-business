**Short answer.** List every system the business runs on (accounting, point of sale, scheduling, customer records, inventory, payroll, email and files), see each one in use, and test whether its data agrees with the others and with the bank. Good systems with clean data make diligence faster and the first months easier; weak ones mean the numbers you're relying on are harder to trust, and you'll spend time and money fixing them after closing.

### Build the system map

For each system, write down:

- What it does and who uses it.
- Whether it's off-the-shelf software, custom software or a spreadsheet.
- Who owns the account and pays for it (the business or the owner personally).
- Whether the subscription or license can be transferred, and at what cost. See [whether contracts transfer](/q/contract-assignment).
- How data gets in (typed, imported, synced) and where it goes next.
- What would happen if it stopped working tomorrow.

Ask to watch staff do a normal day's work: take an order, schedule a job, send an invoice, close the month. Workarounds, double entry and paper notes show up quickly.

### Test the data

- **Does it reconcile?** Sales in the point-of-sale or billing system should match the accounting system, which should match bank deposits. A gap that nobody can explain is a finding. An SBA-required quality of earnings report reconciles accountant-prepared statements, tax returns, internal statements and IRS transcript data,[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-qoe-reconcile] but the operating systems below the accounting are yours to check.
- **Is it complete?** Customer records with contact details and history, inventory counts that match the shelf, job records that match invoices.
- **Is it consistent?** Duplicate customers, inconsistent product codes and free-text fields make the customer analysis in [revenue quality and churn](/q/revenue-quality-churn) hard or impossible.
- **How far back does it go?** If the business changed systems recently, find out whether the older data was moved or is in an old account you'll need access to.
- **Can you export it?** Ask for exports of the main tables. If the seller can't produce them, you'll have trouble too.

### Records beyond the software

- Employee files, contracts, licenses and insurance documents: where they're kept and how complete they are.
- Email and shared files: whose accounts they're in and whether business email sits on a personal address.
- Passwords and admin access: who has them, and whether former employees or contractors still do.

### Security and privacy

- Is customer payment data handled by a payment processor, or stored by the business?
- Does the business hold sensitive data (health, financial, children's) that carries specific legal duties?
- Has there been a breach, ransomware incident or lost device?
- Are backups taken and tested?

### What it means for the deal

- **Cost to fix.** A system that has to be replaced, data that has to be cleaned up, or a custom tool only one contractor understands is a cost you'll carry after closing.
- **Handover.** Build account transfers, admin access and data exports into the closing checklist. See [day-one readiness](/q/day-one-readiness).
- **People.** If one employee is the only person who knows how the system works, see [key employee risk](/q/key-employee-risk).

Ownership of code, domains and online accounts is covered in [IP, code and domains](/q/ip-tech); using AI tools to review the data is covered in [AI-assisted diligence](/q/ai-assisted-diligence).
