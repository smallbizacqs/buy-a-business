**Short answer.** Consider a second deal when the first business is stable without your daily attention: a manager handles operations, monthly financial statements are reliable, cash flow comfortably covers debt, and you have a clear reason the two businesses are worth more together. Buy for a specific gain (customers, capacity, a new service or territory, a key team), check the target as carefully as the first, and plan the integration before you close.

### Are you ready?

Ask honestly:

- **Is the first business stable?** Revenue, margins and key people are holding, and the problems you found after closing are fixed or under control.
- **Can it run without you for weeks at a time?** A second deal and its integration will take much of your attention. See [making the business run without you](/q/professionalize-systems).
- **Is the cash there?** Debt coverage on the first business is comfortable, with a cushion, and you have cash or financing for the equity of the second.
- **Do you have the systems?** Reporting, payroll and controls that can absorb another company.

If the answer to any of these is no, fix that before the next deal. See [buying too much too fast](/q/too-many-too-fast).

### Why buy: the reasons that hold up

- **Customers or territory.** Adding customers you can serve with your existing team and overhead.
- **Capacity or people.** Licensed technicians, a trained crew or a manager you couldn't hire.
- **A service your customers already ask for.**
- **Purchasing or overhead savings** you can actually count, such as one office, one software system or combined buying.

Be wary of a deal whose main reason is that it's available, or that it's cheaper than your first business. A lower price can reflect higher risk.

### Add-on or separate platform?

An add-on (or tuck-in) is merged into the first business: one name, one team, shared systems. A separate company stays independent, perhaps under a holding company. Merging saves more overhead but disrupts more; keeping it separate preserves the brand and team but saves less. See [integration](/q/integration) and [running several companies](/q/holdco-operations).

### Financing a second deal with an SBA loan

Under SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), SBA 7(a) loans (bank loans partly guaranteed by the U.S. Small Business Administration) treat change-of-ownership deals as one of four types, including Business Expansion.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-change-types] A Business Expansion is an existing business buying another, and qualifies when the buyer has operated for at least two full fiscal years under current ownership and buys 100% of the other business.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-business-expansion]

- **Equity.** The required equity injection is 10%, which the lender may reduce or eliminate if it decides the borrower has enough liquidity and working capital.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-business-expansion-equity]
- **Debt coverage.** The minimum debt service coverage ratio is 1.15:1 for a Business Expansion, against 1.25:1 for an initial acquisition.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-dsc-ratio]
- **Your personal finances.** The lender runs a global cash flow analysis, including affiliates, that must meet 1:1.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-global-cash-flow]
- **Limits.** SBA's outstanding guaranty to one business and its affiliates combined can't exceed $3,750,000,[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-affiliate-guaranty-cap] so existing SBA loans reduce what's available for the next one.

SBA rules change; confirm the current SOP with your lender. Seller financing, conventional bank loans and investors are other routes.

### Diligence and integration

Do full diligence on the target, as you did on the first. Familiarity with the industry helps, but doesn't replace checking its earnings, customers, people and contracts. Write an integration plan before closing: who leads it, what changes on day one, what changes later, and what stays as is.
