**Short answer.** You need some environmental review when you're buying or borrowing against real estate, and a Phase I environmental site assessment (a records-and-site review by an environmental professional) when the property's current or past use carries contamination risk. With an SBA loan, the lender decides what's required based on the property's use and the loan size: a property whose current or known prior use is on the SBA's list of environmentally sensitive industries starts with a Phase I regardless of loan size. If you're only buying a business's assets and leasing the space, ask the lender what it needs and whether the business's own operations create risk.

### The SBA rule

Under SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), SBA requires an environmental investigation of all commercial property offered as collateral by a mortgage, deed of trust or leasehold deed of trust, scaled to the risk of contamination.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-environmental-investigation] The steps:

- **Sensitive industries.** The lender compares the property's current and known prior uses with SBA's list of environmentally sensitive industries. A match means the investigation must begin with a Phase I, whatever the loan amount.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-environmental-phase-i-naics]
- **No match.** For a loan up to $250,000 the investigation may begin with an environmental questionnaire; above $250,000 it must begin at least with a questionnaire and a records search with risk assessment.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-environmental-loan-size]
- **Escalation.** If the records search finds the property is anything other than low risk, the lender must obtain a Phase I.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-environmental-escalation]
- **Timing.** Reports must be dated within one year of the date the SBA loan number is issued.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-environmental-report-date]

The lender may pass the cost of environmental reports to you as an out-of-pocket closing expense.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-out-of-pocket]

### What a Phase I is

For SBA purposes, a Phase I is an assessment that complies with the federal All Appropriate Inquiries (AAI) rule, done by an environmental professional under the current ASTM standard, E1527-21.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-phase-i-definition] It reviews records, past uses and the site itself; it doesn't sample soil or water. If the professional recommends further investigation, that is typically a Phase II, which involves sampling.

The AAI rule exists for federal Superfund law (CERCLA): it applies to buyers seeking protections such as the innocent landowner defense and bona fide prospective purchaser status.[^ecfr-40-cfr-312-1-applicability] To count, the inquiries must be done within one year before you acquire the property and include an inquiry by an environmental professional.[^ecfr-40-cfr-312-20-aai] That's a reason to get a Phase I on property you're buying even without a lender requiring one.

### When to think about it even without real estate

- **The business uses hazardous materials**: auto repair, dry cleaning, printing, manufacturing, fuel, chemicals. Even as a tenant, a lease can make you responsible for contamination it causes.
- **You're signing a new lease.** Read the environmental clauses, and ask whether the landlord has a recent Phase I.
- **Permits.** Some operations need air, water or waste permits that must transfer. See [licenses and permits](/q/licenses-permits-transfer) and [regulatory compliance](/q/regulatory-compliance).

### Questions to ask

- What has the property been used for over its history, including before the seller?
- Are there underground tanks, floor drains or past spills?
- Has any environmental report been done, and can you get it?
- Who pays if a problem is found: the seller, the landlord or you?

If a report finds a problem, the options are to price it, have the seller fix it before closing, get an indemnity, or walk away. For property bought as part of the deal, see [real estate in the deal](/q/real-estate-in-deal).
