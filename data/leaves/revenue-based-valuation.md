**Short answer.** Accounting and tax practices, insurance agencies, wealth management firms and some other client-based businesses are often quoted as a percentage or multiple of annual revenue rather than a multiple of profit. The quote reflects how sticky the client base is. Treat it as shorthand for comparing deals, then check the price against what the cash flow can support, because the lender and your own budget run on cash flow, not revenue.

**Why these sectors quote revenue**

- **Profit depends on the owner.** In a practice, the owner's pay, workload and spending vary so much that reported profit says little about what a new owner would earn. Revenue is more comparable from one firm to the next.
- **Clients are the asset.** What you're buying is a book of recurring clients or policies. Revenue that renews each year is the thing being priced.
- **A buyer that already has a firm can fold the clients into it**, where many costs are already covered, so the revenue can be worth more to that buyer than the standalone profit suggests.

**What moves the percentage**

- **Retention.** How many clients stay year after year, and how many will stay after the owner leaves.
- **Recurring vs. one-time.** Annual engagements, renewing policies or fee-based assets vs. project or transactional work.
- **Client mix and concentration.** A few large clients add risk. See [customer concentration](/q/customer-concentration).
- **Owner relationships.** Whether clients know the staff or only the owner. See [owner dependence](/q/owner-dependence).
- **Margins and pricing.** Low fees or underpriced services lower what the revenue is worth.
- **Licensing and transfer rules** for the clients or the business. See [licenses you don't hold](/q/license-dependence).

Ask whoever quotes a percentage which of these it assumes.

**Check it against cash flow**

A revenue price still has to be paid from profit. Recast the P&L as you would run it: your salary or a replacement's, staff, rent, software, insurance. Then see whether that profit covers loan payments with room to spare.

For an SBA 7(a) loan, under SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), the lender tests debt service coverage on EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization),[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-dsc-definition] at 1.25:1 for an initial acquisition.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-dsc-ratio] If the price is higher than the independent business valuation, the difference must come from equity.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-valuation-gap-equity] A required quality of earnings report assesses customer concentration, contract continuity and whether revenue and margins are likely to hold after the sale.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-qoe-revenue-quality]

**Paying for retention**

Outside SBA deals, practice sales sometimes adjust the price later based on how many clients stay. In an SBA 7(a) acquisition, seller earnouts are prohibited, though buyer rebates tied to performance are allowed.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-earnouts] See [buyer rebates](/q/buyer-rebate) and [earnouts](/q/earnouts).
