**Short answer.** The peg is the target amount of net working capital (receivables and inventory, less payables and similar short-term bills) that the seller agrees to leave in the business at closing. It's usually based on what the business has normally carried over a recent period, adjusted for seasons and one-off items. After closing, the actual figure is compared with the peg and the price moves up or down by the difference. That comparison is the true-up.

**Setting it:**

- **Agree the definition first**: which accounts count, and how each is measured (for example, how doubtful receivables and old inventory are treated).
- **Look at a normal level**: buyers often look at month-end working capital over a trailing period, so seasonal swings even out. Ask your accountant which period and method fit this business.
- **Adjust for one-offs**: a large prepayment, a big year-end purchase, or anything that won't recur.
- **Write it down**: put the definition and a sample calculation in an exhibit to the purchase agreement.

**The true-up:**

1. Before closing, the seller estimates closing working capital, and the price paid at closing is adjusted using that estimate.
2. After closing, the buyer prepares the actual figure within an agreed number of days.
3. The seller reviews it and can dispute it, within a set window.
4. Disputes go to an agreed independent accountant.
5. The difference is paid, sometimes from an escrow or holdback set aside for this.

Some agreements add a band (a collar) where small differences are ignored. That's a negotiated term, not a rule.

**SBA note.** Under SBA SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), a working-capital true-up in the purchase agreement is not a rebate to the buyer, so cash the buyer receives from it may stay in the business.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-working-capital-true-up] An SBA 7(a) loan is a bank loan partly guaranteed by the U.S. Small Business Administration (SBA).
