**Short answer.** Give each company its own capable leader and keep the holding company small: a few shared services that are clearly cheaper or better done once (accounting, payroll, insurance, purchasing, sometimes HR and IT), a common reporting format and a monthly review of every company, and a clear set of rules for how cash moves between companies and where it gets reinvested. Your main jobs become choosing and supporting leaders, allocating capital, and watching the numbers.

### Decide what's central and what's local

**Shared at the holding company** (if it saves money or improves quality):

- Accounting, the monthly close and financial reporting.
- Payroll, benefits and insurance.
- Purchasing of common items and services.
- Banking, debt and cash management.
- HR policies, hiring support and IT.

**Left with each company:**

- Customer relationships, sales and service delivery.
- Day-to-day decisions and the local team.
- Its name and brand, unless you've decided to integrate. See [integration](/q/integration).

Centralizing too much can slow the companies down and blur who is accountable; too little leaves savings on the table. Start with finance and reporting, then add shared services where the benefit is clear.

### Leaders and accountability

Each company needs one person accountable for its results. Agree with each leader on the year's goals and the scorecard, give them authority to match, and tie part of their pay to results they can influence. Hold a monthly review with each: financial results against budget, the scorecard, priorities and problems. Bring leaders together from time to time to share what's working.

### Common reporting

Use the same chart of accounts, the same monthly reporting package and the same closing schedule across companies, so you can compare them and spot problems early. Each company still needs its own books. See [reporting and key numbers](/q/financial-controls-kpis).

### Capital allocation

Decide, at the holding company level:

- How much cash each company keeps for its own needs and as a cushion.
- How excess cash moves up (distributions or dividends, management fees, loan repayments) and what loan agreements allow.
- Where to reinvest: growth in an existing company, debt paydown, a new acquisition, or payouts to owners.

Compare each option on its expected return and risk. Be careful moving cash from a strong company to prop up a weak one; it can hide problems and weaken both.

### Legal, tax and lending structure

How you set up the holding company and its subsidiaries affects taxes, liability and financing. The entity type determines which tax return a business files, and legal and tax considerations both enter into the choice.[^irs-business-structures] Charges between companies (management fees, shared-service costs, loans) should be documented in writing and priced on a basis your CPA can support. Keep each company's bank accounts, contracts and records separate.

Lenders look at the group. Under SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), an SBA 7(a) lender runs a global cash flow analysis that includes affiliated businesses and must meet 1:1,[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-global-cash-flow] and SBA's outstanding guaranty to one business and its affiliates combined can't exceed $3,750,000.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-affiliate-guaranty-cap] Loan covenants may also restrict distributions or transfers between companies. Work out the structure with your CPA, lawyer and lender before the second company joins.

### Common mistakes

- An owner who still runs one company day to day while trying to oversee the others.
- A growing holding-company staff whose cost outpaces the savings.
- Moving cash between companies without documentation.
- No common reporting, so problems surface late.
