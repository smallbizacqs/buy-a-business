**Short answer.** Make a calendar from the purchase agreement on closing day: the date your closing balance sheet or true-up statement is due, the seller's window to dispute it, how disputes get resolved, each escrow or holdback release date, and the deadlines for indemnity claims. Assign an owner to each date, keep the records that support your numbers, and send any notice in the form and to the address the agreement requires.

### What these terms mean

- **Working-capital true-up.** The purchase agreement sets a target level of working capital (the "peg"; see [the peg](/q/peg)). After closing, the actual working capital at closing is measured, and the price moves up or down by the difference.
- **Escrow or holdback.** Part of the price held back after closing, by an escrow agent or by you, as security for claims or the true-up. See [escrows and holdbacks](/q/escrow-holdback).
- **Indemnity claim.** A claim against the seller for losses from a breach of a representation or another covered matter, made under the agreement's procedures. See [indemnification](/q/indemnification).

### Build the calendar

Read the purchase agreement, the escrow agreement and any seller note, and list each date and requirement:

1. **Closing statement.** Who prepares it, by when, and what accounting principles and sample calculations it must follow.
2. **Review and dispute period.** How long the other side has to object, and what an objection must contain.
3. **Resolution.** Whether disputes go to an independent accountant, how that person is chosen, and who pays.
4. **Payment.** How and when the adjustment is paid: from escrow, by the seller, by you, or by changing the seller note.
5. **Escrow or holdback releases.** Dates and amounts, and what happens to money tied to a pending claim.
6. **Claim deadlines.** How long each type of representation survives, any minimum claim amount, and the notice requirements.

Put each on a shared calendar with reminders well before the deadline, and name who is responsible: you, your CPA or your lawyer.

### Prepare the true-up carefully

- Close the books for the closing date promptly and get receivables, payables, inventory and accruals right.
- Follow the agreement's definitions and any sample calculation exactly. Departures invite disputes.
- Keep support for each line: aging reports, inventory counts, invoices, bank statements.
- Note receivables that turn out to be uncollectible and inventory that's obsolete, since the agreement may say how they're treated.

### Claims and notices

If you find a problem that may be an indemnity claim, write it down with dates and evidence and tell your lawyer quickly. Agreements require notice within set periods and in a set way, and a late or informal notice can weaken a claim. If you hold a seller note, don't simply stop paying to offset a claim unless the documents allow it and your lawyer agrees. See [set-off rights](/q/set-off-default).

### Rules that can apply

- **SBA loans.** Under SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), a working-capital true-up in the purchase agreement isn't treated as a rebate to the borrower, and cash paid to the borrower under it may be kept for the business's working capital rather than used to pay down the loan.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-working-capital-true-up] Tell your lender about any adjustment anyway.
- **Taxes.** In an asset purchase, buyer and seller each report the sale on IRS Form 8594.[^irs-form-8594-who-files] If the amount allocated to any asset changes in a later tax year, the affected party attaches a supplemental Form 8594 to its return for that year.[^irs-form-8594-allocation-change] Send the final numbers to your CPA.
