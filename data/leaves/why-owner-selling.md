**Short answer.** Ask directly and early, then test the answer against everything else you learn: the trend in the financials, the lease and contracts, what's changing in the industry, and how the owner talks about the future. A stated reason such as retirement can be true and also incomplete. What you're looking for is anything about the business itself, not the owner's life, that's pushing the sale, because that becomes your problem after closing.

**Ask the question several ways**

- "Why are you selling, and why now?"
- "What would you do if the business didn't sell?"
- "If you were staying another five years, what would you change or invest in?"
- "What's the hardest part of running it today?"
- "What do you see coming in the industry over the next few years?"

Ask again at later meetings. A consistent, specific answer is reassuring; one that shifts is worth noting.

**Reasons and what they can signal**

- **Retirement or age.** Can be straightforward. Check whether the owner has been easing off: flat revenue, no recent investment in equipment or marketing, and relationships that depend on them.
- **Health or family.** Personal, but a sudden sale can mean the business has been under-managed recently.
- **Burnout.** Ask what's exhausting. Long hours because the owner does everything is fixable; constant fires because of staffing, customers or margins may not be.
- **Partner disagreement.** Find out who controls the decision and whether all owners will sign.
- **Moving on to something else.** Ask what, and why now.
- **"Time to cash in" after a great year.** Check whether the great year is repeatable or a peak.

**Signs the business is driving the sale**

- Revenue or margins falling in the most recent period.
- A large customer leaving or a key contract up for renewal.
- A lease ending soon, a rent increase, or a landlord who won't extend.
- Equipment, vehicles or facilities that need major spending.
- A new competitor, a regulation change, or technology that threatens the service.
- Key employees about to leave, or a license that depends on the owner.
- Legal disputes, tax problems or debts.

**Test the story against the records**

- **Trends.** SBA lenders look at three years of results[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-three-years] and compare the latest interim statement with the same period a year earlier.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-interim-statements] Do the same yourself; a recent decline can explain the timing.
- **Tax records.** In an SBA deal the lender verifies the seller's financial data against IRS records.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-seller-tax-verification] Gaps between the books and the returns raise questions about the story too.
- **Contracts and lease.** The SBA's guide tells buyers to ask about contracts, leases, existing cash flow and inventory.[^sba-buy-business-ask-questions] Look for expiring terms that line up with the sale.
- **Customers and employees.** Later in diligence, with the seller's agreement, conversations with key customers and staff show whether anything is changing. See [customer diligence calls](/q/customer-dd-calls).

**Using what you learn**

A personal reason makes timing, transition and terms the focus of the deal. A business reason means price, structure or protections in the purchase agreement, or a decision to walk away. See [early red flags](/q/early-red-flags) and [what sellers care about besides price](/q/seller-psychology).
