**Short answer.** If you miss seller-note payments, the seller can do whatever the note and security documents allow: charge late fees or default interest, demand the whole balance, and go after any collateral or guarantee you gave. A right of offset (set-off) lets you reduce what you owe on the note by amounts the seller owes you, such as indemnity claims for breached promises in the purchase agreement. Neither exists automatically in the form you'd want; both are written into the documents, so negotiate them before closing.

### What happens on a missed payment

What follows a missed payment depends on the note:

- **Grace and cure periods.** A note can give a window to pay after a due date, or after written notice, before it's a default. Ask for both.
- **Default interest and late fees.** A higher rate or a fee while you're behind.
- **Acceleration.** The right to declare the whole balance due at once.
- **Collateral and guarantees.** If the note is secured by business assets, a pledge of your shares, or your personal guarantee, the seller can enforce them after default. A share pledge can mean losing the company.
- **Cross-default.** A clause making a default on the bank loan a default on the seller note, or the reverse. Your bank loan documents may have one too.

### When there's a senior lender

When a bank also lends, a subordination agreement typically puts the seller note behind the bank loan and limits what the seller can do. Under SBA rules, a seller note on full standby can't receive principal or interest for the term of the 7(a) loan,[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-seller-debt-equity] and the seller must subordinate liens and take no action against you or the collateral without the lender's consent.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-standby-terms] With a non-SBA lender, the intercreditor or subordination agreement sets the limits. Read it alongside the note.

On an SBA loan you'll have personally guaranteed the bank debt if you own 20% or more,[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-personal-guaranty] so a cash crunch threatens you on both loans. Talk to the bank and the seller early if you see one coming; a negotiated deferral beats a default notice.

### Set-off: using the note to cover seller claims

**Why buyers want it.** If you discover after closing that the seller broke a promise in the purchase agreement, collecting from a seller who has already spent the money is hard. Withholding from note payments is a faster remedy.

**Why sellers resist it.** A broad right lets a buyer stop paying by alleging a claim. Sellers push for limits.

**Terms that strike a balance:**

- Set-off only for claims that are finally decided, agreed, or at least notified in writing with detail.
- Disputed amounts paid into escrow instead of kept by the buyer.
- A cap tied to the indemnity cap in the purchase agreement.
- Clear interaction with any escrow or holdback, so you don't recover twice.
- Written consent from the senior lender if its agreement limits changes to payments. With an SBA standby note there may be no payments to withhold until the bank is repaid.

The note, the purchase agreement and any subordination agreement have to say the same thing. Have a lawyer draft them together. See [indemnification](/q/indemnification), [escrows and holdbacks](/q/escrow-holdback) and [seller note terms](/q/seller-note-terms).
