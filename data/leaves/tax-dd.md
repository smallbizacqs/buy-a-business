**Short answer.** Check three kinds of tax: payroll taxes the business withheld from employees, sales and use tax it collected or should have collected, and income tax. In a stock purchase, unpaid taxes stay with the company you're buying. In an asset purchase they mostly stay with the seller, but some state taxes, sales tax above all, can follow the business to a buyer. Have a CPA review the filings, and get the protection you need in the purchase agreement.

### Payroll taxes

Withheld income tax and the employees' share of Social Security and Medicare are called trust fund taxes, because the business holds the employees' money in trust until it deposits it.[^irs-tfrp-responsible-person] If the business can't pay them, the IRS can assess the Trust Fund Recovery Penalty personally against anyone responsible for collecting or paying them who willfully fails to do so.[^irs-tfrp-who] A responsible person is anyone with the duty and the power to direct collecting, accounting for and paying those taxes,[^irs-tfrp-responsible-person] which after closing includes you.

What to check:

- Quarterly payroll returns (Form 941) and annual returns against the payroll register and bank payments.
- Any IRS or state notices, payment plans or liens.
- Worker classification. People paid as contractors who are really employees create back taxes and penalties. The IRS weighs behavioral control, financial control and the type of relationship.[^irs-worker-classification] See [employee diligence](/q/employee-dd).

In an asset purchase, your company starts payroll under its own employer identification number, not the seller's.[^irs-pub-15-successor-ein]

### Sales and use tax

Sales tax is collected from customers and owed to the state, so a business that under-collected owes the shortfall itself. Whether a buyer of the assets can be held liable for the seller's unpaid sales tax, and whether you can get a clearance certificate from the state before closing, varies by state. Ask a CPA who knows the states where the business sells.

What to check:

- Which states and localities the business collects in, and whether it should collect in others, including for online or out-of-state sales.
- Sales tax returns against reported revenue.
- Exemption certificates for any sales the business treated as tax-exempt.
- Use tax on equipment and supplies bought without sales tax.

### Income tax

- **Tax transcripts.** In an SBA-financed change of ownership, the lender must verify the seller's financial data against IRS records[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-seller-tax-verification] and reconcile any discrepancies against the transcripts.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-transcript-reconcile] Whether or not you use an SBA loan, transcripts show what was actually filed and whether there are balances due.
- **Entity-level tax.** In a stock purchase, look at the company's own tax position: open years, audits and the tax status it claims. Your CPA should look at this before you choose a structure; see [asset or stock purchase](/q/asset-vs-stock).
- **State income and franchise taxes** and annual report fees, which can leave a company not in good standing if unpaid.

### Protecting yourself

- **Representations** in the purchase agreement that all taxes are filed and paid, with an indemnity for pre-closing taxes. See [reps and warranties](/q/reps-warranties) and [indemnification](/q/indemnification).
- **Paying taxes off at closing** from the seller's proceeds, or holding back part of the price until clearance arrives. See [escrows and holdbacks](/q/escrow-holdback).
- **Clearance letters** where the state offers them.

How the deal itself is taxed (allocation, asset or stock) is a separate question; see [tax structuring](/q/tax-structuring).
