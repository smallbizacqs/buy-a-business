**Short answer.** When an acquired business can't make its loan payments, the lender may first try to work things out; if that fails, it declares a default, sells the business's collateral and pursues the guarantors for what's left, which can reach personal assets including a home. Acquisitions fail for a handful of broad reasons: the buyer paid too much or borrowed too much, the earnings weren't what they seemed, customers or key people left after the sale, or the buyer and the business were a poor fit.

### How a failing deal can unfold

1. **Strain.** Cash gets tight, payments to suppliers stretch, and loan payments get harder to make.
2. **Missed payments or broken terms.** The lender takes notice. Talk to your lender early; options are wider before you're in default.
3. **Workout.** Depending on the lender and the facts, a workout can mean deferring payments, changing the loan terms, adding collateral, or selling the business while it still has value.
4. **Default and liquidation.** If no workout succeeds, the lender can call the loan, sell collateral and pursue guarantors. For SBA 7(a) loans, servicing and liquidation follow a separate SOP, 50 57.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-servicing-liquidation]

### What it means for you

- **The guarantee.** Under SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), every individual owning 20% or more of an SBA borrower gives an unlimited personal guarantee.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-personal-guaranty] If collateral falls short, the guarantors owe the rest. See [personal guarantees](/q/personal-guarantee).
- **Your home.** When business assets don't fully secure an SBA loan, the lender must take available equity in personal real estate owned by 20% owners and guarantors.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-personal-real-estate] See [collateral and your home](/q/collateral-home).
- **The SBA guarantee protects the lender, not you.** SBA guarantees part of a 7(a) loan to the lender, up to 75% for loans over $150,000.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-guaranty-percentage] It doesn't reduce what you owe.
- **Credit and next steps.** A default and guarantee call can affect your credit and future borrowing. If it gets that far, get advice from a lawyer who handles business debt and bankruptcy in your state.

### Broad reasons acquisitions fail

- **Too much debt.** A deal sized right at the minimum coverage ratio leaves no room for a bad year. SBA rules treat cash flow as the primary source of repayment, not collateral.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-cash-flow-repayment] See [stress testing](/q/stress-test).
- **Earnings that weren't real.** Aggressive add-backs, cash that never showed up in the bank, or a one-off good year. See [add-backs](/q/add-backs) and [proof of cash](/q/proof-of-cash).
- **Losses after the sale.** Customers, staff or suppliers loyal to the seller leave. See [customer concentration](/q/customer-concentration), [key employee risk](/q/key-employee-risk) and [owner dependence](/q/owner-dependence).
- **The market moved.** Demand fell, costs rose or a new competitor arrived. See [macro exposure](/q/macro-exposure).
- **Fit.** The buyer didn't like or wasn't suited to the work. See [readiness](/q/readiness).

For operating mistakes after closing, see [why businesses fail after the sale](/q/post-close-failure).

### How often it happens

This page doesn't give a failure rate: we haven't found an official figure for business acquisitions specifically. Ask lenders about their own experience with acquisition loans, and talk to owners who have been through a hard stretch.

### Reducing the odds

- Leave a cushion above the lender's minimum coverage.
- Verify earnings against bank deposits and tax returns.
- Keep a personal and business cash reserve.
- Plan the handover so relationships transfer. See [the seller's transition period](/q/seller-transition-period).
- Know when to walk away before closing. See [deal killers](/q/deal-killers).
