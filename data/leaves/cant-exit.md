**Short answer.** If no buyer appears at a price you'll accept, you still have options: keep owning it with a manager in place and take the cash flow, sell to employees or a family member over time, sell part of it, lower your price or change the terms, merge it with a competitor, or, as a last resort, wind it down and sell the assets. Find out why buyers are passing first; the reason may point to the fix, and fixing it can take a year or more.

### First, find out why it isn't selling

Ask your broker or advisor, and any buyers who looked, what stopped them. Reasons a business can be hard to sell:

- **It depends on you.** Customers, know-how or licenses are tied to the owner. See [making the business run without you](/q/professionalize-systems).
- **Earnings are unclear or falling.** Records don't support the claimed earnings, or the trend is down.
- **Too much rides on a few customers.** See [customer concentration](/q/customer-concentration).
- **The price is above what buyers can finance.** Buyers using loans can pay only what cash flow can support. See [debt service coverage](/q/dscr).
- **Industry, lease or legal issues.** A short lease, a pending dispute, or an industry buyers see as declining.

### Options

**Keep it and step back.** Put a manager in place, build reporting, and own it for the cash flow. This can be a good outcome if the business is profitable and someone capable runs daily operations. See [running several companies](/q/holdco-operations) for how owners oversee businesses they don't operate.

**Fix it and try again later.** Address the reasons buyers passed: reduce owner dependence, clean up financial statements, diversify customers, renew the lease. This takes time but widens the pool of buyers. See [increasing the value of the business](/q/value-creation).

**Sell to employees or partners.** Under SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), an SBA 7(a) loan (a bank loan partly guaranteed by the U.S. Small Business Administration) can finance a sale between existing owners or to current employees, under conditions in the SBA rules.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-owner-buyout] You may also need to finance part of it yourself with a seller note.

**Transfer to family.** Transferring a family business can carry legal effects such as estate and gift tax obligations,[^sba-family-transfer-taxes] so start with a CPA and estate lawyer.

**Change the terms.** A lower price, more seller financing, an earnout, or staying on longer can make a deal workable. Weigh the risk of being paid over time by a business you no longer control.

**Merge or sell to a competitor.** A competitor may value your customers, people or territory even if a financial buyer won't.

**Wind it down.** Sell equipment, inventory and the customer list, collect receivables, and close. A business that closes must file a final tax return for the year it closes.[^irs-closing-business-final-return] There are also employee, lease, license and contract obligations to handle; get advice from your CPA and lawyer before you start.

### If you still have acquisition debt

Selling or closing the business may not end your personal guarantee. Under SOP 50 10 8.1, every individual owning 20% or more of an SBA 7(a) borrower gives an unlimited personal guarantee.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-personal-guaranty] Read your guarantee with your lawyer to see what ends it. Any sale has to pay off or be approved by your lender. Talk to the lender early if you're considering winding down.

### Plan ahead

The best protection is planning years before you want out: build a business that doesn't depend on you, keep clean records, and get a valuation from time to time so you know where you stand. See [exit options](/q/exit-options).
