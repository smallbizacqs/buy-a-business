**Short answer.** Identify the few people the business can't run without, judge how likely each is to stay and what it would cost to replace them, and reflect that in your price and plan. Before closing, you can reduce the risk with retention bonuses, a carefully timed introduction, and making sure what they know is written down. You can't remove it entirely, so don't buy a business that only works if everyone stays.

**Who counts as key**

SBA defines a key employee as someone with the experience, qualifications or required license to run the business, or who manages it day to day.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-key-employee-definition] In practice, also include anyone who holds the main customer relationships, does work no one else can, or knows how the systems and pricing work.

**How to judge the risk before an offer**

Ask the seller, early and in confidence:

- Who would you call first if you were away for a month?
- Which employees do customers ask for by name?
- Who holds licenses, certifications or the relationships with key suppliers?
- How long has each been there, how are they paid compared with the market, and do they have any ownership promise, side deal or expectation of buying the business themselves?
- Has anyone been told about the sale?

Then look for signs: pay well below market, a long-time manager who expected to be offered the business, or a team whose loyalty is to the owner personally.

**Why the lender cares**

Under SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), the lender's credit memo describes the buyer's industry experience and who will run day-to-day operations.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-management-experience] Where a quality of earnings report is required, it assesses whether revenue and margins are likely to hold after the sale.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-qoe-revenue-quality] A business whose results depend on one manager is a business whose earnings are less certain.

**Ways to price and structure it**

- **Price.** Lower your multiple, or set aside the cost of a replacement hire and a few months of overlap.
- **Retention bonuses.** Paid over time after closing to the people who matter most, funded by the seller, you, or both. Write down the terms.
- **Meet them before closing.** Agree with the seller when and how. Too early risks leaks and departures; too late leaves you buying blind. See [employee due diligence](/q/employee-dd).
- **Employment terms.** Offer letters or agreements, and where allowed by state law, non-solicitation terms. Have a lawyer check what's enforceable where you are.
- **Document the knowledge.** Use the transition period to get processes, pricing and passwords written down. See [the seller's transition period](/q/seller-transition-period).

**After closing**

Talk to key people in your first days, explain what will and won't change, and keep promises small and specific. See [keeping employees](/q/employee-retention) and [announcing the sale](/q/announce-to-employees).
