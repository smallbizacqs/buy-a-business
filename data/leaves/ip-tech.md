**Short answer.** Make a list of every name, mark, domain, piece of software, content library and online account the business uses, then check who legally owns or controls each one: the business, the owner personally, an employee, a contractor or a vendor. Anything not owned by the business has to be transferred to it, or to you, at closing, in writing, with the logins handed over.

### Build the inventory

- **Names and trademarks.** The business name, product names and logos, and whether any are registered with the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) or a state.
- **Domains.** Every domain the business uses or redirects, with the registrar and the account it's registered under.
- **Software and code.** Custom software, websites, apps and integrations, where the code is stored, and who wrote it.
- **Content.** Photos, videos, course materials, templates and written content the business sells or markets with.
- **Online accounts.** Social media, review profiles, marketplace seller accounts, ad accounts, email marketing lists, payment processors and app store accounts.
- **Licensed tools.** Software, data and fonts the business uses under someone else's license.

### Check who owns each item

**Trademarks.** Look up registrations in the USPTO's trademark search and check the listed owner. When a business is sold, the trademark moves by a transfer of ownership called an assignment,[^uspto-trademark-assignment] and the USPTO's Assignment Recordation Branch records assignment documents for patents and trademarks,[^uspto-assignment-recordation] so you can see the recorded chain of ownership and record your own assignment after closing. A registration in the owner's personal name, or in an old company's name, needs its own assignment.

**Code and content.** Under U.S. copyright law, work an employee creates within the scope of their job is a work made for hire, and the employer is its author.[^copyright-circ30-work-for-hire] Work by an outside contractor qualifies as a work made for hire only if it meets every statutory requirement, including a signed written agreement saying so; if it fails any of them, it isn't one.[^copyright-circ30-work-for-hire] So for anything built by freelancers or an agency, ask for the contract and look for a written assignment of rights to the business. If there isn't one, get one signed before closing.

**Domains and accounts.** In practice, a domain or online account is controlled by whoever holds its login, which may be tied to the owner's personal email. Check the registrar record and the account settings, not the seller's description. Platform terms sometimes limit how accounts can be transferred; read them before you rely on taking over an account.

**Open source and licensed code.** Ask what third-party code and services the software depends on and under what licenses, especially if the business sells software.

### Get it transferred properly

- **In the purchase agreement:** a list of the IP and accounts being transferred, a representation that the business owns them and that nothing infringes someone else's rights, and a covenant to deliver credentials.
- **At closing:** signed assignments for trademarks, domains and copyrights, and a handover of logins, two-factor devices and recovery emails, with passwords changed once you're in.
- **After closing:** record trademark assignments with the USPTO, update registrar and account ownership, and remove the seller's access.

An SBA 7(a) loan can finance intangible assets in an acquisition, including trademarks and intellectual property, as long as the SBA's financial due diligence rules are met.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-intangibles] For the systems the business runs on and the quality of its data, see [software, records and data](/q/systems-data).
