**Short answer.** Do two things. First, review the seller's insurance as diligence: the policies, what they cover, and the claims history, which tells you about risks the financials don't. Second, arrange your own coverage to start the moment you take over, including whatever your lender requires. With an SBA 7(a) loan that includes hazard insurance on the collateral and, in some cases, life insurance on you.

### Review the seller's insurance

Ask for:

- **Every current policy** with declarations pages: general liability, property, commercial auto, workers' compensation, professional liability (errors and omissions), product liability, cyber, umbrella, and any industry-specific coverage.
- **Loss runs** for the last three to five years. A loss run is the insurer's report of claims and payments. Frequent workers' comp claims, auto accidents or liability claims can point to operational problems and affect your premiums.
- **Premium history** and any non-renewal, cancellation or rate increase notices.
- **Required coverage** under the lease, customer contracts and licenses. Some customers require specific limits or naming them as additional insured.

What to look for: gaps (no workers' comp where employees exist, no professional liability for advice-based work), limits that are low for the business's risks, open claims, and coverage that depends on the seller personally.

### Arrange your own coverage

**What happens to the seller's policies.** In an asset purchase, your new company generally needs its own policies from closing; the seller's policies stay with the seller. In a stock purchase, the company's policies may continue, but check whether the policy treats a change of ownership as a reason to cancel or re-underwrite. Ask the insurer or a broker either way.

**Claims from before closing.** Some coverage, such as professional liability, can be written on a claims-made basis, covering only claims made while the policy is in force. If the seller's policy ends at closing, claims about past work may have no coverage. Ask about "tail" coverage (an extended reporting period) for the seller's policy, and who pays for it.

**Lender requirements.** Under SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026):

- For 7(a) loans over $50,000, SBA requires hazard insurance on all assets pledged as collateral.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-hazard-insurance]
- Flood insurance requirements turn on FEMA's Standard Flood Hazard Determination for the property.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-flood-insurance]
- For a Standard 7(a) loan that is not fully secured, life insurance in the amount of the collateral shortfall is required on the principal of a sole proprietorship, a single-member LLC, or a business that depends on one owner.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-life-insurance-shortfall]
- The lender's credit memo for an acquisition addresses life insurance (on whom and how much, or why none) and business hazard and liability insurance.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-insurance-credit-memo]

Your lender may want to be named on property policies and assigned the life policy, so ask for the exact wording early. Life insurance underwriting can take time.

**Your own protection.** Disability insurance, key person coverage for an employee the business can't lose, and coverage for the personal guarantee are worth pricing. See [insurance for a personal guarantee](/q/pg-insurance).

### Timing

Get quotes during diligence, not the week of closing. Bind coverage to start at the closing time, have certificates ready for the lender and landlord, and make sure vehicles, property and workers' comp are covered from the first minute you own the business. See [day-one readiness](/q/day-one-readiness).

Insurance that covers a breach of the seller's representations is a separate product; see [representations and warranties insurance](/q/rw-insurance).
