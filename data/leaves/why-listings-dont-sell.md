**Short answer.** There's no official count of how many listed businesses sell, so treat any percentage you hear with care. The reasons listings stall are well known, though: an asking price the earnings or a lender won't support, records that don't back up the claimed profit, a business that depends on the owner, or a seller who isn't really ready. A listing that has sat for a while tells you to find out which of these it is; that answer shapes your price and your leverage.

**Why listings stall**

- **Price above what financing supports.** In an SBA 7(a) deal, under SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), all the debt in the deal, including seller debt not on full standby, is capped at the independent business valuation and must be covered by cash flow,[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-total-debt-cap] and any amount by which the price exceeds the valuation must come from equity.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-valuation-gap-equity] A price far above what the earnings justify leaves buyers to fill the gap in cash.
- **Earnings that can't be proven.** Lenders verify the seller's numbers against IRS records.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-seller-tax-verification] If the profit in the listing relies on unreported cash or large add-backs, the deal can fail at underwriting. Marketplaces don't check listings for you; BizBuySell's terms, for example, say it has no obligation to verify what others post.[^bizbuysell-terms-no-verification]
- **No valuation before listing.** The SBA's guide tells owners to get a business valuation before marketing to buyers,[^sba-sell-sales-agreement] but an owner can list at any price they choose.
- **Owner dependence.** If the owner is the business (the sales, the license, the relationships), buyers and lenders worry the profit leaves with them.
- **A declining business.** Falling revenue or margins, a lost customer, or a lease ending soon.
- **A seller who isn't ready.** Some owners list to test the market, then turn down every offer. See [when the owner isn't ready](/q/when-owner-not-ready).
- **Deal problems found later.** A lease that won't transfer, licenses tied to the seller, or environmental issues.

**What a stale listing tells you**

A long time on the market isn't automatically a bargain or a warning. Ask the broker or owner:

1. How long has it been listed, and has the price changed?
2. Have there been offers or signed letters of intent, and why didn't they close?
3. Has a lender looked at it, and what did they say?
4. What does the seller plan to do if it doesn't sell?

Answers that point to price give you room to negotiate. Answers that point to the earnings, the lease or the seller's commitment are diligence questions, and sometimes reasons to walk away.

**Using the leverage well**

A seller who has watched deals fail may value certainty more than the top price. Show you can close (see [proof of funds](/q/proof-of-funds)), explain your price from the numbers, and keep the tone respectful; owners don't sell to buyers who insult their life's work. For handling a price that's too high, see [an unrealistic asking price](/q/asking-price-gap).
