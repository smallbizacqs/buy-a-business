**Short answer.** The problems that come out in diligence can be about the seller rather than the business: debts or liens the seller forgot or didn't mention, co-owners or family members who don't agree with the sale, a spouse with a claim to the shares, tax surprises that change what the seller nets, and cold feet as closing gets real. They can be understandable rather than sinister, and some can be worked through if you find them early and talk about them openly.

### Debts and liens the seller didn't mention

A lien search turns up an old equipment loan, a merchant cash advance, a tax lien or a personal guarantee tied to business assets. Sellers sometimes forget small loans or don't think of them as debts. Each one has to be paid off and released at closing, which reduces what the seller takes home. In an SBA initial acquisition, paying off the business's existing debt is part of the change-of-ownership transaction rather than a separate refinancing,[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-debt-payoff] so the lender will want the full list. See [hidden liabilities](/q/litigation-liabilities).

### Disagreement among owners and family

- **A co-owner** who never agreed to sell, or agrees but wants different terms.
- **A spouse** who has a legal or practical say, especially if the business is marital property or the spouse works in it.
- **Children or relatives** who expected to inherit or take over the business, or who work there and fear for their jobs.
- **Estate and gift tax questions** when the business has been passed down or ownership is shared within a family. Transferring a family business can carry estate and gift tax consequences.[^sba-family-transfer-taxes]

Confirm early who owns what, from the formation documents and ownership records, and who must sign. Ask the seller directly who else cares about the outcome.

### The seller's own numbers

- **The tax bill.** Once the seller's accountant runs the numbers, the after-tax proceeds may be lower than expected, and the seller may push for a different structure or allocation. See [tax structuring](/q/tax-structuring).
- **Payoffs.** Debts, broker fees and transaction costs come out of the price. A seller who hadn't added these up may suddenly feel the deal is too cheap.
- **What comes next.** A seller without a plan for life after the business may hesitate.

### Cold feet

Sellers can have second thoughts at any stage, including late: when employees are about to be told, when the closing date is set, or when they read the non-compete. Signs include slower responses, new conditions, reopening agreed terms, or a sudden "better offer." It can be grief or fear about losing an identity rather than a negotiating tactic, or both.

What can help:

- Keep the seller informed, and keep the timeline moving.
- Ask what's worrying them, and listen to the answer.
- Address the specific fear: the employees' future, the business's name, their role during the transition. See [seller transition](/q/seller-transition-period).
- Avoid surprising them with new demands late in the process. See [retrading](/q/retrade).

### Other things that come out

- The seller's health, a divorce, or a dispute with a key employee or partner.
- Promises made to employees (bonuses, ownership, job guarantees) that the buyer is expected to honor.
- Side arrangements with customers or suppliers that aren't in writing.
- Personal assets mixed in with business assets: a vehicle, a building, a phone number.

How to judge whether any of these should change the deal is covered in [findings that kill or reprice a deal](/q/deal-killers); deliberate misstatement is covered in [fraud and misstatements](/q/fraud-misstatement).
