**Short answer.** The purchase agreement is the binding contract for the sale. In an asset purchase (often called an APA) it says what you're buying, which liabilities you take, the price and how it's paid and adjusted, what the seller promises is true about the business, what happens if those promises turn out to be wrong, and what has to happen at closing. The letter of intent is the outline; this is where each point gets its final wording.

**The main parts:**

- **Purchased and excluded assets**: equipment, inventory, contracts, intellectual property, the name, phone numbers and domains, customer lists, goodwill, and what the seller keeps (usually cash, and sometimes vehicles or personal items).
- **Assumed and excluded liabilities**: what you take on (often only listed contracts and ordinary payables), and everything else, which stays with the seller.
- **Price and payment**: cash at closing, seller note, holdback or escrow, and the working-capital adjustment. See [the peg](/q/peg).
- **Price allocation**: how the price is split among the assets for tax reporting, which both sides use for IRS Form 8594.[^irs-form-8594-residual-method] See [taxes and allocation](/q/tax-structuring).
- **Representations and warranties**: the seller's statements of fact about the business. See [reps and warranties](/q/reps-warranties).
- **Covenants**: promises about conduct, such as running the business normally until closing, and the seller's non-compete and non-solicit after it.
- **Conditions to closing**: what must be true or done before either side has to close. See [closing conditions](/q/closing-conditions).
- **Indemnification**: who pays for losses from breaches and excluded liabilities, with limits. See [indemnification](/q/indemnification).
- **Transition and employees**: the seller's help after closing and how employees move over.
- **Closing deliverables**: bill of sale, assignment of contracts and the lease, the seller note, consulting and non-compete agreements, lien releases.
- **Schedules**: the lists that the agreement points to (assets, contracts, exceptions to the reps). Much of the real negotiation happens here.

**SBA notes.** The lender's file must include the purchase agreement with all its schedules and amendments, so expect the lender to read it.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-purchase-agreement-in-file] Under SBA SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), it can't include a seller earnout.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-earnouts] An SBA 7(a) loan is a bank loan partly guaranteed by the U.S. Small Business Administration (SBA).

**Templates and guides.** Some buyers start from a free template, such as the [online business asset purchase agreement at openagreements.org](https://openagreements.org/templates/openagreements-online-business-asset-purchase-agreement), and read its [asset purchase practice guide](https://openagreements.org/practice-guides/asset-purchase/us). Buyers who hire counsel can look for AI-using law firms with fixed fees that are lean and run by an attorney with big-law experience.
