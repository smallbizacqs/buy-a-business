**Short answer.** Take control of the money on day one (bank access, approvals, cards, payroll), then build a monthly close that produces financial statements you trust within a few weeks of month-end, a weekly cash report, and a short scorecard of the handful of numbers that drive the business. Separate duties so no one person can both move money and record it, and make sure payroll taxes and loan payments are paid on time every time.

### Day one: control of money

- **Bank accounts.** Add yourself as a signer, remove the seller, and set up online access. Turn on alerts for large payments.
- **Approvals.** Decide who can approve payments, up to what amount, and require a second person above a limit.
- **Credit cards and vendor accounts.** Cancel or reissue cards in your name; change passwords on supplier portals, payment processors and software.
- **Payroll.** Approve every payroll yourself at first.

### Separation of duties

In a small business one bookkeeper may do everything. Where you can, split these: the person who enters bills and invoices shouldn't also approve payments or reconcile the bank. Where you can't split them, review the work yourself: read the bank reconciliation, scan the list of payments and new vendors, and look at voided invoices and credit memos.

### The monthly close

A simple close each month:

1. Reconcile every bank and credit card account.
2. Record all invoices and bills for the month.
3. Review receivables (who owes you, how long) and payables.
4. Count or estimate inventory, if you carry it.
5. Produce a profit and loss statement, balance sheet and cash flow, and compare them with the prior month, the same month last year and your budget.

The IRS notes that good records help you monitor the progress of your business, prepare financial statements and support your tax returns.[^irs-recordkeeping-why] Decide early whether your bookkeeper, an outsourced accounting firm or a part-time controller will own the close, and set a firm date each month by which it's done.

### Weekly cash

A one-page weekly report: cash in the bank, what's coming in over the next few weeks (receivables you expect to collect), what's going out (payroll, rent, loan payments, big bills), and the resulting low point. Cash surprises are harder to fix than profit surprises.

### Pick a few key numbers

KPIs (key performance indicators) are the few numbers that tell you early whether the business is on track. Choose five to ten that fit the business, for example:

- Revenue and gross margin, by service line or customer type.
- Sales pipeline, backlog or bookings.
- Days sales outstanding (how long customers take to pay).
- Labor efficiency: billable hours, revenue per technician, jobs per day.
- Customer counts: new, lost, repeat.
- Cash balance and the weekly low point.

Track them on a simple scorecard weekly or monthly, with a target and an owner for each. Start with what you can measure reliably today and add more later.

### Non-negotiables

- **Payroll taxes.** Taxes withheld from employees' pay are held in trust for the government. The IRS can assess the Trust Fund Recovery Penalty personally against anyone responsible for paying withheld taxes who willfully fails to pay them.[^irs-tfrp-who] Use a payroll provider that deposits them automatically, and check that it does.
- **Loan reporting and covenants.** Calendar what your lender requires (financial statements, tax returns, covenant tests) and when.
- **Sales tax, licenses and insurance renewals.**
