**Short answer.** The profit and loss statement (P&L) shows whether the business made money over a period. The balance sheet shows what it owns and owes on one date. The cash flow statement shows where the cash actually went. Read them together, over at least three years, and ask about anything that moves sharply or doesn't connect.

**The P&L (income statement)**

Read it top to bottom:

- **Revenue** (sales).
- **Cost of goods sold** (COGS): direct costs of delivering the product or service, such as materials and field labor.
- **Gross profit** = revenue minus COGS. Gross margin is gross profit as a percentage of revenue; watch whether it holds steady.
- **Operating expenses**: rent, office staff, marketing, insurance, software, owner pay.
- **Depreciation and amortization**: the cost of equipment and intangibles spread over their useful life, not cash spent this year.[^irs-pub-946-depreciation]
- **Interest and taxes**, then **net income**.

Questions to ask: Which customers and services make up revenue? Is owner pay in COGS, operating expenses, or not on the P&L at all? Which costs are fixed and which rise with sales?

**The balance sheet**

A snapshot on one date:

- **Assets**: cash, accounts receivable (money customers owe), inventory, prepaid expenses, equipment and vehicles (less accumulated depreciation).
- **Liabilities**: accounts payable (money owed to suppliers), credit cards, customer deposits, loans, accrued wages and taxes.
- **Equity**: assets minus liabilities.

Look for receivables growing faster than sales (customers paying slowly), inventory piling up, customer deposits (work you'll owe after closing), and debt or loans to the owner. See [receivables, payables and inventory](/q/ar-ap-inventory) and [working capital](/q/what-is-nwc).

**The cash flow statement**

Some small businesses don't produce one. It reconciles net income to the change in cash in three parts: operations, investing (buying equipment) and financing (loans and owner draws). If there isn't one, you can approximate it from two balance sheets and the P&L, or compare net income with the change in the bank balance plus owner draws. Profit that never shows up as cash is a question to ask.

**How the pieces connect**

Net income flows into equity on the balance sheet. Equipment purchases show up on the balance sheet and then, over time, as depreciation on the P&L. Debt payments reduce cash and loan balances but only the interest hits the P&L.

**What a lender asks for**

Under SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), an SBA application to buy a business includes three years of the seller's financial statements and a pro forma balance sheet as of the date of transfer.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-application-business-purchase] The lender analyzes the three most recent year-ends[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-three-years] and compares the latest interim statement with the same period a year earlier.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-interim-statements] It measures coverage on EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization).[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-dsc-definition]
