**Short answer.** A retrade is when the buyer asks to lower the price or change terms after the letter of intent (LOI). It's generally seen as fair when diligence finds something material that the seller's information didn't show, and you can point to it and show the math. Asking for a cut because you feel you can, or because you're near the finish line, costs trust and often the deal.

**When a change is usually justified:**

- Earnings turn out lower than presented once the books, bank statements and tax returns are checked.
- A large customer, contract, license or lease won't carry over.
- Liabilities show up that weren't disclosed.
- The loan won't support the agreed structure.

**SBA rules can force a change.** With an SBA 7(a) loan (a bank loan partly guaranteed by the U.S. Small Business Administration), the numbers that come out of diligence can change what the lender can lend. Under SBA SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), when a quality of earnings (QoE) report (an accountant's independent check of whether the earnings are real and repeatable) is required, the lender must calculate debt coverage from its findings and reduce the loan if coverage doesn't support the deal.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-qoe-dsc] If the price is above the independent business valuation, the difference must be made up with equity, not more debt.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-valuation-gap-equity] Either one can leave a gap that someone has to fill.

**How to raise it:**

1. Share the finding and the evidence, not just a new number.
2. Show how it changes value or what the bank will lend.
3. Offer options: a lower price, more of the price paid later, a holdback, or a specific indemnity (a promise to cover a known risk).
4. Ask once, and be ready to walk away if the gap is too big.

## What practitioners say
- PMI Acquisitions says a buyer should make one offer and not cut the price later unless something major turns up, because re-pricing costs the seller's trust.[^ced94b2]
