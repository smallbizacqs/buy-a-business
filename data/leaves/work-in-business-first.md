**Short answer.** If the seller agrees and you can spare the time, yes: a few days to a few weeks inside the business before closing shows you things no document will, such as how work really flows, which employees hold it together, how customers are treated and what the owner actually does all day. Keep it structured, keep it short, and agree the ground rules in writing, because you aren't the owner yet and the seller still runs the business.

### What it's good for

- **Testing what you've been told.** Watch a normal week. Does the volume match the reports? Do jobs take as long as the seller says?
- **Seeing the owner's real job.** List every decision, call and task the seller handles. That list becomes your [transition plan](/q/seller-transition-period) and shows how much of the business depends on the seller. See [owner dependence](/q/owner-dependence).
- **Meeting the team.** Not as the new owner, but enough to see who the business depends on and how they work. See [key employee risk](/q/key-employee-risk).
- **Checking your fit.** Whether you want to do this work, with these people, every day.
- **Planning day one.** Systems, suppliers, routines and the problems you'll want to fix first. See [day-one readiness](/q/day-one-readiness).

### Ways to do it

- **Shadowing.** You follow the seller for a set number of days, observing and asking questions, without doing the work.
- **Working a role.** You do some of the work yourself, such as on a crew or behind the counter. This teaches more but raises insurance, liability and pay questions.
- **Visits.** Several shorter visits at different times: a busy day, a slow day, month-end.

### Ground rules to agree first

- **Confidentiality.** What employees, customers and suppliers are told about why you're there. If the sale hasn't been announced, agree a cover story or limit what you see. See [telling employees](/q/announce-to-employees).
- **No authority.** You don't direct employees, make commitments to customers or change anything before closing.
- **Insurance and liability.** If you'll do any physical work, confirm the business's insurance covers you, or don't do it.
- **Pay.** Whether you're paid, and if so how; a paid role can create employment questions.
- **What happens if the deal falls through.** Your confidentiality obligations continue, and the seller may want a non-solicit of employees.

Put these in a short letter or in the LOI's access provisions.

### Limits and trade-offs

- **The seller may say no,** especially before the sale is announced or if staff would guess what's happening. Offer a narrower version: after hours, at the seller's side as an "advisor," or with key employees only after the sale is announced.
- **Time costs.** Days spent inside one business are days not spent on the rest of diligence or the loan.
- **It's not a substitute for checking the numbers.** A good week inside the business doesn't verify three years of financials.

### If you're using an SBA loan

The lender must make and document its own site visit to the business,[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-site-visit] and its credit memo describes your industry experience and who will run day-to-day operations.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-management-experience] Time spent in the business can strengthen what you tell the lender about how you'll run it.

After closing, the seller's role in a 7(a) initial acquisition is limited; see [seller transition](/q/seller-transition-period).
