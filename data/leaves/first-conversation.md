**Short answer.** The first call is for finding out whether the owner is open to a conversation at all, not for making an offer. Say who you are and why you're calling about their business in particular, ask about them and the business, and aim to leave with permission for a second conversation.

No rule governs what you say, so treat this as a checklist rather than a script.

**What a first call usually covers**

- **Who you are.** One or two sentences: that you're looking to buy and run one business (or whatever is true), and how you'd pay for it in general terms.
- **Why this business.** A specific reason (its reputation, its trade, its town) lands better than a generic note that could go to anyone.
- **Their story.** How they started, what they're proud of, what a good next chapter looks like for them, their employees and their customers. Owners of long-held businesses often care about these as much as about price.
- **A next step.** A follow-up call or a visit, at a time they choose.

**What usually waits**

- Price, valuation multiples and deal terms. Without numbers, any figure you name is a guess, and a low one can end the conversation.
- Requests for tax returns or financial statements. These normally come after interest is established and, in many cases, after a nondisclosure agreement (NDA), a contract to keep what they share confidential.

**Afterwards**

Write down what you learned (age of the business, the owner's role, any timeline they mentioned, why they might sell) while it's fresh. If they say they're not ready, see [when an owner isn't ready](/q/when-owner-not-ready).

## What practitioners say
- Wolf of Main Street opens a cold call to an owner by asking permission first ("Do you have a minute?") before any pitch.[^c6d7f1b]
