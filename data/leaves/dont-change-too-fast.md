**Short answer.** In the first months, leave alone the things customers and employees feel directly: the people, pay and benefits, prices and terms, the name and brand, the main software and processes, and key vendor relationships. You don't yet know why they are the way they are, and changing several at once makes it hard to tell what caused a problem. Act right away only on things that are urgent, unsafe, illegal or clearly broken, and on taking control of cash.

### What to leave alone at first, and why

- **People and roles.** Every business has informal knowledge held by particular people. Moving or letting go of someone before you know what they actually do can remove knowledge you only discover later. It also tells everyone else their job may be next.
- **Pay, benefits and schedules.** Employees watch these closely after a sale. See [keeping employees](/q/employee-retention).
- **Prices and payment terms.** A change of owner is a moment when customers look at alternatives. Learn your costs, contracts and customers first. See [raising prices](/q/raise-prices).
- **The name, brand, phone numbers and invoices.** Customers trust what they recognize. A sudden change of bank details on invoices can even look like fraud to a customer. See [integration and rebranding](/q/integration).
- **Core systems.** Replacing the software that runs scheduling, accounting or job costing interrupts work while people learn it, and moves data you may need. Learn the current system's workarounds first.
- **Vendors.** Long-standing suppliers may give informal credit, priority or pricing that a cheaper option won't.
- **Policies and perks.** Small things (a flexible Friday, a holiday bonus, an old truck someone drives home) can matter more to people than their cost suggests. Find out what they are before you touch them.

### What you should change quickly

- **Control of money.** Bank access, who can approve payments, credit cards, payroll approval and passwords. See [reporting and financial controls](/q/financial-controls-kpis).
- **Safety, legal and compliance problems.** Licenses, insurance, payroll tax deposits, worker safety. These don't wait.
- **Anything that is clearly losing money every day** and has an obvious fix.
- **Communication.** Introduce yourself, explain what is and isn't changing, and set a regular way to hear from people.

### How to decide on everything else

Keep a running list of changes you want to make. For each, write why things are done the current way (ask the people who do it), what the change would gain, what could go wrong, and who it affects. Then:

- Make changes one or a few at a time, so you can see their effect.
- Tell people before the change, not after, and explain why.
- Start with changes employees have asked for. They build credibility for the harder ones later.

### Signs you're moving too fast

- Employees go quiet in meetings, or the good ones start taking calls from recruiters.
- Customers ask whether the business is being "taken over" or call to check on service.
- You find yourself reversing decisions.

There's no fixed waiting period. Some changes are safe in the first weeks, others are best after a full annual cycle. The test is whether you understand why the current way exists and whether the people affected are ready.
