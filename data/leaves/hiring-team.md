**Short answer.** Start by learning what each current person actually does, then fill the gaps that hold the business back, starting with the jobs the seller did personally. Hire for the role you need over the next few years, define it in writing, use a structured process (the same questions and a work sample for every candidate), and check references. Follow the basic employment rules from the first hire: no discrimination, Form I-9 on time, correct classification and overtime.

### Know the team you have

In the first months, map who does what, including informal work that isn't in anyone's job title. Then ask:

- Which jobs did the seller do personally (sales, estimating, key customer contact, purchasing, bookkeeping), and who does them now?
- Where is the business relying on one person with no backup?
- Who could take on more with training or a raise?
- Where is someone in the wrong role, or not performing?

Developing people you already have can be faster and less risky than hiring, and it signals to the team that there's a future for them.

### Decide what to hire for

Natural first hires after an acquisition replace what the seller did: a general manager or operations lead, a salesperson or estimator, or a bookkeeper or controller. If you plan to step back from daily work, a general manager is the key hire. See [making the business run without you](/q/professionalize-systems).

Write a short role description: the results expected in the first year, the main responsibilities, who the person reports to, and the pay range.

### A structured process

- **Source widely.** Employee referrals, local trade schools, industry groups, job boards and recruiters for senior roles.
- **Same questions for everyone.** Ask each candidate about specific past situations ("tell me about a time you…") so you can compare.
- **A work sample.** Have estimators estimate, technicians troubleshoot, bookkeepers reconcile.
- **Involve the team.** Let a future colleague meet finalists.
- **Check references,** and ask about specific strengths and weaknesses.
- **Make a clear offer** in writing with pay, start date and any probationary period.

### Rules from the first hire

- **Discrimination.** Under the laws the EEOC enforces, it's illegal to discriminate against an applicant or employee because of race, color, religion, sex (including transgender status, sexual orientation and pregnancy), national origin, age (40 or older), disability or genetic information.[^eeoc-prohibited-practices] Ask job-related questions only.
- **Form I-9.** Section 2 must be completed within 3 business days of the employee's first day of work for pay.[^uscis-i9-section-2-timing]
- **Employee or contractor.** The IRS looks at behavioral control, financial control and the relationship to decide whether a worker is an employee or an independent contractor.[^irs-worker-classification] Calling someone a contractor doesn't make them one.
- **Overtime.** Federal law requires overtime for covered employees at not less than one and one-half times their regular rate.[^ecfr-29-cfr-778-107-overtime] Some employees are exempt; your payroll provider or employment lawyer can help classify roles.

State and local rules (minimum wage, paid leave, new-hire reporting, pay transparency) vary by state; check yours.

### Strengthening the team over time

- Hold regular one-on-ones and set clear expectations.
- Pay for skills that matter, and create a path for people who want to grow.
- Address poor performance directly and fairly, and document it.
- If a key person must go, plan the handover of their knowledge and relationships first.
