**Short answer.** Yes. Under SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), every direct and indirect owner of the business, and every guarantor SBA requires, must be a U.S. citizen or U.S. national whose principal residence is in the United States.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-citizenship-owners] Lawful permanent residents (green card holders) no longer qualify.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-lpr-ineligible] This rule has changed twice since 2023, so check the current SOP before you plan around it.

### What the rule covers

- **All owners, not just the majority.** The test applies to 100% of direct and indirect ownership, and entity owners must be formed in the United States.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-citizenship-owners] An investor holding a few percent through an LLC counts the same as you.
- **Residence matters too.** A U.S. citizen whose principal residence is abroad doesn't meet it.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-citizenship-owners]
- **Green card holders are "Ineligible Persons."** The SOP's definition includes lawful permanent residents, whether their status is permanent or conditional.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-lpr-ineligible]

### Recent ownership counts

If an owner is an Ineligible Person, the business is ineligible unless that person completely divests before SBA issues the loan number. The SOP also looks back six months: anyone who was an owner during the six months before the loan number and is an Ineligible Person makes the business ineligible, unless they have fully divested.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-ineligible-divest] Moving shares to a relative on paper while keeping a role or an economic interest is the kind of arrangement to raise with your lender and lawyer before you try it.

### Limited guaranties

There's one narrow opening: when the lender requires a limited or supplemental guaranty, or SBA needs one to support jointly held collateral, an Ineligible Person (other than someone in the U.S. illegally) may give a limited guaranty.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-ineligible-limited-guaranty] That's a guaranty, not ownership. It doesn't let a non-citizen own part of the business.

### How the rule has moved

| SOP version | Effective | Ownership rule |
|---|---|---|
| 7.1 | November 15, 2023[^sba-sop-50-10-versions] | Businesses at least 51% owned and controlled by lawful permanent residents could be eligible[^sba-sop-50-10-7-1-lpr-ownership] |
| 8 | June 1, 2025[^sba-sop-50-10-versions] | 100% of owners had to be U.S. citizens, U.S. nationals or lawful permanent residents[^sba-sop-50-10-8-citizenship] |
| 8.1 | October 1, 2026[^sba-sop-50-10-versions] | 100% of owners must be U.S. citizens or U.S. nationals living in the U.S.; lawful permanent residents are ineligible[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-citizenship-owners][^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-lpr-ineligible] |

Which version applies depends on when your loan gets its SBA number, so ask the lender which SOP they're underwriting under. See [what changed in the SBA rules](/q/sop-rule-changes).

### If you or a partner isn't eligible

- **Buy without SBA.** Conventional bank loans, seller financing and investor equity have no SBA citizenship test; each lender or investor sets its own rules. See [conventional loans](/q/conventional-vs-sba) and [seller financing](/q/seller-financing-basics).
- **Restructure ownership.** Only if the ineligible person truly exits, before the loan number and with the lookback in mind. Get legal advice before changing ownership to qualify, and disclose every owner to the lender.
- **Immigration questions are separate.** Whether you can live and work in the U.S. as a business owner is an immigration matter. See [buying on a visa](/q/foreign-buyer-visa) and talk to an immigration lawyer.
