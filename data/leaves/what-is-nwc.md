**Short answer.** Net working capital (NWC) is the money tied up in running the business day to day: mostly receivables (what customers owe) and inventory, minus payables (what the business owes suppliers) and other short-term bills. In a deal it matters because the price assumes a normal level of it will be left in the business at closing. Without an agreement on that, a seller could collect receivables, stop buying inventory or stretch payables before closing, and you would have to refill the gap with your own cash.

**How deals define it.** The accounting definition is current assets minus current liabilities. Purchase agreements usually narrow it: in a cash-free, debt-free deal, cash and debt are left out and handled separately (see [cash-free, debt-free](/q/cash-free-debt-free)). The agreement should list which accounts count, with a sample calculation attached.

**How it's protected.** The parties agree a target level (the peg). After closing, the actual working capital is compared with the peg and the price is adjusted up or down. See [how the peg is set](/q/peg).

**Lenders look at it too.** For an SBA 7(a) acquisition loan (a bank loan partly guaranteed by the U.S. Small Business Administration), the lender analyzes whether working capital is adequate for at least the next 12 months.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-working-capital-adequacy] Under SBA SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), a working-capital true-up in the purchase agreement is not treated as a rebate to the buyer, so cash paid to the buyer under it can stay in the business rather than going to pay down the loan.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-working-capital-true-up]

**Questions to ask in diligence:** Does working capital swing by season? Are receivables collected on time, or are some old and doubtful? Is any inventory slow-moving or obsolete? Do customers pay deposits in advance? Those deposits are work you'll owe after closing.
