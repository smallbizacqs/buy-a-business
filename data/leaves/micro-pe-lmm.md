**Short answer.** Micro private equity and lower-middle-market funds are pools of investor money managed by a professional team that buys several companies, owns each for a few years, and sells. An individual buyer usually buys one business with personal money, a loan and a personal guarantee, and runs it. The fund spreads risk across companies and hires managers; the individual concentrates risk in one company and is the manager. That shapes what each can pay, how each finances a deal, and what each offers a seller.

### Terms

"Lower middle market" and "micro PE" have no official definitions. People use them loosely for funds that buy smaller companies than traditional private equity, often measured by the target's earnings. Ask any fund what size of company it actually buys.

### How the two compare

| | Micro PE / lower-middle-market fund | Individual buyer |
|---|---|---|
| Money | A fund raised from outside investors, called as deals close | Personal savings, outside investors deal by deal, SBA or bank debt |
| Who runs the company | A hired CEO, or existing management, with the fund on the board | Usually the buyer, as owner-operator |
| Personal guarantee | Set by the lender and the fund's structure | Required on an SBA loan from anyone owning 20% or more[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-personal-guaranty] |
| Hold period | Set by the fund's life, with a planned exit | Up to the owner |
| Deals per year | Several, across a portfolio | One, maybe a second later |
| Seller's role after close | Can keep a stake (rollover) and stay, if both sides want it | With an SBA loan, the seller can't keep a stake in an initial acquisition and may stay only as a consultant for up to 24 months[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-seller-consultant] |

### What each tends to do well

- **Funds** can pay with more equity and less debt, close without SBA rules, offer the seller a second payday through rollover equity, and add capital for growth or add-on acquisitions. They also bring a board, reporting and a set exit timeline that some sellers don't want.
- **Individual buyers** can promise to run the business themselves and keep it, move fast on smaller deals that funds pass on, and use SBA financing with a minimum equity injection of 10%.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-equity-minimum] They have less room to absorb a bad year.

### Where they compete with you, and where they don't

At the small end, a fund looking for its first platform company can bid against individual buyers. Below the size a fund's economics allow, individuals mostly compete with each other. When you do meet a fund across the table, a seller may weigh certainty of close, the role they'll keep, and what happens to employees as heavily as price.

### Funds as a source of money for you

Some funds and their managers invest in individual buyers: backing a search fund, co-investing in an independent sponsor's deal, or lending. Small Business Investment Companies (SBICs) are privately owned, SBA-licensed funds that invest in small businesses through debt and equity.[^sba-sbic-page] An entity such as a fund with more than $5 million in assets, not formed to buy your securities, is an accredited investor.[^ecfr-17-cfr-230-501-entity-assets] See [raising from investors](/q/raise-from-investors).
