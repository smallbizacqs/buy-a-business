**Short answer.** Earnouts (part of the price paid later only if the business hits targets) go wrong when the target is measured differently by each side, when the buyer's decisions after closing affect the result, or when the seller can't see the numbers. Disputes are prevented by defining the metric precisely, stating how the business may and may not be run during the earnout, giving the seller regular reports, and agreeing in advance who settles a disagreement and how.

**First, check whether an earnout is even allowed**

Under SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), seller earnouts are prohibited in an SBA 7(a) acquisition, though buyer rebates tied to business performance are allowed.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-earnouts] A rebate the buyer receives must be used to pay down the 7(a) loan.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-seller-rebate-principal] See [buyer rebates](/q/buyer-rebate) and [the SBA earnout ban](/q/earnout-ban-forgivable-note). The rest of this page applies to deals without SBA financing, and many of the same drafting points apply to a rebate.

**How earnouts go wrong**

- **Vague metrics.** "Revenue" or "profit" without saying which accounting method, which revenue counts (new services? acquired customers?), and which costs are charged against it.
- **Changed accounting.** The buyer switches from cash to accrual, changes how overhead is allocated, or adds management fees, and profit drops on paper.
- **Business decisions.** The buyer raises prices, drops a product line, merges the business into another, or cuts the marketing budget, and the seller says that killed the earnout.
- **Integration.** Once the business is folded into a larger one, its own results are hard to separate.
- **No visibility.** The seller gets an annual number with no backup and assumes the worst.
- **All-or-nothing targets.** Missing by a small amount means paying nothing, which invites a fight over every adjustment.
- **The seller's role.** If the seller stays on and is let go or sidelined, they'll argue they were prevented from hitting the target.

**Terms that prevent disputes**

1. **Define the metric** precisely, with an example calculation attached. Gross profit or revenue is often easier to measure than net profit, which the buyer controls more.
2. **Fix the accounting rules**: the method, consistency with past practice, and a list of items excluded (deal costs, new owner salary changes, interest, management fees).
3. **Operating covenants**: what the buyer will and won't do during the period, balanced so you can still run your business.
4. **Reporting**: monthly or quarterly statements with backup, and a right for the seller to review the books.
5. **A sliding scale** instead of a cliff, so small misses produce small differences.
6. **Dispute resolution**: a short negotiation period, then an independent accountant who decides calculation disputes, with costs shared or shifted to the side that was further off.
7. **Set-off**: whether the buyer may deduct indemnity claims from earnout payments. See [indemnification](/q/indemnification).
8. **What happens on a sale** of the business or a change in the seller's role during the period.

**Keep it simple**

The shorter the period and the simpler the metric, the fewer the arguments. If the two sides can't agree on a clean definition, a lower fixed price or a seller note may serve both better. See [earnouts](/q/earnouts) and [price vs. terms](/q/price-vs-terms). Your lawyer drafts these provisions; your CPA should check the accounting definitions.
