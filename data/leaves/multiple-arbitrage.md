**Short answer.** Sometimes, but it isn't automatic. Multiple arbitrage means buying small companies at a lower multiple of earnings and selling the combined, larger company at a higher one. It works only if the combined company genuinely is what higher-paying buyers want: integrated, professionally managed, less dependent on any one person, with clean financial records and earnings that hold up. A stack of small companies that still operate separately may be valued like a stack of small companies. Plan for the deal to work on cash flow and operating improvements alone, and treat any higher exit multiple as upside.

### The idea

The thesis rests on the premise that small businesses sell at lower multiples of earnings than larger ones because they're riskier: more dependent on the owner, fewer customers, thinner management, less reliable records, and a smaller pool of buyers who can finance them. Whether, and by how much, that holds in your industry is something to check against current deals. The thesis says that if you buy several small companies and combine them, the result is a larger company that a different, higher-paying group of buyers (such as private equity firms or strategic acquirers) will value at a higher multiple. See [what multiple to pay](/q/what-multiple).

### Why it can work

The higher multiple, where it appears, isn't a reward for size alone; it reflects a buyer's view that the company is less risky and easier to own. A roll-up that is genuinely integrated can offer:

- A management team that doesn't depend on any founder.
- Diversified customers and locations.
- Professional financial reporting that holds up to a quality of earnings review.
- Systems that make further growth and add-ons easier.

If the combined company truly has these, it can attract buyers who wouldn't look at any of the pieces alone.

### Why it may not

- **No real integration.** If each acquired company keeps its own systems, brand, management and quirks, a buyer sees several small businesses with a holding company on top.
- **Earnings don't hold up.** Add-backs and synergy estimates that a buyer's diligence rejects reduce the earnings the multiple applies to.
- **Market changes.** Multiples move with interest rates, credit availability and buyer appetite. The multiple at your exit may be lower than you planned, regardless of what you did.
- **Overpaying on the way in.** If many buyers are pursuing the same small companies, entry prices rise and the gap narrows.
- **Debt and costs.** Holding-company overhead, deal costs and interest eat into returns before any exit.
- **Timing.** Integration takes longer than planned, and you may need to sell before it's done.

### How to think about it in your plan

1. Model the deal assuming you sell at the same multiple you paid. Does it still return enough from cash flow, debt paydown and earnings growth?
2. Then list, specifically, what would make a buyer pay more: which management roles, systems, customer mix and reporting. Make those part of the plan, with costs.
3. Be skeptical of any model where most of the return comes from the exit multiple.
4. Ask advisors who sell companies of the size you're aiming for what buyers actually require and how valuations have moved. Get a view based on current deals, not on general rules.

### Schools of thought

Some acquirers build their whole strategy around the multiple step-up from small to larger company; others treat it as unreliable and focus on operating cash flow. Both groups agree the step-up, when it happens, follows real improvements in how the company is run.
