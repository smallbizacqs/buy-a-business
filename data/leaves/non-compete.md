**Short answer.** Ask for a non-compete that covers the business you're actually buying, in the area where it actually competes, for long enough to make the customers and staff yours, plus non-solicitation of customers and employees. Keep it reasonable rather than broad. Enforceability is set by state law and varies widely, and many states treat a non-compete signed in the sale of a business differently from an employee's. Have a lawyer in the relevant state draft or review it.

**Why it matters**

Part of what you pay for in a small business is goodwill: customer relationships, reputation and know-how that sit with the seller. A seller who opens across the street, or calls their old customers, can take it back. The non-compete and non-solicit protect what you paid for.

**The pieces**

- **Non-compete.** The seller won't own, run, work for or help a competing business.
- **Customer non-solicit.** The seller won't approach the business's customers to move their work, whether or not they compete directly.
- **Employee non-solicit (no-hire).** The seller won't recruit the business's employees.
- **Confidentiality.** The seller keeps the business's information private.
- **Non-disparagement.** Optional; neither side runs the other down.

**Setting scope, length and area**

- **Scope:** the activities the business performs today and ones it has concrete plans for, not the whole industry.
- **Area:** where the business's customers actually are, which you can map from customer addresses.
- **Length:** long enough for you to establish your own relationships with customers and staff. Judge it by how long that takes in this business, and what your lawyer says courts in that state accept.
- **Who signs:** every selling owner, and spouses or family members who were active in the business.

**State law decides**

Rules on non-competes differ from state to state, and many states treat a seller's covenant in a sale of a business differently from an employee's. Minnesota is one example: it voids employee non-competes but allows a seller's non-compete in the sale of a business if it's limited to a reasonable area and time.[^mn-stat-181-988-sale-of-business] Other states have their own tests. At the federal level, the FTC says its 2024 Noncompete Rule is not in effect and not enforceable, after a court order in August 2024.[^ftc-noncompete-rule-not-in-effect] Ask your lawyer which state's law applies, what's enforceable there, and whether any recent changes affect your deal.

**Consulting and the non-compete**

If the seller stays on to help, their consulting agreement should have its own restrictions while it lasts, and the non-compete should run from the later of closing or the end of the consulting role. With an SBA loan in an initial acquisition, the seller can stay only as a consultant, for up to 24 months in total.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-seller-consultant] See [seller consulting agreements](/q/seller-consulting-agreement).

**Price allocation and financing**

A non-compete signed as part of buying a business is a section 197 intangible for tax purposes,[^irs-form-8594-section-197] and how much of the price is allocated to it affects both sides' taxes. That allocation is for your CPA; see [purchase price allocation](/q/purchase-price-allocation). Under SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), a 7(a) loan can finance intangible assets in an acquisition, including agreements not to compete.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-intangibles]
