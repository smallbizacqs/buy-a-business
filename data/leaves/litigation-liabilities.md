**Short answer.** Combine three things: public-record searches you can order yourself (court dockets, lien filings, tax liens and judgments), the documents that show liabilities indirectly (bank statements, the general ledger, insurance claims history, tax notices), and direct questions to the seller, answered in writing and backed by representations in the purchase agreement. No single source finds everything, so use all three.

### Public-record searches

These are cheap and fast, and they look at records the seller doesn't control. Run them on the business, on every name it has used, and on the owners.

- **Lien searches (UCC searches).** A lender that takes a security interest in business assets (equipment, inventory, receivables) usually files a UCC financing statement with the secretary of state where the business is organized. A search there shows who claims an interest in the assets. Each lien needs a payoff letter and a release at closing.
- **Court dockets.** Search state courts in the counties where the business operates and federal courts for pending and past lawsuits, judgments and bankruptcies. Some state court systems have online search; others require a request to the county clerk.
- **Tax liens and judgment liens.** Federal and state tax liens and recorded judgments are usually filed with the county recorder or the secretary of state, depending on the state.
- **Regulators and licensing boards.** Complaints, citations and disciplinary actions. See [regulatory compliance](/q/regulatory-compliance).

Services that run these searches across many jurisdictions exist; a lawyer or the lender may order them. SBA lenders discuss any liens, judgments, bankruptcy filings or pending litigation in their credit analysis,[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-credit-memo-liens-litigation] so ask your lender what it has already searched.

### Clues in the seller's own records

- **Bank statements and the general ledger.** Recurring payments to a lender or a merchant cash advance company that isn't on the debt schedule, payments to lawyers, settlement payments.
- **Tax records.** Notices, payment plans and late filings. In an SBA deal the lender verifies the seller's financial data against IRS records.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-seller-tax-verification] Unpaid withheld payroll taxes deserve special attention, because the IRS can pursue them from the business and from people responsible for paying them.[^irs-tfrp-responsible-person] See [tax diligence](/q/tax-dd).
- **Insurance.** Loss runs (the insurer's claims history) show claims, including ones that never became lawsuits. See [insurance](/q/insurance-review).
- **Customer deposits, warranties and refunds.** Work paid for but not done, and warranty obligations on past work, are liabilities that don't always show on a small business's balance sheet.
- **Employment.** Unpaid overtime, misclassified contractors, accrued vacation and promised bonuses. See [employee diligence](/q/employee-dd).

### Questions to ask in writing

- Are there any pending or threatened claims, demand letters or government inquiries?
- Has the business received any notice from a tax agency, regulator or licensing board?
- Are there any loans, advances, guarantees or leases not on the debt schedule?
- Has any customer asked for a refund, credit or warranty repair it hasn't received?

Written answers become the basis for the seller's representations and disclosure schedules, which give you a claim if they turn out to be false. See [reps and warranties](/q/reps-warranties) and [indemnification](/q/indemnification).

### Structure is part of the answer

In an asset purchase, you take the liabilities you agree to and leave most others with the seller; in a stock purchase, the company's past comes with it. See [asset or stock purchase](/q/asset-vs-stock). For deliberately misstated financials, see [fraud and misstatements](/q/fraud-misstatement); for the full legal checklist, see [legal diligence](/q/legal-dd-scope).
