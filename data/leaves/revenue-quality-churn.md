**Short answer.** Get a customer-level sales file (every invoice or transaction for at least the last two or three years, with a customer ID and date) and rebuild the revenue yourself. From it you can see how many customers stay year to year, how much the ones who stay spend, how much revenue comes from new customers each year, and how much is one-time. Revenue that repeats from customers who stay is worth more than the same total made of one-off jobs.

### The file to ask for

Ask for an export from the billing, point-of-sale or accounting system: one row per invoice or transaction, with customer, date, amount and, if possible, product or service line. Summary reports prepared by the seller aren't a substitute, because you can't test them. Tie the export's yearly totals to the tax returns and the bank deposits before you trust it; see [proof of cash](/q/proof-of-cash).

### What to calculate

- **Customer retention.** Of the customers who bought in a year, how many bought again the next year? Churn is the reverse: the share who stopped.
- **Revenue retention.** Of last year's revenue from a group of customers, how much did the same customers spend this year? This can be above 100% when customers who stay spend more, even if some leave.
- **Cohorts.** Group customers by the year (or quarter) they first bought, then track each group's revenue over time. Cohorts show whether newer customers stick as well as older ones, which a single total hides.
- **New versus returning.** How much of each year's revenue came from customers who were new that year? A business that must replace a large share of revenue every year depends heavily on its marketing. See [marketing and lead sources](/q/marketing-dd).
- **One-time items.** Large projects, price increases that customers may not accept again, pandemic-era or weather-driven spikes, and sales to related parties.
- **Trend in the latest months.** Compare the most recent months with the same months a year earlier. SBA lenders do the same with interim statements.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-interim-statements]

### Contracts and recurring revenue

For revenue described as recurring or contracted, read the contracts. Check the term, renewal and cancellation rights, price escalators, and whether the contract can be assigned to you or ends on a change of control. See [whether contracts transfer](/q/contract-assignment). A month-to-month service plan is recurring in habit, not in law.

### When a QoE covers this

A quality of earnings (QoE) report is an accountant's review of whether earnings are real and recurring. Under SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), an SBA-required QoE, which applies to 7(a) initial acquisitions and business expansions with a business purchase price of $3 million or more,[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-qoe-threshold] must assess revenue quality, including customer concentration and contract continuity.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-qoe-revenue-quality] It still helps to do your own pass first: you'll know which questions to ask the accountant, and you'll understand the business better on day one.

### Questions to ask the seller

- Who were the largest customers you lost in the last three years, and why?
- Which customers buy because of you personally? See [owner dependence](/q/owner-dependence).
- Have you raised prices recently, and how did customers react?
- Is any of this year's revenue pulled forward, discounted to close, or from work not yet done?

Customer concentration (too much revenue from a few customers) is its own question; see [customer concentration](/q/customer-concentration). To hear retention from the customers themselves, see [customer calls](/q/customer-dd-calls).
