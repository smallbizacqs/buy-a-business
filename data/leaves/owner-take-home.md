**Short answer.** Start with the business's real cash earnings, then subtract a salary for anyone who replaces the seller's work, equipment spending, cash tied up in working capital, every loan payment (bank and seller), and taxes. What's left, plus any salary you pay yourself, is your take-home. In a leveraged deal the loan payments take a large share of cash flow in the early years, so build the number yourself rather than reading it off the listing.

### Walk the cash down

1. **Start with earnings the lender will accept.** Listings commonly quote seller's discretionary earnings (SDE: profit with the owner's pay and personal expenses added back). SBA lenders measure coverage on EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization). Know which you're looking at. See [SDE vs EBITDA](/q/sde-vs-ebitda) and [add-backs](/q/add-backs).
2. **Subtract replacement pay.** If you won't do everything the seller did, pay someone who will. See [owner replacement cost](/q/owner-replacement-cost).
3. **Subtract capital spending.** Equipment, vehicles and software that wear out. Depreciation is added back in EBITDA, but the cash still goes out. See [capex and equipment](/q/capex-equipment).
4. **Subtract growth in working capital.** A growing business ties up more cash in receivables and inventory. See [working capital](/q/what-is-nwc).
5. **Subtract all debt service.** The SBA loan plus any seller note or other debt.
6. **Subtract taxes.** On the business's profit, however it reaches you.

### Why debt payments are large

Under SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), an SBA 7(a) loan for a change of ownership amortizes over no more than 10 years and can't have a balloon,[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-change-of-ownership-term] so you repay principal quickly. The lender defines historical debt coverage as EBITDA divided by all debt service after the deal,[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-dsc-definition] and needs at least 1.25 to 1 for an initial acquisition.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-dsc-ratio]

**An illustration, not a forecast.** If the lender counts $500,000 of cash flow and the deal is sized at exactly 1.25x, annual debt service is $400,000, leaving $100,000 before capital spending, working capital, taxes and, depending on how the lender built its number, your own pay. Ask the lender whether its cash flow figure already deducts a salary for you. See [debt service coverage](/q/dscr).

### Your salary and the lender

The lender checks that your compensation from the business covers your obligations and living expenses.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-owner-compensation] Bring your household budget to the first lender conversation.

### How you're paid and taxed

It depends on the entity. A corporate officer is generally an employee and should be paid wages in line with the work;[^irs-paying-yourself-officer] partners take distributions or guaranteed payments instead of W-2 wages.[^irs-paying-yourself-partners] Ask your CPA to show how loan principal payments, as opposed to interest, affect your tax bill; the answer changes how much cash you keep. See [entity setup](/q/entity-setup).

### Common mistakes

- Treating SDE as your salary while also paying the loan from it.
- Forgetting the seller note's payments, or assuming they'll be deferred.
- Leaving out capital spending in an equipment-heavy business.
- Planning on your current salary from month one. See [the pay dip after closing](/q/j-curve) and [stress testing](/q/stress-test).
