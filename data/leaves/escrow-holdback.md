**Short answer.** Often yes. Setting aside part of the price for a period after closing gives you a ready source of money if the seller's representations prove false or the working-capital true-up goes your way. In an escrow, a neutral third party holds the money; in a holdback, you keep it and pay it later. The amount and length are negotiated around the risks diligence found.

**Escrow vs holdback:**

- **Escrow**: an escrow agent holds the funds under a written agreement and releases them on joint instructions or a final decision. It's neutral, but costs fees and paperwork.
- **Holdback**: you keep part of the price and pay it on a schedule, minus valid claims. It's simpler, but the seller carries the risk that you won't pay.
- **Set-off against a seller note**: a similar effect when part of the price is a seller note: claims reduce what you owe.

**Terms to agree:**

- What it secures: indemnity claims, the working-capital true-up, or a specific risk.
- Amount and release dates (all at once, or in stages).
- How claims are made and disputed, and what happens to money tied up in a pending claim.
- Who pays the escrow agent, and who gets any interest.

**Not the same as the lender's closing escrow.** An SBA lender may hold loan funds in escrow for no more than 5 business days to facilitate closing (SBA SOP 50 10 8.1, effective October 1, 2026).[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-closing-escrow] That's a short-term closing mechanic, not protection for you after closing. An SBA 7(a) loan is a bank loan partly guaranteed by the U.S. Small Business Administration (SBA).

If you're using an SBA loan, tell your lender about any escrow or holdback early, since it affects the sources and uses of funds. See [indemnification](/q/indemnification) and [cash at closing versus later](/q/cash-at-close).
