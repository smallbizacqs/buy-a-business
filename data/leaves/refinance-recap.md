**Short answer.** Sometimes. Once the business has a track record under you and has paid down debt, a lender may refinance to lower your payments, pay off a seller note, or, less often, let you take money out (a recapitalization, or "recap"). Whether it's possible depends on the business's cash flow, what lenders will lend against it, and any limits in your current loans.

**Common reasons to refinance:**

- Replace expensive or short-term debt with cheaper or longer debt.
- Pay off the seller note early, sometimes at a discount the seller agrees to.
- Fund growth or an add-on acquisition.
- Return some of your equity, or buy out a partner.

**SBA rule on seller notes.** Under SBA SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), a seller note from an acquisition can be refinanced with SBA financing only after it has been in place and current (not on standby) for at least 36 months, and the refinancing must improve the installment payment by at least 10 percent without reducing the lender's exposure.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-seller-note-refi-terms] Your lender confirms whether a refinancing qualifies. An SBA 7(a) loan is a bank loan partly guaranteed by the U.S. Small Business Administration (SBA).

**What to check first:**

- Prepayment penalties or fees in your current loans.
- Covenants or standby agreements that limit new debt or payments to the seller.
- Your personal guarantee: a new loan may mean a new guarantee.
- Whether taking cash out leaves the business enough working capital and cushion.

A recap adds risk: more debt on the same business. Model it with your CPA. See [exit options](/q/exit-options) and [add-on acquisitions](/q/add-on-acquisitions).
