**Short answer.** Under SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), seller earnouts (extra price paid later if the business hits targets) are prohibited in a 7(a) acquisition, but buyer rebates tied to business performance are allowed.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-earnouts] A rebate runs the other way: the seller pays money back if the business underperforms, and the buyer must use it to pay down the 7(a) loan.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-seller-rebate-principal]

**Tools that can do some of an earnout's work**

- **A buyer rebate.** Allowed; any rebate proceeds must go to reducing the principal of the 7(a) loan that funded the purchase.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-seller-rebate-principal]
- **A seller note with performance terms.** Some buyers negotiate a seller note whose balance can be reduced (sometimes called a forgivable note) if results fall short. How such a note fits SBA rules on seller debt and standby is a question for the lender before you put it in a letter of intent. A note counts as equity only on full standby.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-seller-debt-equity]
- **A consulting agreement.** In a full purchase the seller may consult for up to 24 months in total,[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-seller-consultant] paid for real work.
- **A lower price with less risk to bridge.** Sometimes the simplest answer.

**Not a rebate**

A working-capital true-up in the purchase agreement isn't treated as a rebate, and cash paid to the buyer under it can stay in the business.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-working-capital-true-up]

Each of these changes the tax and legal picture for both sides; your lawyer, CPA and lender decide how they're written.
