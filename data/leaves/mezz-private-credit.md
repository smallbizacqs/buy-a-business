**Short answer.** Mezzanine debt is a loan that ranks behind the senior bank loan, costs more, and often comes with a piece of the equity; private credit is lending by non-bank funds, senior or junior. They make sense when a deal is too big or too leveraged for a bank alone, the business has steady cash flow to cover a higher rate, and the alternative is giving up more ownership to equity investors. SBA says a typical loan from a Small Business Investment Company (SBIC), one source of this kind of money, runs $250,000 to $10 million at 9% to 16% interest.[^sba-sbic-page] Compare that with the senior rate your bank quotes.

### What these lenders are

- **Mezzanine lenders** provide subordinated debt (repaid after the senior lender) and often take warrants or a small equity stake to boost their return. Payments can be cash interest, interest added to the balance ("payment in kind"), or both.
- **Private credit funds** lend outside the banking system. Some provide a single "unitranche" loan in place of separate senior and junior loans; others sit behind a bank.
- **SBICs** are privately owned companies licensed and regulated by SBA that invest in small businesses through debt and equity.[^sba-sbic-page]

### When it fits

- The business is too large or the deal needs more debt than a bank or SBA loan alone will provide. The largest Standard 7(a) loan is $5 million.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-max-loan]
- Cash flow is steady enough to cover the higher interest with room to spare.
- You or your investors would rather pay interest than give up the equity a larger raise would take.
- There's a clear way to repay or refinance it, such as cash flow paydown or a later refinancing.

### When it doesn't

- Thin or volatile cash flow. Expensive debt magnifies a bad year.
- Small deals, where fees and legal costs eat up the benefit.
- When the senior lender won't allow it, or the intercreditor terms leave the junior lender able to disrupt the business.

### How it interacts with an SBA loan

Under SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026):

- **No junior SBA position.** A 7(a) loan can't be in a "piggyback" structure, where another lender lends at about the same time for the same purpose and the SBA loan is junior or unsecured behind it.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-piggyback] A shared (pari passu) lien is allowed if the other loan's maturity isn't shorter.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-pari-passu]
- **All debt is capped and tested.** Total acquisition debt, including other debt not on full standby, is limited to the business valuation and must be covered by cash flow.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-total-debt-cap] Interest-only debt is tested as if it amortized over no more than 10 years.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-interest-only-debt]
- **Standby debt.** Debt on full standby may accrue interest payable after the 7(a) loan is repaid, and the creditor must subordinate and take no action without the lender's consent.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-standby-terms] A standby creditor can't also take equity in the business,[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-standby-no-equity] which rules out pairing standby debt with a warrant.
- **The lender reviews it all.** The SBA lender must underwrite and review the terms of all other debt and equity in the deal.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-external-capital-review]

Taken together, these rules leave little room for a classic mezzanine loan inside an SBA deal.

### Terms to negotiate

- Cash interest versus payment-in-kind interest, and the all-in rate.
- Warrants or equity: how much, and at what price.
- Prepayment penalties and call protection.
- Covenants, and how they interact with the senior lender's.
- The intercreditor agreement: when the junior lender can be paid, and what it can do on a default.
