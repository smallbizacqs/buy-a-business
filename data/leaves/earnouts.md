**Short answer.** An earnout is part of the price that the seller gets later only if the business hits agreed targets after closing. It can bridge a gap when buyer and seller disagree about where the business is heading. But it only works when the target is simple to measure and hard to argue about, and it isn't available at all if you finance the deal with an SBA 7(a) loan (a bank loan partly guaranteed by the U.S. Small Business Administration).

**SBA rule.** Under SBA SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), seller earnouts are prohibited in an SBA-financed change of ownership. Buyer rebates tied to business performance are allowed, because they benefit the borrower.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-earnouts] A rebate runs the other way: the seller pays some of the price back if results fall short. Any rebate the buyer receives must go to pay down the SBA loan.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-seller-rebate-principal] Your lender decides how this applies to your deal.

**When an earnout can make sense (outside SBA financing):**

- Revenue depends on something uncertain, such as a contract renewal or a new product line.
- The seller believes in growth you won't pay for up front.
- The seller is staying involved and can affect the result.

**How to structure one:**

- **Metric.** Revenue is easier to measure than profit, which depends on costs you now control. Gross profit sits in between.
- **Period and payment dates**, and whether payments are all-or-nothing or scale with results.
- **Accounting rules**: how the metric is calculated, written into the agreement with an example.
- **Operating covenants**: what you promise, or don't promise, about running the business during the earnout period.
- **Cap**: the most the seller can earn.
- **Information and disputes**: what reports the seller gets, and who decides a disagreement (often an independent accountant).

See [how earnouts go wrong](/q/earnout-disputes) and [cash at closing versus later](/q/cash-at-close).
