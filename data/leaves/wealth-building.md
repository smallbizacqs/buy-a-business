**Short answer.** Ownership builds wealth in three main ways over years: the business's cash flow pays down the acquisition debt, so your equity in it grows; earnings grow, which raises what the business is worth; and a sale, recapitalization or years of distributions eventually turn that value into cash. None of it is guaranteed. The same leverage that speeds up equity growth magnifies losses, and much of your net worth ends up in one illiquid asset.

### Engine 1: paying down the debt

When you buy with a loan, the business's cash flow repays it. Each principal payment shifts value from the lender to you. Under SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), an SBA 7(a) acquisition loan amortizes over no more than 10 years and can't have a balloon,[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-change-of-ownership-term] so the debt is paid off on a fixed schedule if the business performs. See [take-home pay](/q/owner-take-home) for what that does to cash in the early years.

### Engine 2: growing earnings

Higher earnings make the business worth more to the next buyer, as long as the growth is durable. Levers include pricing, new customers, better margins and add-on acquisitions. See [value creation](/q/value-creation) and [add-on acquisitions](/q/add-on-acquisitions).

### Engine 3: making it easier to sell

Buyers pay for lower risk. A business that runs without its owner, keeps steady recurring customers and has clean books is easier to finance and sell than one that depends on one person. Work that reduces those risks can raise value even without higher earnings. See [professionalizing systems](/q/professionalize-systems), [owner dependence](/q/owner-dependence) and [buying small and selling bigger](/q/multiple-arbitrage).

### Cash along the way

Once debt is under control, profit beyond what the business needs can be paid to you. Over a long hold, those distributions can matter as much as the sale.

### Turning value into cash

Value on paper becomes wealth when you sell, recapitalize or take distributions over time. See [exit options](/q/exit-options), [refinance or recap](/q/refinance-recap) and [taxes on exit](/q/exit-taxes). A sale can be harder than expected; see [when you can't exit](/q/cant-exit).

### The risks that cut the other way

- **Leverage.** If earnings fall, debt payments don't. Every individual owning 20% or more of an SBA borrower gives an unlimited personal guarantee.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-personal-guaranty] See [personal guarantees](/q/personal-guarantee).
- **Concentration.** Your savings, income and much of your net worth sit in one business.
- **Illiquidity.** You can't sell a slice of a small business the way you sell shares.
- **Your time.** Wealth built this way can take years of hard work and, early on, pay below what you could earn elsewhere. See [the pay dip after closing](/q/j-curve).

### Questions to ask yourself

- What would the business need to earn, and for how long, to pay off the debt?
- What would make it more valuable to a buyer than it is today?
- What's my plan if I need cash before I can sell?
