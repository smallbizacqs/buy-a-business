**Short answer.** Owning a US business and being allowed to live and work in the US are separate questions. Buying a business can support an E-2 treaty investor visa if you're a national of a qualifying treaty country and meet USCIS's investment requirements, but ownership alone gives you no immigration status. SBA loans are limited to owners who are US citizens or nationals living in the US, so non-citizen buyers generally finance the purchase another way. Talk to an immigration attorney before you sign a letter of intent or move money.

Immigration rules and policies change. Check the current USCIS page and get legal advice for your situation.

### The E-2 treaty investor visa

USCIS says an E-2 treaty investor must:[^uscis-e2-qualifications]

- be a national of a country with which the US maintains a treaty of commerce and navigation;
- have invested, or be actively investing, a substantial amount of capital in a real US enterprise; and
- be coming to the US solely to develop and direct that enterprise.

The treaty countries are listed by the State Department, and the initial stay is at most two years.[^uscis-e2-treaty-countries]

**What "substantial" and "investment" mean.** Whether the capital is substantial is judged against the total cost of buying an established business or starting a new one.[^uscis-e2-substantial-capital] The investment has to be capital placed at commercial risk with the aim of making a profit.[^uscis-e2-at-risk]

**The business can't be marginal.** It must be able to earn more than a minimal living for you and your family.[^uscis-e2-marginal] A very small business that only replaces one person's wages may not qualify.

### How this shapes an acquisition

- **Timing and risk.** The money generally needs to be committed to the deal, but you don't want to lose it if the visa is refused. Ask your attorney how to structure the purchase agreement, escrow and closing conditions around the visa decision.
- **Your role.** E-2 is built around developing and directing the business. Plan the deal so your role matches.
- **Diligence still applies.** A visa timeline is not a reason to skip checking the business. Sellers who know a buyer is under time pressure may push on price; see [negotiation tactics](/q/tactics).
- **Other routes.** Other visa and green card categories exist, including investor-based ones with different requirements. An immigration attorney can tell you which fits.

### Financing

Under SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), SBA financing requires every direct and indirect owner and required guarantor to be a US citizen or US national with a principal residence in the US.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-citizenship-owners] See [the SBA citizenship rule](/q/citizenship-rule). Options to explore include your own funds, seller financing, conventional bank loans and investors. See [seller financing](/q/seller-financing-basics) and [conventional or SBA loans](/q/conventional-vs-sba).

### Tax and structure

Where you're tax resident, how the business is owned and how profits reach you all affect taxes in the US and at home. Bring in a CPA with cross-border experience before choosing the entity. See [entity setup](/q/entity-setup).

### Questions for your immigration attorney

- Am I a national of a treaty country, and does my situation fit E-2 or another category?
- How much investment would be considered substantial for this business?
- How should the purchase be structured so the funds are at risk without losing them if the visa is refused?
- Can I work in the business before the visa is approved?
