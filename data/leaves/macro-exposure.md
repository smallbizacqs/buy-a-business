**Short answer.** Trace how money flows in and out of the business and ask where the economy touches it: do customers buy with borrowed money or discretionary spending, do inputs come from abroad, does the business carry variable-rate debt, and did revenue fall in the last downturn? Then decide which shocks matter for this business and test the deal against them.

**Four channels of exposure**

**1. Interest rates**

- **Your own debt.** 7(a) loans can carry a variable rate set as a base rate plus a fixed spread,[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-variable-rate] so your payments can rise after closing. Test a higher rate.
- **Customers' borrowing.** Businesses that sell big-ticket items customers finance (home improvement, pools, vehicles, equipment, new construction) can slow when borrowing costs rise.
- **Real estate.** Businesses tied to home sales or commercial construction move with those markets.

**2. Tariffs and trade**

- List the main products and materials the business buys and where they're made, including what your suppliers import.
- Ask suppliers how recent tariff changes have affected their prices and lead times.
- Check whether the business has been able to pass price increases on to customers, and how quickly.
- If the business exports, ask about tariffs or restrictions in its customers' countries.

**3. The economic cycle**

- **Needs vs. wants.** Repairs, compliance work and essential services are harder for customers to postpone than discretionary purchases, upgrades and luxuries.
- **Who the customers are.** Consumer, business or government, and how each behaves in a slowdown.
- **Contract length.** Long contracts and recurring service plans soften a downturn; one-off projects don't.
- **Operating leverage.** A business with high fixed costs (rent, salaried staff, equipment) loses profit faster when revenue falls than one whose costs move with sales.

**4. Input costs and labor**

Fuel, materials, insurance and wages can rise faster than prices. Look at how gross margin moved when costs rose in the past. See [labor availability](/q/labor-availability).

**Use the business's own history**

Look as far back as records go. Under SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), an SBA lender analyzes the three most recent year-ends[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-three-years] and compares the latest interim period with the same period last year,[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-interim-statements] but you can ask for more. If the business was operating during the 2008-2009 recession or the 2020 shutdowns, ask what happened to revenue and margins then, and what the owner did. Reports from the owner are useful, but tie them to tax returns where possible.

**Turn it into a decision**

- Pick the two or three shocks that matter most for this business.
- Run each through your model. See [stress-testing a deal](/q/stress-test).
- Adjust price, debt level and cash reserves so the deal survives a realistic bad year.
- Don't treat a good recent year in a cyclical business as the new normal. See [TTM and trends](/q/trends-ttm).
