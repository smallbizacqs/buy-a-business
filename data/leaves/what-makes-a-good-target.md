**Short answer.** A business worth buying makes steady, provable profits that comfortably cover the debt and pay you; keeps its customers, employees and know-how when the owner leaves; and sells something people will keep needing. It also has to fit you: your money, your skills and the life you want. The rest is price.

**What you're buying**

The SBA's guide describes the main advantage of buying over starting as an existing blueprint: an established customer base, known operating expenses and trained employees.[^sba-buy-business-blueprint] A good target is one where that blueprint keeps working after the seller walks away.

**Qualities to look for**

- **Earnings you can prove.** Profits that show up in tax returns and bank deposits, not just in the seller's adjusted numbers. In an SBA deal the lender checks the seller's figures against IRS records[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-seller-tax-verification] and analyzes three years of results.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-three-years] See [proof of cash](/q/proof-of-cash) and [add-backs](/q/add-backs).
- **Enough cash flow to carry the deal.** Historical cash flow that covers all loan payments with room to spare. For an SBA initial acquisition, under SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), the minimum is 1.25:1.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-dsc-ratio] Thin coverage leaves no room for a bad year. See [DSCR](/q/dscr).
- **Stable or growing results.** A steady trend over several years. A sharp recent jump or drop needs an explanation.
- **Durable demand.** Customers need the service whether or not the economy is good, and it's hard to replace with software or do yourself. See [resilient businesses](/q/resilient-businesses).
- **Spread-out customers.** No single customer whose loss would sink the business. See [customer concentration](/q/customer-concentration).
- **Not dependent on the owner.** Employees who do the work, written processes, and customer relationships held by more than one person. See [owner dependence](/q/owner-dependence).
- **Transferable foundations.** A lease you can take over, licenses you can get, contracts that survive a change of owner. The SBA's guide tells buyers to ask about contracts, leases, existing cash flow and inventory.[^sba-buy-business-ask-questions] See [lease assignment](/q/lease-assignment) and [licenses and permits](/q/licenses-permits-transfer).
- **Reasonable capital needs.** Equipment, vehicles and facilities that won't need a large outlay right after closing. See [capex and equipment](/q/capex-equipment).
- **A clear reason for sale.** Retirement, health or a move is easier to understand than a vague answer. See [why the owner is really selling](/q/why-owner-selling).

**Problems that can be fine**

Some weaknesses are the opportunity, if you can fix them and the price reflects them: outdated marketing, prices below market, no online booking, paper records, an owner who never hired a manager. Distinguish those from structural problems you can't fix: a shrinking market, a key customer leaving, a license only the seller can hold. See [hidden upside](/q/hidden-upside).

**Fit with you**

The same business can be right for one buyer and wrong for another. Check it against your [buy box](/q/define-buy-box): size, industry, location, the hours and role it demands, and whether your skills cover what the seller does today.

**A quick screen**

Before signing a letter of intent (LOI, the non-binding offer that starts exclusive diligence), you should be able to say in a sentence why customers buy, why they stay, who does the work, what the business really earns, and what you'd do in your first year. If you can't, keep asking. See [early red flags](/q/early-red-flags) and [deal killers](/q/deal-killers).
