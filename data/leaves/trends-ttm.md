**Short answer.** Look at the trailing twelve months (TTM) and at least three full years side by side, then ask why each change happened. A steady or growing business can be priced on its recent results. A declining one should be priced on where it's heading, not on its best year, and a sudden spike should be treated as unproven until you know it will repeat. An SBA lender tests coverage on full fiscal years, so its view and yours may differ.

**What each period tells you**

- **TTM** is the most recent full year of results, ending at the latest month. It shows where the business is now.
- **Three prior fiscal years** show direction and whether TTM is normal or unusual.
- **Year-to-date vs. the same period last year** shows whether the trend is continuing right now.

**What the SBA lender looks at**

Under SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026):

- The lender's analysis is based on the three most recent year-ends of the business being bought.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-three-years]
- Debt service coverage must be met on the last fiscal year-end or the average of the last two.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-dsc-ratio]
- The lender also compares the latest interim statement with the same period a year earlier.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-interim-statements]
- Projections are reviewed but can't be relied on to meet coverage (with a narrow special purpose property exception).[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-projections]
- A required quality of earnings report runs its cash proof on the trailing 12 months and the last two fiscal years,[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-qoe-cash-proof] and assesses whether revenue and margins are likely to hold after the sale.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-qoe-revenue-quality]

So a business whose TTM is strong but whose last full year is weak may not support the loan you expect. Ask your lender which period it will use before you set a price.

**Declining results**

1. Find the cause: a lost customer, a price change, the owner slowing down before a sale, a market shift.
2. Decide whether you can reverse it, and what that costs.
3. Price on the current run rate or lower, not on an average that includes better years.
4. Check that the newest months don't show a sharper drop than TTM.

An owner who is easing toward retirement can cause a decline that a new owner can fix. A shrinking market is harder. See [buying a struggling business](/q/distressed-turnaround).

**Spikes**

A big jump can be real growth or a one-off: a single large job, a price increase competitors haven't matched, a temporary shortage. Ask what drove it, whether it's in the backlog or contracts, and whether margins rose with revenue. Treat unexplained jumps as one-time until proven otherwise. See [unusual years like COVID and PPP](/q/covid-ppp-normalization).

**Seasonality**

For seasonal businesses, compare months to the same months in prior years, never one month to the next, and make sure TTM doesn't start or end in a way that flatters the numbers.
