**Short answer.** It depends on how the sale is structured. In an asset sale, the price is allocated across the business's assets and each is taxed separately: some gain is capital gain, some (such as inventory and depreciation recapture) is ordinary income. In a sale of stock or membership interests, the gain is generally capital gain, long-term if you held it more than a year. Being paid over time can spread the tax, and state taxes and a federal investment income tax may also apply. Your CPA should model the tax before you agree on structure and price.

This page describes how the rules work in general. It isn't tax advice; the tax result of a sale depends on your entity, your history and the deal.

### Asset sale or stock sale

**Asset sale.** The buyer buys the business's assets. The IRS treats each asset as sold separately for figuring gain or loss, and the assets fall into categories (capital assets, depreciable and real property used in the business, inventory) with different treatment; the sale of inventory produces ordinary income or loss.[^irs-pub-544-sale-of-business] The price is allocated across the assets using the residual method, which sets the seller's gain or loss on each asset,[^irs-form-8594-residual-method] with goodwill and going concern value in the last class.[^irs-form-8594-class-vii] Buyer and seller each report the sale on Form 8594.[^irs-form-8594-who-files] Because the allocation changes the tax for both sides, it's a real negotiating point.

**Stock or membership interest sale.** The owner sells the ownership of the company itself. A capital gain is generally long-term if the asset was held more than one year, and short-term if held one year or less.[^irs-topic-409-holding-period]

Your entity type (C corporation, S corporation, partnership or LLC, or sole proprietorship) changes how each of these is taxed; ask your CPA how it applies to you. See [tax structuring](/q/tax-structuring) for the buyer's side.

### Getting paid over time

In an installment sale, where at least one payment comes after the tax year of the sale, you may be able to report part of the gain as each payment arrives.[^irs-pub-537-installment-sale] Limits apply: depreciation recapture is reported in the year of sale even if no payment arrives that year,[^irs-pub-537-recapture] and the regular sale of inventory doesn't qualify for installment reporting.[^irs-pub-537-inventory] Seller notes and some earnouts can raise these questions. See [earnouts](/q/earnouts).

### Other taxes that can apply

- **Net investment income tax.** A 3.8 percent tax applies to net investment income above threshold amounts, and reaches business income when the business is a passive activity for you.[^irs-topic-559-niit] Whether it applies to your sale depends on how involved you were in the business and how the sale is structured.
- **State income tax.** Varies by state, and can depend on where you live and where the business operates.
- **Family transfers.** Transferring a family business can carry estate and gift tax obligations.[^sba-family-transfer-taxes]

### What to do

1. Bring in a CPA with transaction experience before you sign an LOI (letter of intent), since structure is set there.
2. Ask them to compare the after-tax proceeds of the offers you're considering, including the allocation, any rollover equity, seller note or earnout.
3. Plan for estimated tax payments in the year of sale.
4. Keep records of your basis (what you paid and invested) in the company and its assets.

Tax rules change. Confirm the current rules with your CPA.
