**Short answer.** In a traditional search fund the searcher puts in little or no money and earns a share of the company's equity as a reward for finding, buying and running it. That share is typically structured in pieces tied to different triggers: closing the deal, staying on as CEO, and investors' returns. The exact percentages are negotiated in each fund's documents, so read yours rather than relying on a "standard."

### Why searchers get equity at all

A search fund is an investment vehicle in which investors back an entrepreneur to find, buy, run and grow a private company.[^stanford-gsb-search-fund-definition] Investors supply the money for the search and the purchase; the searcher supplies the work. The searcher's equity, often called carry or promote, is how that work gets paid beyond a salary. Stanford GSB publishes a periodic study of search funds' financial returns and key characteristics.[^stanford-gsb-2026-search-fund-study]

### The three pieces

Fund documents can split the searcher's equity into tranches (portions earned on different triggers):

1. **On acquisition.** A portion earned when the company is bought. This rewards the search itself.
2. **Over time.** A portion that vests on a schedule while the searcher stays employed, typically as CEO. The documents say what happens to unvested equity if the searcher leaves early.
3. **On performance.** A portion earned only if investors reach set return levels, for example an internal rate of return (IRR) on their money, when the company is sold or recapitalized.

The way these pieces add up, and what the thresholds are, varies by fund. Ask investors for their term sheet early and compare it with what other searchers in your network signed.

### Terms to read closely

- **Vesting and acceleration.** What happens to unvested equity if the company is sold early, if you're fired without cause, or if you leave.
- **Cause and good reason.** How "cause" is defined can decide whether you keep vested equity.
- **Repurchase rights.** Whether the company can buy back your vested shares when you leave, and at what price.
- **The performance hurdle.** How the IRR or multiple is calculated, whether it includes the search capital, and when it's measured.
- **Dilution.** How later equity raises, option pools for managers, or add-on acquisitions change your share.
- **Step-ups on search capital.** Some funds give search investors a premium on the money they put in during the search when it rolls into the acquisition. If yours does, find out where that premium sits ahead of your equity.
- **Taxes.** Equity earned for services raises tax questions about timing and elections. Talk to a tax advisor before you sign.

### How this compares with other paths

A self-funded buyer who uses an SBA loan owns most or all of the equity but puts in personal money and personal guarantees. See [self-funded search](/q/self-funded-search). ### Securities note

As a director or executive officer of the company raising money, you count as an accredited investor in that offering.[^ecfr-17-cfr-230-501-insiders] For how the raise itself is done, see [Rule 506(b) or 506(c)](/q/506b-506c-securities).
