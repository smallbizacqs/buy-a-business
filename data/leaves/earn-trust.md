**Short answer.** Retiring owners tend to trust buyers who show up repeatedly, listen more than they pitch, are straight about how the deal will be financed and what happens afterwards, and keep their word once terms are agreed. Trust is built over many conversations and can be lost with one surprise.

**What owners often worry about**

- **Their people.** Whether employees keep their jobs and are treated well.
- **Their customers and name.** Whether the business they built keeps its reputation.
- **Getting paid.** Whether the buyer can actually close, and on what terms.
- **What happens to them.** Whether they'll be pushed out on day one or asked to stay longer than they want.

**Things that build trust**

- **Clarity about money.** Explain early how you'd finance the purchase (your own funds, a bank loan, a seller note, investors). If you'll use an SBA 7(a) loan, a bank loan partly guaranteed by the U.S. Small Business Administration, say what it means for them. Under SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), in a full purchase the seller can't stay on as an owner or employee, but the business may hire the seller as a consultant for up to 24 months in total.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-seller-consultant]
- **Consistency.** Do what you said you'd do, when you said you'd do it.
- **Respect for confidentiality.** Owners worry about employees, customers and competitors finding out. Keep what they share to yourself.
- **No surprises later.** A change to agreed terms after the letter of intent (LOI) needs a reason the owner can see for themselves; see [when it's fair to change the price](/q/retrade).

## What practitioners say
- The Acquisitions Academy Podcast describes staying in touch with owners who aren't ready to sell yet, treating sourcing as many long conversations.[^ca59f3a]
- PMI Acquisitions says a buyer should make one offer and not cut the price later unless something major turns up, because re-pricing costs the seller's trust.[^ced94b2]
