**Short answer.** Use three layers: own the business through an entity such as an LLC or corporation so its debts and lawsuits generally stay with the company; carry insurance for the risks the entity doesn't cover; and keep business and personal money strictly separate. How personal assets are titled and what your state protects (homes, retirement accounts, property held with a spouse) is state law, so plan it with a lawyer in your state before closing. None of this limits a personal guarantee you sign for the loan.

### Layer 1: the entity

The SBA notes that an LLC protects owners from personal liability in most instances, so personal assets like a car, house and savings aren't at risk if the LLC faces bankruptcy or lawsuits.[^sba-business-structure-llc] A sole proprietorship gives no such separation: business assets and liabilities aren't separate from personal ones.[^sba-business-structure-sole-prop] The entity also decides which tax return the business files,[^irs-business-structures] so choose it with your CPA as well as your lawyer. See [entity setup](/q/entity-setup).

**Keep the separation real.**

- Open business bank accounts and run every business dollar through them.
- Sign contracts, leases and invoices in the company's name, with your title.
- Don't pay personal bills from the business or business bills personally.
- Keep the entity's records and filings current.

### Layer 2: insurance

An entity doesn't cover everything; the SBA describes business insurance as filling the gaps, and notes that laws requiring insurance vary by state.[^sba-business-insurance-required] Common types to discuss with an insurance agent include general liability, professional liability, commercial property, auto, workers' compensation and an umbrella policy. Lenders have their own requirements too: for SBA 7(a) loans over $50,000, hazard insurance on all collateral.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-hazard-insurance] See [insurance review](/q/insurance-review).

### Layer 3: how personal assets are held

How your home is titled, what's in retirement accounts, and what's owned jointly with a spouse can change what a creditor can reach. Protections vary widely by state. Ask a lawyer in your state what applies and how timing affects it; plan before you sign, not after a problem appears.

### What these layers don't do

- **The loan guarantee.** For an SBA 7(a) loan, under SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), every individual owning 20% or more of the borrower gives an unlimited personal guarantee.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-personal-guaranty] The guarantee reaches you regardless of the entity. See [personal guarantees](/q/personal-guarantee) and [collateral and your home](/q/collateral-home).
- **Your own conduct.** An entity doesn't shield you from liability for your own actions, such as personally guaranteed leases or obligations you take on in your own name. Ask your lawyer what else you're signing personally.
- **Inherited liabilities.** In a stock purchase, the company keeps its past liabilities. See [asset or stock purchase](/q/asset-vs-stock) and [indemnification](/q/indemnification).

### Questions for your lawyer and agent

- Which entity fits this deal, and what keeps its protection intact?
- What else am I signing personally: the lease, supplier credit, equipment financing?
- What does our state protect, and how should we title our home and accounts?
- What coverage do this business's risks call for, and what does the lender require?
