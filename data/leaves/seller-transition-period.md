**Short answer.** Long enough to hand over what only the seller knows: key customer and supplier relationships, how the work actually gets done, pricing, and any licenses tied to them. Set the length by that list rather than by a standard number of months, and put it in a written agreement. If you're using an SBA 7(a) loan (a bank loan partly guaranteed by the U.S. Small Business Administration) for an initial acquisition, the seller can stay only as a consultant, for up to 24 months in total.

**SBA rule.** Under SBA SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), in an initial acquisition or business expansion the seller may not remain as an officer, director, stockholder or employee. If a transition period is needed, the business may contract with the seller as a consultant for no more than 24 months, including extensions.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-seller-consultant]

**Build the handover list in diligence:**

- Customers: which ones need a personal introduction, and in what order.
- Suppliers and partners: who the seller calls, and what terms are informal.
- Know-how: estimating, pricing, scheduling, quality checks, seasonal tasks.
- Licenses, permits and certifications that depend on the seller.
- Systems and records: where things are, and what's only in the seller's head.

**How the time usually works:** more hours at first, tapering as you take over, with a clear end. Many buyers agree a set number of hours per week for the first period and on-call help after.

**What the seller should and shouldn't do.** The seller introduces, explains and answers questions; you make the decisions. Agree how they'll describe their role to employees and customers, and how either of you can end the arrangement early. See [seller consulting agreements](/q/seller-consulting-agreement) and [a seller who won't let go](/q/seller-wont-let-go).
