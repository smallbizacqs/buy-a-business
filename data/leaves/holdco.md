**Short answer.** A holding company (holdco), a parent entity meant to own several businesses, adds cost and complexity before you've shown you can run one business. It makes sense when you already have the capital, the operating bench and a plan for what you'll buy next, or when investors are backing a multi-company strategy from day one. If you plan to use SBA loans, know that SBA treats the holdco and every company it owns as one borrower group for loan limits.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-affiliates-max]

### What a holdco is, and isn't

A holdco is an entity whose job is to own other companies. The operating businesses typically sit in separate subsidiaries, each with its own contracts, employees and liabilities. Which entity type you use, and how it's taxed, are legal and tax questions; the IRS notes that your form of business determines which return you file and that legal and tax considerations enter into the choice.[^irs-business-structures] A holdco isn't a strategy by itself. The strategy is what you buy, why owning them together helps, and who runs each one.

### Reasons to start with one deal

- **Your first business needs your attention.** Learning a business you just bought leaves little room to run a second search.
- **Lenders and investors back track records.** One business run well is the strongest evidence you can do the next.
- **Structure can come later.** A parent company can be added once you know what you're building. Ask a lawyer and CPA what moving an existing business under a holdco would cost in taxes, consents and lender approvals.

### Reasons to set up a holdco from the start

- Investors have committed capital for several acquisitions, and the documents need one entity to hold them.
- You're buying a platform company and already have identified add-ons in the same industry.
- You want shared services (finance, HR, purchasing) across companies and have the people to run them.

### How SBA treats a holdco

- **Limits are combined.** If affiliation exists, SBA applies its loan maximums to the borrower and all affiliates as if they were a single business.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-affiliates-max] The largest Standard 7(a) loan is $5 million,[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-max-loan] so a holdco that keeps borrowing SBA money runs into the cap across all its companies.
- **Size is combined.** In an acquisition, the buyer and the acquired business together must meet SBA size standards.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-combined-size]
- **Follow-on deals.** A business that has operated for at least two full fiscal years under its current owners can buy 100% of another business as a "Business Expansion"[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-business-expansion] if the target is in the same four-digit NAICS industry group.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-business-expansion-naics] Otherwise the deal falls into the default Initial Acquisition category.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-initial-acquisition-default]
- **The holdco guarantees.** An entity owning 20% or more of the borrower must give an unlimited guaranty,[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-entity-guaranty] which exposes the holdco's other holdings.
- **Some structures are out.** SBA won't finance a partial change of ownership done by forming a new entity shared by old and new owners.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-multi-step-partial]

### Questions to answer before you decide

1. Do you have the money for more than one deal without starving the first?
2. Who will run business number one while you buy number two?
3. Why are these businesses worth more together than apart?
4. How will lenders, guarantees and cross-default clauses connect the companies?
