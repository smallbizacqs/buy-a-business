**Short answer.** Read the lease early, find out whether the landlord must consent to a transfer (many commercial leases require it), and make the landlord's signed consent, or a new lease, a condition of closing. Check that the remaining term and renewal options cover the time you need, including your loan term. Talk to the landlord only with the seller's agreement and at a point you've both planned.

**Asset or stock purchase.** In an asset purchase, the seller assigns the lease to your company, which usually needs the landlord's consent under the lease. In a stock purchase, the tenant doesn't change, but many leases treat a change of ownership as an assignment, so check for that clause.

**What to check in the lease:**

- Remaining term and renewal options, and who can exercise them.
- Rent, scheduled increases, and who pays taxes, insurance and repairs.
- The assignment clause: whether consent can be withheld, and any fee or rent increase on transfer.
- Personal guarantees: the landlord may want one from you, and the seller will want theirs released.
- Use clause, exclusivity, relocation or early-termination rights, and the condition the space must be returned in.

**Documents landlords often sign at closing:** a consent to assignment, and an estoppel certificate, which is the landlord's written confirmation that the lease is in effect, rent is paid, and no defaults are known.

**With an SBA 7(a) loan.** A copy of the lease goes in the application file.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-application-lease-copy] Where a large share of the loan or collateral relates to leasehold improvements or attached equipment, SBA SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026) says the lender must get the lease, and the term, including your renewal options, should equal or exceed the loan term.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-lease-term] Your lender tells you what it needs; ask early, because a landlord's response can take time. An SBA 7(a) loan is a bank loan partly guaranteed by the U.S. Small Business Administration (SBA).
