**Short answer.** In a small deal, legal diligence answers a short list of questions: does the seller actually own what it's selling, can it be transferred to you, what debts and claims come with it, and can the business keep operating legally after closing? That means the entity and ownership records, liens, the lease and key contracts, licenses and permits, lawsuits and claims, employment practices, and intellectual property. It's narrower than in a large deal, and it should be aimed at the risks this business actually has.

### What it covers

**Entity and ownership.** Formation documents, operating agreement or bylaws, the list of owners, and good standing with the state. In a stock purchase you need to know who owns every share or unit and whether anyone else has rights to them (options, buy-sell agreements, a spouse's interest). In an asset purchase you need to know the selling entity has authority to sell.

**Liens and debts.** Public filings show which lenders have a security interest in the business's assets. Each one has to be paid off and released at closing, or you take assets subject to it. See [hidden liabilities and lawsuits](/q/litigation-liabilities). SBA lenders discuss any liens, judgments, bankruptcy filings or pending litigation in their credit analysis,[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-credit-memo-liens-litigation] so problems here also affect the loan.

**The lease.** Whether it can be assigned, how long it runs, and what the landlord will require. Under SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), where the lease rule applies, the lease term including renewal options should equal or exceed the 7(a) loan term.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-lease-term] See [the landlord and the lease](/q/lease-assignment) and [landlord estoppels](/q/landlord-estoppel).

**Key contracts.** Customer, supplier, franchise, software and equipment agreements: whether they can be assigned or end on a change of control, exclusivity, minimum purchase commitments, and termination rights. See [whether contracts transfer](/q/contract-assignment).

**Licenses and permits.** Which state, county and city licenses a business needs depends on what it does and where,[^sba-licenses-permits] and some are personal to the owner. See [licenses and permits](/q/licenses-permits-transfer) and [regulatory compliance](/q/regulatory-compliance).

**Litigation and claims.** Pending and threatened lawsuits, demand letters, government inspections and complaints, and insurance claims history.

**Employment.** Offer letters, non-competes and confidentiality agreements with key staff, worker classification (the IRS weighs behavioral control, financial control and the type of relationship[^irs-worker-classification]), wage-and-hour practices, and benefit plans. An employer that acquires a company has to decide how to handle the existing employees' Forms I-9.[^uscis-m274-acquisitions] See [employee diligence](/q/employee-dd).

**Intellectual property and online assets.** Trademarks, domains, software and social accounts, and whether they're owned by the business or by the owner personally. See [IP, code and domains](/q/ip-tech).

**Real estate and environmental**, where the deal includes property or the business handles hazardous materials. See [environmental reports](/q/environmental).

### How the scope changes with structure

In an asset purchase you can leave most liabilities behind, so more effort goes into whether each asset, contract and license can be transferred. In a stock purchase the company's history comes with it, so more effort goes into claims, taxes and compliance. See [asset or stock purchase](/q/asset-vs-stock).

### What legal diligence feeds

The findings become the disclosure schedules (the seller's written list of exceptions to its representations), closing conditions, payoff and release requirements, and any special indemnity. See [reps and warranties](/q/reps-warranties) and [closing conditions](/q/closing-conditions). For how to choose and scope counsel, see [choosing counsel](/q/choosing-counsel).
