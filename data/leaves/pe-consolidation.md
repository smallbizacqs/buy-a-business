**Short answer.** It can be both. Private equity (PE) firms buying up many small companies in an industry (a "roll-up") can raise the prices sellers expect and compete with you for deals and workers. They can also become the eventual buyer of your business, at a higher multiple than you paid. Whether it's good or bad for you depends on whether you're buying now, competing locally for customers and staff, or planning to sell to them later.

**How to tell whether it's happening**

- Owners and brokers mention offers from "platforms" or groups buying several companies.
- Local competitors keep their names but now share a parent company, website footer or benefits plan.
- Trade association events and industry press feature acquirers and "partnership" pitches.
- Job ads from competitors offer signing bonuses for the same technicians you need.
- Sellers quote multiples that only make sense for a larger buyer.

**Where it can hurt a small buyer**

- **Higher asking prices.** Sellers hear what a roll-up paid a larger company and expect the same for a smaller one. A price paid for a larger, professionalized company doesn't carry over to a small owner-run one. See [what earns a higher multiple](/q/value-drivers).
- **Competition for deals.** A well-funded buyer can move fast and pay cash.
- **Competition for workers.** Consolidators may raise wages or recruit your staff. See [labor availability](/q/labor-availability).
- **Competition for customers.** A larger competitor may outspend you on marketing or win contracts that want scale.
- **Supplier terms.** Bigger buyers may get better pricing from the same distributors.

**Where it can help**

- **An exit.** A consolidator may be a buyer for your business later, and may pay more than an individual buyer would if the business fits their platform. See [selling to private equity](/q/sell-to-pe).
- **Local service gaps.** After a roll-up, some customers and employees may leave if service gets less personal or policies change. A local, owner-run business can win them.
- **Proof of value.** Active consolidation signals that investors see steady demand in the industry.

**Questions to ask in diligence**

- Who has bought competitors in this market in the last few years, and what changed afterward?
- Has the target lost staff or customers to a consolidated competitor?
- Has the seller been approached? What did they hear, and why didn't they sell? (The answer may explain the asking price.)
- Would this business fit what consolidators look for, and how far is it from that today?

**A balanced view**

Don't pay today's price based on a hoped-for sale to a consolidator: their appetite can change. Buy at a price that works on the business's own cash flow, and treat a future roll-up buyer as possible upside. See [exit options](/q/exit-options) and [roll-up strategy](/q/rollup-strategy).
