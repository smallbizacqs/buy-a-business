**Short answer.** An independent sponsor finds a business, negotiates the deal and then raises equity for that one deal, instead of raising a fund first. The sponsor may put in some of its own money, gets paid through fees at closing and while it owns the company, and earns a larger share of the profits (a "promote" or "carry") if investors get their money back plus an agreed return. Because there's no committed fund, the hard part is convincing a seller you'll close and investors that the deal is worth backing.

### How a deal runs

1. **Source a business** and get it under a letter of intent (LOI), ideally with exclusivity. See [the LOI](/q/loi-contents) and [exclusivity](/q/exclusivity).
2. **Line up debt**: a bank, an SBA lender, private credit or a seller note. See [capital stack](/q/capital-stack).
3. **Raise equity for the deal** from individuals, family offices or funds that back independent sponsors. Expect investors to ask for a deal memo, a financial model and diligence results.
4. **Close** through a new entity, such as an LLC, that the investors and the sponsor own.
5. **Run or oversee** the business, as CEO or as chair with a hired CEO, and later sell or recapitalize it.

### How the sponsor is paid

The economics are negotiated deal by deal. Pieces to negotiate include:

- **A closing fee** paid by the company when the deal closes.
- **A management or monitoring fee** paid while the sponsor oversees the company.
- **A co-investment**: the sponsor's own money, invested on the same terms as investors.
- **A promote**: a share of profits above the investors' return, often stepping up as returns rise.

Investors will weigh every fee against the cash the business needs. See [investor terms](/q/investor-terms) for preferred returns and carry, and [co-sponsors](/q/co-gp-jv) if you share the deal with a partner.

### Rules that apply

- **Securities law.** If you raise under the SEC's Rule 506: under Rule 506(b) you can't advertise the raise;[^ecfr-17-cfr-230-502-general-solicitation] under Rule 506(c) you can, but every purchaser must be accredited and you must take reasonable steps to verify it.[^ecfr-17-cfr-230-506-c-accredited][^ecfr-17-cfr-230-506-c-verification] A Form D notice is due within 15 days of the first sale.[^ecfr-17-cfr-230-503-form-d] Certain past violations by you or others involved can disqualify the exemption.[^ecfr-17-cfr-230-506-bad-actor] See [Rule 506(b) or 506(c)](/q/506b-506c-securities).
- **Paying people to raise money.** A "broker" under federal securities law is anyone in the business of effecting securities transactions for others.[^usc-15-78c-broker-definition] Paying someone a success fee to bring you investors can raise broker registration issues; ask securities counsel before you agree to one.
- **SBA loans.** If the deal uses an SBA loan, anyone owning 20% or more must guarantee it personally.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-personal-guaranty] Passive investors under 20% with no control can supply part of the equity, but their distributions are limited to taxes until the loan is repaid.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-minority-investor][^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-investor-distributions] Weigh that against conventional debt. See [SBA rules for investors](/q/investor-equity-rules).

### When the model fits

- You have a track record or industry depth that investors will pay for.
- The deal is larger than you could fund with an SBA loan and your own money.
- You can carry the cost of searching and diligence until a deal closes, since there's no fund paying your salary.

For the securities and entity work, buyers who hire counsel can look for AI-using law firms with fixed fees that are lean and run by an attorney with big-law experience. See [raising from investors](/q/raise-from-investors).
