**Short answer.** Check before you buy, in the business's own market. Look at how long the business has taken to fill jobs, how much turnover it has, what it pays compared with local competitors, and how many open postings exist for the same role nearby. If growth or even current volume depends on hiring people the business has struggled to find, treat that as a cost and a cap on growth, and price it in.

**Signs the business has a labor problem**

- Jobs turned down or scheduled weeks out because there aren't enough crews.
- Heavy overtime, or the owner doing fieldwork to fill gaps.
- Positions open for months.
- High turnover in the first year of employment.
- Pay below what competitors post for the same role.
- A workforce near retirement age with no apprentices behind them.
- Reliance on contractors or temp agencies to fill core roles.

**What to look at in diligence**

1. **Headcount history.** Hires, departures and open roles by month for two or three years.
2. **Pay vs. market.** Compare wages and benefits with current local job postings for the same role.
3. **Where hires come from.** Referrals, trade schools, apprenticeship programs, job boards, poaching.
4. **Training.** How long until a new hire is productive, and who trains them.
5. **Who the critical people are.** Skilled leads and licensed workers matter most. SBA's definition of a key employee includes someone with the experience, qualifications or required license to run the business.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-key-employee-definition] See [key employee risk](/q/key-employee-risk).
6. **Contractor use.** If the business uses 1099 contractors for core work, check how they're classified. The IRS weighs behavioral control, financial control and the type of relationship to decide whether a worker is really an employee.[^irs-worker-classification] Misclassified workers are a tax and legal exposure, and fixing it raises costs; ask your CPA and lawyer to review.

**Check the local market yourself**

Search job sites for the role in the business's area and note how many openings, at what pay. Call a trade school or apprenticeship program and ask how many graduates they place and who hires them. Talk to a recruiter who works in the trade. Your goal is a realistic answer to: if two techs quit next month, how long to replace them, and at what wage?

**In the price and plan**

- **Model higher wages.** If the business pays below market, assume you'll have to raise pay to keep and hire people, and run that through earnings before setting a price.
- **Discount growth.** Don't pay for revenue growth that needs crews you can't hire.
- **Budget for recruiting and training** in your first year: bonuses, ads, apprentice wages.
- **Plan your pipeline.** Relationships with schools, apprentice programs, referral bonuses and a reputation as a good employer take time to build.
