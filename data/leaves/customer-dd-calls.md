**Short answer.** Usually yes, for the customers that matter most, but only with the seller's agreement, late in diligence, and in a way the seller is comfortable with. Customer calls are often the only way to learn whether customers are loyal to the business or to the owner personally, and whether they plan to stay. Agree in advance who calls, which customers, and what you'll say.

**When and how:**

- **Timing**: after the letter of intent, once the financial work looks solid, and close enough to closing that a leak would do limited harm.
- **Who**: the largest customers by revenue, a few typical ones, and, if possible, someone who recently left.
- **Framing**: often the seller introduces you, for example as a new partner or as their planned successor, as you and the seller agree. Don't contact anyone on your own.
- **Confidentiality**: the seller may worry about customers, employees or competitors hearing of the sale. Respect the limits you agree.

**What to ask:** Why do they buy from this business? Who do they deal with? What would make them switch? Are prices, contracts or volume about to change? How do they feel about a new owner?

**What to listen for:** loyalty to the owner rather than the business, upcoming bids or contract renewals, complaints about service, or plans to bring work in-house.

**Other ways to check:** contract terms and renewal dates, revenue by customer over several years, online reviews, and churn data (how many customers leave each year). If a quality of earnings report (an accountant's independent check of whether the earnings are real and repeatable) is required for an SBA loan (a bank loan partly guaranteed by the U.S. Small Business Administration), SBA SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026) says it must assess customer concentration and contract continuity.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-qoe-revenue-quality]
