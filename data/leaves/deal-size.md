**Short answer.** There's no standard right size. The range that fits you comes from three things: the cash you can put in, the earnings the business needs to pay its loans and pay you a living, and how much risk you can carry. Work those out first, then set a range of earnings and price you'd look at, and adjust it once you see real listings.

**Start from your money**

The SBA's guide for buyers starts in the same place: decide how much you're willing to spend to buy the business and then run it.[^sba-buy-business-quantify-investment] If you plan to use an SBA 7(a) loan (a bank loan partly guaranteed by the U.S. Small Business Administration), two rules set the outer limits. Under SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), an initial acquisition needs at least 10% equity from the buyer,[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-equity-minimum] measured against the total project cost (everything needed to become operational, not just the price).[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-equity-project-cost] The largest single Standard 7(a) loan is $5 million.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-max-loan] Your cash, less a reserve you won't spend, divided by the equity share your lender asks for, gives a rough ceiling on project cost. See [how much of my own money an SBA loan requires](/q/equity-injection).

**Then check the earnings**

A business has to cover its debt and pay you. Under the same SOP, the lender tests historical cash flow against all loan payments after the deal, at 1.25:1 or better for an initial acquisition,[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-dsc-ratio] and your pay from the business must be enough to cover your current obligations and living expenses.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-owner-compensation] So the floor on size is the earnings that can carry the debt and still leave you a salary you can live on. Two common earnings measures are SDE (seller's discretionary earnings: profit before the owner's pay and perks, interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) and EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, after paying a manager or owner a salary). See [SDE vs EBITDA](/q/sde-vs-ebitda) and [how much you can pay given the loan payments](/q/affordability).

**What changes as deals get bigger or smaller**

- **Smaller businesses** cost less and are easier to finance, but they can depend more on the owner, have fewer employees to share the work, and give you less room for a bad year. You'll likely work in the business, not just on it.
- **Larger businesses** are more likely to have managers and settled systems, but need more equity, bring more debt, and draw more experienced buyers.
- **SBA process steps scale with price.** Under SOP 50 10 8.1, for a business purchase price of $350,000 or less the lender may do its own valuation instead of hiring an outside valuation professional (unless buyer and seller are closely related),[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-valuation-350k] and at $3 million or more the lender must also get a quality of earnings report (QoE, an accountant's review of whether the earnings are real and repeatable).[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-qoe-threshold]

**Questions that settle your range**

1. How much cash can you invest and still keep a reserve?
2. What salary do you need, and how long could you go without one?
3. Would you guarantee the loan personally? See [personal guarantees](/q/personal-guarantee).
4. Do you want to run the work yourself or manage people who do? See [run it yourself or hire a manager](/q/operator-vs-absentee).
5. What has your experience prepared you to manage?

**Common mistakes**

Picking a size because it sounds impressive rather than because you can afford it, forgetting closing costs and working capital in the project cost, and assuming a business with thin earnings will grow fast enough to cover the debt. A lender won't count on that, and you shouldn't either. Put your range into your [buy box](/q/define-buy-box) and ask your lender to sanity-check it before you start making offers.
