**Short answer.** Prevent it with a written transition plan that defines the seller's role, hours, end date and limits, and agree before closing how the seller will describe the change to staff and customers. If interference starts anyway, raise it early and privately, point back to what you agreed, and redirect employees to you. If it continues, the consulting agreement is the tool: narrow the scope, reduce the hours, or end it under its terms, with advice from your lawyer.

### Why it happens

Sellers interfere for understandable reasons: the business was their identity, they worry about employees and customers they care about, they disagree with your changes, or they still have money at stake through a seller note or an earnout. Knowing which reason applies helps you choose the response. A seller worried about their note needs information about how the business is doing; a seller who misses the work needs a defined, useful role and an end date.

### Prevent it before closing

- **Write the role down.** A [consulting agreement](/q/seller-consulting-agreement) should say what the seller does (introductions, training, answering questions), what they don't do (direct employees, set prices, commit the business), the hours, the end date, and how either side can end it early.
- **Decide the chain of command.** Employees should hear from both of you that decisions now come from you.
- **Agree on information.** If the seller holds a seller note, agree what reports they'll get and how often, so they don't need to walk the floor to feel safe.
- **Plan the exit.** Set dates for handing back keys, passwords, email accounts, bank access and the seller's office.

**If you have an SBA loan.** Under SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective October 1, 2026), in an initial acquisition or business expansion financed with an SBA 7(a) loan (a bank loan partly guaranteed by the U.S. Small Business Administration), the seller may not remain as an officer, director, stockholder or employee, and may stay only as a consultant for no more than 24 months in total, including extensions.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-seller-consultant] A seller who keeps acting like an officer can be a problem for your loan as well as for your team, so tell your lender if the role is drifting.

### When it starts

Signs include employees going to the seller for decisions, the seller countermanding you, the seller contacting customers or vendors about changes without telling you, or the seller criticizing decisions in front of staff.

1. **Talk privately and early.** Name the specific behavior and its effect. Assume good intent at first.
2. **Restate the agreement.** Point to the written role and hours.
3. **Give the seller a better outlet.** A weekly meeting where they can raise concerns with you, rather than with staff.
4. **Redirect employees.** When someone goes to the seller, send them back to you, kindly and consistently. Ask the seller to do the same.
5. **Document.** Keep notes of incidents and conversations.

### If it doesn't stop

Options, depending on your agreements: reduce the seller's hours, limit access to the premises or systems, move the remaining work to remote or on-call, or end the consulting arrangement as the agreement allows. If the seller is breaching a non-compete, non-solicitation or confidentiality promise, or if a seller note or earnout makes the relationship contentious, get your lawyer's advice before acting, since your obligations under those documents continue too.

### Common mistakes

- No written role, so every disagreement becomes a negotiation.
- Letting the seller keep authority on bank accounts or vendor accounts after closing.
- Avoiding the conversation until staff are confused about who is in charge.
