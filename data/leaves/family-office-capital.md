**Short answer.** A family office manages one wealthy family's money, and some put part of it directly into private companies. For small-business buyers they show up in a few ways: as investors in a searcher or independent sponsor, as co-investors alongside other backers on one deal, or as direct buyers of companies they plan to hold. Because it's the family's own money and not a fixed-life fund, a family office can be patient, but each one decides for itself, and they vary widely in what they buy and how involved they get.

### How family offices back buyers and deals

- **Backing a searcher.** Buying units in a search fund, which pays for the search and gives a right to invest in the acquisition. See [investor-backed search funds](/q/traditional-search-fund).
- **Deal-by-deal equity.** Investing in one acquisition led by an independent sponsor or self-funded buyer. See [the independent sponsor model](/q/independent-sponsor).
- **Debt or preferred equity.** Some lend or take preferred stock instead of common equity. See [mezzanine and private credit](/q/mezz-private-credit).
- **Direct deals.** Buying companies themselves, sometimes with an operator they hire or back as CEO. See [permanent capital](/q/permanent-capital).

### What they tend to look for

Each office sets its own criteria. Questions they'll likely ask you:

- **You.** Your operating background, why you'll be good at running this business, and how much of your own money is in.
- **The business.** Durable cash flow, a reason it will still be around in ten years, and fit with the family's background or other holdings.
- **Hold period and liquidity.** Whether you plan to sell and when, or how they'd get cash out of a long hold.
- **Governance.** Board seats, information rights and approval rights on big decisions.
- **Reputation.** How the deal and its public footprint reflect on the family name.

See [what investors want](/q/what-investors-want).

### Rules that come up

- **Accredited status.** A family office with more than $5 million under management that meets the rule's other conditions is an accredited investor,[^ecfr-17-cfr-230-501-family-office] as is a company or partnership with more than $5 million in assets not formed to buy your securities.[^ecfr-17-cfr-230-501-entity-assets]
- **How you approach them.** Under Rule 506(b) you can't use general solicitation to find investors;[^ecfr-17-cfr-230-502-general-solicitation] under 506(c) you can, but you must take reasonable steps to verify each purchaser is accredited.[^ecfr-17-cfr-230-506-c-verification] See [Rule 506(b) or 506(c)](/q/506b-506c-securities).
- **If the deal uses an SBA loan.** Any owner of 20% or more, person or entity, must guarantee the loan.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-personal-guaranty][^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-entity-guaranty] Every direct and indirect owner must meet SBA's citizenship test, which reaches through a family office's entities to the people behind them.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-citizenship-owners] Investor money used for the equity injection can receive only tax distributions until the loan is repaid.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-investor-distributions] Some investors won't accept those terms, so raise them early.

### How to find and approach them

- Warm introductions through lawyers, accountants, bankers, other searchers and their investors.
- Conferences and communities focused on search funds and independent sponsors.
- A short, specific ask: who you are, what you're buying, how much you need, and the terms.

Ask how the office decides and who signs off; a family office may have no investment committee calendar, and the principal's attention can be hard to get.
