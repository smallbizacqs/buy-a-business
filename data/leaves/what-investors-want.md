**Short answer.** Investors in small-business buyers weigh three things: the person, the business and the deal. They want to believe you can run this company, that the business will keep producing cash after the owner leaves, and that the price and debt leave room for things to go wrong. Then they look at the terms. A strong business with a weak operator, or a great operator overpaying with too much debt, can be a pass.

### The person

- **Why you, for this business.** Relevant operating experience, management of people, sales ability, or deep knowledge of the industry. Search fund investors are backing an entrepreneur to find, buy, run and grow a company,[^stanford-gsb-search-fund-definition] so the person is the bet.
- **Commitment.** Your own money in the deal, your willingness to move or relocate, and whether you'll run it full time.
- **Judgment.** How you talk about risks and what you walked away from. A buyer who can't name a weakness in their own deal raises doubts.
- **Integrity and history.** Expect background checks. Certain past securities or financial violations by people involved in an offering can disqualify the Rule 506 exemption.[^ecfr-17-cfr-230-506-bad-actor]
- **Coachability.** Whether you'll use a board and listen to it.

### The business

- **Durable cash flow.** Several years of steady earnings, recurring or repeat customers, and a reason the business will still be needed in ten years.
- **Low dependence on the owner.** Customers, suppliers and know-how that will stay after the seller leaves. See [owner dependence](/q/owner-dependence).
- **Diversified revenue.** No customer, supplier or channel that could sink the business alone. A quality of earnings review looks at customer concentration and contract continuity for this reason.[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-qoe-revenue-quality] See [customer concentration](/q/customer-concentration).
- **Verified numbers.** Earnings that tie to tax returns and bank statements.
- **A plan.** A believable, modest set of improvements you'll make, not a turnaround.

### The deal

- **Price and structure.** A price you can defend against comparable deals, and some seller financing showing the seller believes in the business.
- **Debt that fits.** Coverage with room to spare. An SBA lender needs at least 1.25:1 for an initial acquisition;[^sba-sop-50-10-8-1-dsc-ratio] investors will ask what happens to coverage in a bad year. See [stress-testing a deal](/q/stress-test).
- **Terms that align.** Your promote paid when investors do well, fees that don't drain the company, and governance that lets them protect their money. See [investor terms](/q/investor-terms).
- **A path to returns.** How and when they get cash back: distributions, refinancing or a sale.

### How to show it

1. Lead your memo with the three-part case: you, the business, the deal.
2. Put the risks up front with what you'll do about each.
3. Show a downside case where the business still pays its debt.
4. Offer references, including people who've seen you manage through trouble.
5. Make diligence easy: a clean data room and fast answers.
