**Short answer.** Indemnification is the seller's promise in the purchase agreement to pay you for specific losses after closing: losses from a false representation, a broken promise, or a liability the seller kept. It's how the agreement turns "the seller said X" into money if X proves wrong. Its value depends on the limits that come with it and on whether there's money available to pay a claim.

**What it usually covers:**

- Breaches of the seller's representations and warranties.
- Breaches of the seller's covenants (promises), such as the non-compete.
- Excluded liabilities: debts and obligations the seller kept.
- Taxes for periods before closing.
- Specific known risks found in diligence (a "special indemnity").

**The limits that shape it:**

- **Basket**: losses must add up past a threshold before you can claim. It works either as a deductible (you recover only the excess) or as a tipping basket (once over, you recover from the first dollar).
- **Cap**: the most the seller has to pay. Some items, such as fraud or title to the assets, are often outside the cap.
- **Survival period**: how long after closing a claim can be made.
- **Exclusive remedy**: whether indemnification is your only way to recover.
- **Process**: notice, the seller's right to defend third-party claims, and how disputes are decided.

**Where the money comes from.** A promise is only as good as the ability to pay. Buyers secure it with an escrow or holdback, a right to set off claims against payments on a seller note, or insurance. See [escrow and holdbacks](/q/escrow-holdback). If you have an SBA loan with a seller note on standby, ask your lender and lawyer how a set-off would work under the standby agreement. An SBA 7(a) loan is a bank loan partly guaranteed by the U.S. Small Business Administration (SBA).

The numbers (basket, cap, survival period) are negotiated; your lawyer can tell you what's reasonable for your deal size. See [reps and warranties](/q/reps-warranties).
