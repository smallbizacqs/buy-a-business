**Short answer.** The business is mostly accounts, code and data instead of a building and a crew, so three things change: where you find deals (online marketplaces), how you check revenue (connected payment and analytics data, which still needs your own review), and what closing means (moving domains, code, app-store listings, payment accounts and customer data into your name, one platform at a time). The platforms the business runs on also become a risk you're buying.

**Where online businesses are listed**

Marketplaces such as Acquire.com list SaaS (software sold by subscription), e-commerce, agencies, content sites, newsletters and mobile apps.[^acquire-home-categories] See [listing marketplaces](/q/listing-marketplaces). The marketplaces described here aren't parties to your deal: Acquire.com's terms say it isn't a party to the transaction[^acquire-terms-not-a-party] and doesn't stand behind what sellers put in listings,[^acquire-terms-listing-accuracy] and Flippa's terms say it isn't a party to the sale contract.[^flippa-terms-role]

**Checking revenue**

- **Connected data helps but isn't diligence.** Some platforms show revenue pulled from connected payment providers;[^trustmrr-home-verified-revenue] Flippa's "Data Verified" badge means the seller connected at least one third-party source such as analytics or payments,[^flippa-terms-data-verified] and its terms say that isn't a substitute for your own diligence.[^flippa-terms-not-diligence]
- **Do your own pass.** Get read access to the payment processor, app-store reports and analytics. Check refunds, chargebacks, churn (customers who cancel), and revenue by month and by customer cohort. Reconcile to bank deposits and tax returns. See [proof of cash](/q/proof-of-cash) and [revenue quality and churn](/q/revenue-quality-churn).
- **Find out where customers come from.** One search engine, one ad channel or one marketplace driving most sales is a concentration risk, like a big customer.

**What you're actually buying**

Make a list of every asset and who controls it today: domain names, source code repositories, hosting and cloud accounts, app-store developer listings, payment processor accounts, email lists, social accounts, ad accounts, third-party software and API (application programming interface) subscriptions, trademarks, and contracts with contractors. Check that the code was written by the seller or by contractors who assigned their rights in writing. See [intellectual property, code and domains](/q/ip-tech).

**Transfers are platform by platform**

Platforms that allow transfers have their own process for moving an asset to a new owner, with their own conditions. Google Play, for example, has a formal process for transferring apps from one developer account to another.[^google-play-app-transfer] Some accounts can't be transferred at all and must be recreated, which can mean new merchant approvals or losing reviews and history. Confirm each transfer route before you sign, and build the list into the purchase agreement and closing checklist.

**Risks that are different online**

- **Platform dependence.** A change to an app store's rules, a search algorithm or a payment processor's terms can cut revenue overnight.
- **Key-person risk in code.** If only the seller understands the codebase, plan the handover carefully. See [the seller's transition period](/q/seller-transition-period).
- **Security and data.** Rotate every password, key and secret after closing; check what personal data the business holds and under what privacy terms.

**Paying and financing**

Read each marketplace's terms on fees, escrow (a neutral party holding the money until the transfer is done) and where payments must go. Whether an SBA loan can fund an online business is its own question: see [SBA loans for online businesses](/q/online-saas-eligibility).
