# Exit playbook

## A. Exit routes compared

Descriptions are general patterns, not promises. Who shows up for a particular business depends on its size, industry, location, records and market conditions.

| Route | Who buys | What they look for | How they usually pay | Owner's role after | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Private equity, as a platform | A fund buying its first company in a sector | Larger earnings, a management team beyond the owner, room to grow by acquisition | Cash plus often rollover equity (owner keeps a stake) and sometimes an earnout | Often stays for a period; may keep a board seat | Rollover stake depends on the fund's later sale; detailed diligence; formal reporting |
| Private equity, as an add-on | A fund's existing company in the sector | Fit with the platform: territory, services, customers | Cash, sometimes rollover or earnout | Shorter transition, often | Integration changes the business quickly |
| Strategic or industry buyer | A competitor, supplier or adjacent company | Customers, territory, people, licenses | Cash, sometimes seller note or earnout | Transition period | Confidentiality: a competitor sees your numbers; keep sensitive data back until late |
| Individual buyer or search fund | A person (often with an SBA-backed loan) or a searcher with investors | Steady earnings, transferable relationships, a business one person can run | Bank loan + buyer equity + seller note; search funds may add investor equity | Transition period; seller note means continued exposure to the buyer's success | Buyer's financing can fail late; lender rules shape the seller note and the seller's role |
| Employees, direct sale | Managers or key staff | Continuity | Seller note over years; sometimes a bank loan | Often longer transition | Payment depends on the business doing well under them |
| Employees, ESOP | A trust buying for employees | Size and earnings enough to carry setup and ongoing costs | Often bank debt plus seller notes | Can stay | Setup cost and complexity; needs specialist advisors |
| Family | Children or relatives | Willingness and ability to run it | Gift, sale, or mix | Varies | Family dynamics; tax planning (CPA) |
| Partial sale or recapitalization | A PE fund or investor buying a stake | Growth story; an owner who stays | Cash for part; owner keeps the rest | Stays, with a partner | A new partner's rights and exit timeline |
| Refinance to take money out | A lender | Coverage and collateral | New debt pays the owner | Stays, fully | More debt, less room in a downturn |
| Keep it, hire a manager | Nobody | Cash flow after the manager's pay | Distributions over time | Board-like role | Manager risk; less value if owner dependence was the issue |
| Wind down | Nobody, or asset buyers | Equipment, customer lists | Asset sales | Ends | Obligations to employees, landlord, lenders (lawyer) |

Practitioner views differ on timing (sell at a peak vs. hold for steady income) and on whether to run a broad sale process or deal with one interested buyer; present the views.

## C. Recapitalization and refinancing

- **Refinance:** new or larger loan; proceeds go to the owner. Questions for the lender: how much it will lend against earnings and assets, terms, covenants, personal guarantee. Risks: higher payments, less cushion.
- **Partial sale (recap):** an investor buys a stake; the owner takes cash for part and keeps the rest. Questions for a lawyer: the investor's rights (board, vetoes, forced sale), the timeline to a full exit, what happens if goals diverge.
- **Paying off the original seller note early:** check the note's prepayment terms and any lender standby or subordination agreement first (lawyer, lender).
- Tax treatment of any of these: CPA.

## D. What if no one will buy

Early signs: owner dependence still high; earnings falling; one customer dominant; industry buyers not interested at the asking price; no lender will finance a buyer.

Options: fix the gaps and try later; lower the price or carry more of it as a seller note; sell to employees over time; keep it and hire a manager; sell the assets or the customer list; wind down in an orderly way (lawyer for obligations to employees, landlord and lenders). Say these plainly; don't offer false comfort.

## E. Questions for the CPA (tax): list, don't answer

1. How would the proceeds be taxed under an asset sale versus a stock sale for my entity type?
2. How does the purchase price allocation affect my tax, and what should I watch in a buyer's proposed allocation?
3. How are a seller note, an earnout or rollover equity taxed, and when?
4. Is there planning I should do now, years before a sale, and what are its costs?
5. State tax: does where I live or where the business operates change the answer?
6. What records will a buyer's diligence expect from me on taxes?

## F. Questions for a sell-side broker or M&A advisor (by category)

1. How many businesses of this size and industry have you sold in the last three years, and can I speak to those sellers?
2. How do you charge (success fee, retainer, minimums), and what is the fee if a buyer I already know buys?
3. Who would you approach (individuals, search funds, strategics, PE), and why?
4. How do you keep the sale confidential from employees, customers and competitors?
5. What would you list it for, how did you get that figure, and what evidence supports it?
6. How long is the engagement, and how do I end it?
