# Asset purchase vs stock (or membership interest) purchase

Plain-language comparison so the buyer can have a useful conversation with their CPA and lawyer. It describes how the two usually work; it does not tell anyone which is better for their taxes or how a specific contract or license behaves.

## What each is

- **Asset purchase.** The buyer (usually a new entity the buyer forms) buys the business's assets: equipment, inventory, customer lists, phone numbers, domains, trade names, goodwill. The seller's company stays with the seller, along with liabilities the buyer doesn't agree to take.
- **Stock purchase / membership interest purchase.** The buyer buys the company itself (the shares of a corporation or the membership interests of an LLC). The company keeps everything it has, including its history, contracts, licenses and liabilities, known and unknown.

## Side by side

| Question | Asset purchase (usual pattern) | Stock purchase (usual pattern) |
|---|---|---|
| Which liabilities come along? | Only those the buyer agrees to assume in the purchase agreement; some can follow the business anyway by law (ask the lawyer about successor liability, sales tax and payroll tax rules in the state) | All of the company's, past and present; protection comes from reps, indemnity, escrow and insurance |
| Contracts | Each one must be assigned; some need the other side's consent | Stay with the company; some have change-of-control clauses that need consent anyway |
| Licenses and permits | Often must be reissued to the buyer; some can't be transferred | Usually stay with the company, but some issuers treat a change of owner as a new application |
| Employees | Seller terminates; buyer rehires those it chooses | Stay employed by the same company |
| Lease | Assigned with landlord consent, or a new lease | Stays in place; check change-of-control language |
| Bank accounts, tax IDs, payroll accounts | Buyer sets up new ones | Continue |
| Tax effects | Usually different for buyer and seller; both buyer and seller report the sale on IRS Form 8594 (source `irs-form-8594-who-files`) | Different again; allocation rules differ | 
| Complexity | Lists of assets and assumed liabilities; many assignments | Fewer transfers; heavier diligence on history |

## When a stock purchase usually comes up

- Licenses, permits, government or agency contracts or certifications that can't be transferred or would take months to reissue.
- Key customer contracts that can't be assigned without consent and consent is risky to ask for.
- The seller insists for tax reasons (often a C corporation seller); ask the buyer's CPA what the buyer gives up, and whether any election or restructuring changes the picture. Those are CPA and lawyer questions, not a choice to make from a table.

## Questions for the CPA

1. For this seller's entity type, how do asset and stock purchases differ for the buyer's taxes over the next several years?
2. In an asset purchase, how should the buyer think about the price allocation on IRS Form 8594, and when do we agree it with the seller?
3. If the seller wants a stock sale, is there a structure that changes the buyer's tax position, and what does it cost?
4. How do payments to the seller under a consulting agreement or non-compete get treated for each side?
5. Any state sales, transfer or payroll tax issues with either structure in this state?

## Questions for the lawyer

1. Which liabilities could follow the business to the buyer even in an asset purchase in this state and industry?
2. Which of the key contracts, licenses, permits and the lease need consent or reissue under each structure?
3. In a stock purchase, what protections do we need for unknown past liabilities (reps, indemnity, escrow, holdback, insurance)?
4. Does the lender's structure require a particular form of purchase?

## Questions for the lender

1. Does the lender have a preference or requirement between asset and stock purchase for this deal?
2. In a stock purchase, what does the lender need about the company's existing debts and liens?

## Partial purchases (buying a majority now, the rest later)

Some deals buy a majority stake with a defined path to buy the rest. Points to raise with the lawyer and lender:

- How the remaining stake is priced later (fixed price, formula on earnings, appraisal) and who can trigger it (buyer call, seller put, both).
- Governance until then: who makes which decisions, what the minority owner can block.
- Whether the lender will finance a partial purchase and what it requires of the remaining owner. For SBA 7(a) loans, a selling owner who keeps less than 20% must guarantee the full loan for at least two years after final disbursement (source `sba-sop-50-10-8-1-partial-change-guaranty`), and anyone owning 20% or more gives an unlimited full personal guaranty (source `sba-sop-50-10-8-1-personal-guaranty`). Check `review_by` dates and `sba-sop-rule-check`.
- What happens if the seller dies, becomes disabled or stops working.
