# Transition playbook

## A. Owner-job inventory

Sit with the seller for one long session (before closing if possible) and walk each category. Ask "what did you do last Tuesday?" rather than "what do you do?"; people forget routine work when asked in general.

| Category | Examples to ask about |
|---|---|
| Selling and pricing | Who quotes jobs, how prices are set, discounts given to whom, which bids they turn down and why |
| Key customer relationships | Who calls the owner directly, who has the owner's cell number, who gets special terms or favors |
| Supplier relationships | Credit terms, who to call when something's late, verbal deals, rebates |
| Operations | Scheduling, dispatch, quality checks, the jobs only the owner can fix |
| People | Hiring, firing, pay decisions, who complains to whom, informal promises (raises, time off, loans) |
| Money | Who pays bills, who signs checks, how payroll is run, how cash is handled, the bookkeeper's routine |
| Compliance | Licenses held in the owner's name, permits, inspections, renewals and their dates |
| Systems and access | Every login, admin right, domain, phone number, alarm code and key |
| Calendar | Annual events: seasonal peaks, renewals, insurance audits, tax deadlines, trade shows, contract rebids |
| Unwritten knowledge | Quirks of equipment, the customer who must never be sent a certain technician, the landlord's preferences |

Mark each item: **transfer to buyer**, **transfer to employee**, **document and retire**, or **seller keeps for now** (with an end date).

## B. Handover phases

Fit the phases to the agreed length. A common shape for a short transition:

| Phase | Typical share of the period | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Shadow | first quarter | Buyer watches the seller run the business; seller introduces buyer to staff, key customers and suppliers; buyer writes down how things are done. |
| 2. Co-run | second quarter | Buyer does, seller watches and corrects. Buyer takes over money and payroll first (control), selling and pricing next. |
| 3. Buyer leads | third quarter | Buyer runs it; seller handles only items marked "seller keeps"; staff bring questions to the buyer. |
| 4. On call | last quarter | Seller off-site, available by phone for set hours; scheduled check-ins only. |

Each item needs a "done when" test that someone other than the buyer could check. Examples: "Buyer ran payroll twice without help." "Top five customers have met the buyer and have the buyer's number." "All admin passwords changed and in the buyer's password manager."

Weekly 30-minute check-in with the seller: what transferred, what's stuck, what's coming in the calendar.

## C. Announcements

### Order and timing (a common sequence; adjust to the deal)
1. Key employees: many buyers tell one to three key people shortly before closing, with the seller, under a confidentiality request; others wait until closing day to avoid leaks and to avoid people hearing before the deal is certain. This is a school-of-thought question; present both, with the trade-off (earlier: retention conversation before the news spreads; later: no risk of a deal that falls through after people have been told).
2. All employees: on closing day or the next working morning, in person, seller and buyer together. Before customers hear.
3. Top customers: within the first days, by the seller and buyer together, by visit or call, in order of revenue.
4. Other customers: a letter or email from both, after the top customers have heard personally.
5. Suppliers, landlord, bank, insurers: as part of the closing checklist; account changes need their own notices.

### Employee announcement (draft for the user's voice)
> "[Seller] has decided to retire / step back, and as of today I'm the new owner of [business]. [Seller] will be here through [date] to help me learn how you all do things. First, your jobs: [state what's true, for example 'everyone's job, pay and schedule stay the same' only if that is the plan and the lawyer has confirmed what changes on paper in an asset purchase]. I'm not here to change what works. Over the next few weeks I'll meet each of you one to one, mostly to listen. Payroll will come from [new entity] starting [date]; you'll get some paperwork to sign. Questions now, and any time, come to me."

Things not to say: promises about pay, roles or jobs you haven't decided; "nothing will ever change"; criticism of the seller; plans for growth or new systems in the first meeting.

### Customer message (draft)
> "After [N] years, [seller] is passing [business] to me, [buyer]. The same team will be serving you, and [seller] is staying on through [date] to make sure nothing drops. Your [pricing, schedule, contract, contact] stays the same. [Practical change, if any: new invoice details, new remittance account; give the date and say you'll confirm it by phone.] I'd like to meet you; I'll call this week."

Anything about payment details changing should be confirmed by phone; customers are targeted by invoice fraud during ownership changes too.

## D. Retention

For each key employee:
- What they'll worry about: pay, job security, a new boss's style, the seller leaving, changes to their role.
- What the buyer can honestly say, and what not to promise yet.
- Who meets them (buyer alone, after the joint announcement), when (first week), and the follow-up date.
- Options the user may consider with their CPA and lawyer: a stay bonus paid over time, a title or responsibility, a written employment offer. The skill lists options; the user decides.

For each top customer:
- Who holds the relationship today (often the seller personally).
- The handover: joint visit, then buyer leads the next touchpoint, then seller steps back.
- Risk signals: order volume drops, late payments, the customer asks for the seller by name after the handover, a competitor gets a bid in.
- What not to change in the first months: price, terms, the person they deal with day to day.

## E. Seller boundaries and escalation

Agree these at the start, in writing between buyer and seller (an email both reply to is enough for the working rules; the legal terms stay in the signed agreement):
- **Decision rights:** from day one, the buyer decides. The seller advises in private, never contradicts the buyer in front of staff or customers.
- **Referral rule:** when staff or customers ask the seller to decide, the seller says "check with [buyer]".
- **Access:** what the seller keeps access to (email, bank, systems) and the date each ends.
- **Check-ins:** set time each week; outside it, by phone for urgent items.

### When the seller won't let go
1. Name it privately and specifically: "On Tuesday you told the crew to redo the job I'd approved."
2. Restate the decision rights and the referral rule.
3. Reduce on-site time; shift to scheduled calls.
4. Remove remaining system access early, if the agreement allows (ask counsel).
5. If it continues, ask counsel what the transition agreement provides.

### When the seller disappears
1. Check the agreed duties and hours in the signed agreement.
2. Ask for the specific items still owed, with dates, in writing.
3. Prioritize what only the seller can give (key introductions, license transfers, passwords) over general help.
4. If it continues, ask counsel; if a seller note exists, ask counsel whether and how it is affected. Don't withhold a payment without counsel's advice.

### Signs the handover is working
Staff bring questions to the buyer first; top customers have called the buyer directly; the buyer has run payroll, pricing and scheduling alone; the seller's on-site days are down to the plan.
