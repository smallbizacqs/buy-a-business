# Industry scoring

## Criteria, default weights and anchors

Score 1 (poor for this buyer) to 5 (strong). Weights total 100; adjust two or three to the user.

| # | Criterion | Default weight | 5 looks like | 1 looks like |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Repeat or recurring revenue | 15 | Contracts or routine service visits; customers buy every month or season | One-off projects; every sale is a new hunt |
| 2 | Customer spread | 10 | No customer above a small share of revenue | One or two customers are most of revenue |
| 3 | Owner dependence (typical for the type) | 15 | Staff deliver and sell; owner manages | Owner is the product, the license or the main seller |
| 4 | Fit with the buyer's edge | 15 | Buyer knows the customers, the trade or the license | No connection at all |
| 5 | Labor availability | 10 | Roles are easy to hire and train locally | Scarce licensed trades, high turnover |
| 6 | Capital intensity | 5 | Little equipment to replace | Heavy, aging fleet or plant |
| 7 | Cyclicality | 10 | Demand holds in a downturn (needs, not wants; mandated; break-fix) | Discretionary, tied to new construction or consumer spending |
| 8 | Technology and AI exposure | 10 | Physical, local, regulated or relationship-based work | Core service is information work software could do |
| 9 | Financeability | 5 | Lenders see many of these; hard assets or steady history | Lenders rarely finance the type, or revenue is hard to verify |
| 10 | Deal availability nearby | 5 | Many owners near retirement; listings appear regularly | Rare locally |

Weighted score = Σ(score × weight) ÷ Σ(weights), giving a 1 to 5 figure. Show the arithmetic for the top row.

Typical adjustments:
- Keeping a job or planning manager-led: raise #3 to 20 or 25.
- Licensed professional (engineer, nurse, CPA): raise #4.
- Little equity: raise #6 and #9.
- Dislikes managing hourly staff: raise #5 and score field-labor businesses lower on #4.

## Long-list starting points

Pick rows that match the user's must-haves; these are categories, not recommendations.

- **Home and commercial services** (HVAC, plumbing, electrical, pest control, pool service, landscaping, cleaning): often repeat work and need-based demand; labor and licensing vary by trade and state.
- **Business services** (bookkeeping and accounting, insurance agency, payroll, IT managed services, staffing): recurring relationships; owner dependence and licensing can be high.
- **Healthcare-adjacent** (dental, veterinary, home health, therapy practices): steady demand; heavy regulation and licensing, and ownership rules for licensed practices vary by state. A lawyer confirms who may own the practice.
- **Light industrial** (manufacturing, distribution, logistics): customer concentration and capital intensity need a close look.
- **Asset-heavy local** (car wash, laundromat, self-storage): fewer staff; equipment, real estate and lease terms drive results.
- **Online** (e-commerce, software, content): can be remote; platform dependence and verifying revenue need extra care; lender appetite varies, so ask lenders early.

## First-time-buyer cautions (explain, don't ban)

| Caution | Why it's harder for a first deal | What would make it acceptable |
|---|---|---|
| Restaurants and most retail | Thin margins, lease risk, high turnover, earnings swing with local spending | Real operating experience; a long lease; earnings that cover debt with room to spare |
| Owner is the license holder | The business may not be able to operate without a licensed owner or manager | Buyer holds the license, or a licensed manager is staying under a written agreement; a lawyer confirms the rule |
| Heavy customer concentration | Losing one account can break debt coverage | Long contracts that transfer; price that reflects the risk; seller financing that shares it |
| Project-based with no backlog | Revenue resets each year | Documented backlog and repeat clients |
| Turnarounds and declining revenue | Fixing and learning at once, with debt | Experience in the industry; price based on current, not past, earnings |
| Single platform or supplier dependence | A policy change can cut revenue overnight | Diversified channels; contracts |
| Fads and trends | Demand may not last the loan term | Years of steady history through cycles |
| Heavily regulated billing (some healthcare) | Compliance errors can mean repayment demands | Specialist diligence; buyer experience |

## Resilience questions (top five rows)

1. Did businesses of this type keep revenue through the last downturn? (Ask owners; look for the seller's own numbers through those years.)
2. Is the service a need, a mandate (inspection, compliance) or a want?
3. Could software or AI do the core work, or the part customers pay most for, within the loan term? If plausibly yes, run `ai-disruption-risk-check`.
4. Does a large competitor or a platform control access to customers?
5. Can prices rise with costs, or are they fixed by contracts or insurers?

## Why "boring" businesses (for the user who asks)

Practitioners use "boring" for steady, unglamorous local businesses that customers need, with years of history, often owned by someone near retirement. The appeal: predictable demand, fewer competing buyers than trendy sectors, and room to improve basic operations. The risks don't disappear: labor shortages, owner dependence and deferred spending are common. Treat it as a description of what to look for, not a promise of returns.
