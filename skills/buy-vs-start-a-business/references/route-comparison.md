# Route comparison: buy, start, franchise resale, new franchise

Use this to fill the table in `acquisition/decide/buy-vs-start.md`. Score each factor for this user as **fits**, **mixed** or **poor**, with one line of reason. The patterns below are general; the user's facts override them.

## The eight factors

| Factor | Buy an independent business | Start from scratch | Franchise resale | New franchise unit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Time to owner pay | Cash flow exists on day one, but debt payments come first; see `business-owner-take-home-pay` | Usually the longest: no revenue until you build it | Cash flow exists on day one (if the unit is profitable) | Ramp-up period before the unit earns; ask existing franchisees how long theirs took |
| Cash needed | Equity for the down payment plus closing costs and working capital | Whatever the build costs until breakeven, plus your living costs | Down payment plus franchisor transfer fee and any required remodel | Franchise fee, build-out, equipment, opening inventory, working capital |
| How it can be financed | Lenders can lend against the business's history of cash flow, often with a seller note | Hard to borrow against an idea; mostly savings, friends and family, or investors | Like buying a business, plus franchisor approval | Some lenders finance new units; terms depend on the brand's record and your collateral |
| Main risk | Paying for earnings that don't last; inheriting hidden problems; debt | Nobody wants the product at a price that works | Same as buying, plus the franchisor's rules and fees | The location or the brand underperforms; you carry the build-out cost |
| What you inherit | Customers, staff, systems, reputation, and the problems that came with them | Nothing: a blank page | Customers and staff plus the franchise system | The system and brand, but no customers |
| Skills it rewards | Managing people, keeping customers, steady improvement | Selling, building, living with uncertainty | Running a system someone else designed | Opening and marketing a location, following a playbook |
| Control | Full | Full | Limited by the franchise agreement | Limited by the franchise agreement |
| Exit | Can sell again later if it keeps its value | Value only if you build something durable | Sale needs franchisor approval; the agreement term matters | Same as resale, once established |

## Which route fits whom (decisive questions)

Ask these in order; the first strong answer usually decides.

1. **Do you need a salary within about a year?** A strong yes points away from starting from scratch and from a new franchise unit, toward buying an established business or a resale.
2. **Would you sign a personal guarantee on a large loan?** A firm no points away from a loan-financed purchase. Options left: a smaller deal funded mostly with equity, a start-up, or investor-backed paths (see `search-fund-vs-sba-loan`).
3. **Do you light up at building and selling something new, or at fixing something that works?** Builders often chafe at owning someone else's business; improvers often stall on a blank page.
4. **Do you want a playbook?** If yes and you accept rules and ongoing fees, consider a franchise. If you want full control of prices, suppliers and branding, an independent business fits better.
5. **Do you have an edge in a specific industry** (relationships, a license, deep know-how)? A real edge makes starting more realistic; without one, buying lets you rent the previous owner's.

## Why people pay a multiple for an existing business

Write this for the user in plain words when they ask "why not just build it":

- You buy proof that customers pay, at a known margin, today.
- A lender can underwrite that history; it cannot underwrite an idea the same way.
- You get time back: years of building customers, staff and systems.
- What you pay for: the risk that earnings were overstated or depend on the seller. That is what evaluation and diligence are for (see `sde-and-add-backs-analysis`, later).

## Franchise checklist (for either franchise route)

Documents to get and have a lawyer read:

- The franchisor's disclosure document and the franchise agreement. Note the term, renewal, fees (initial, royalty, marketing fund, technology), required purchases and territory.
- For a resale: the transfer section of the agreement (approval, transfer fee, training required, any remodel or upgrade the franchisor can require on transfer), and the seller's unit-level financials.

Questions to ask (the user asks; never contact anyone without their OK):

- Ask current and former franchisees: how long until you paid yourself, what you'd do differently, how the franchisor treats owners in a dispute.
- Ask the franchisor: how many units opened and closed in recent years, and whether there is pending litigation with franchisees.
- Ask yourself: after royalties and fees, does the unit's cash flow still cover debt service and a salary?

## What ETA means (for the user who asks)

Entrepreneurship through acquisition (ETA) means becoming a business owner by buying an existing company instead of starting one. The path usually runs: decide, pick how to finance the search and the deal, search for a business, evaluate it, make an offer, confirm everything in diligence, close, and run it. This repo's ten stages follow that path.

## Common mistakes to flag

- Comparing a startup's best case with an acquisition's worst case (or the reverse). Compare like with like.
- Ignoring the buyer's living costs during a startup's ramp-up or a search.
- Treating a franchise as low-risk because it has a brand. Unit economics and the agreement decide.
- Assuming an acquisition pays the full previous owner's earnings to the new owner from day one. Debt service comes first.
