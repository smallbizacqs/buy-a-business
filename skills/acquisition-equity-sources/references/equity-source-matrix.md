## ROBS in plain words (explain, then hand to advisors)

A rollover for business start-ups (ROBS) is a structure in which a new corporation sets up a retirement plan, the buyer rolls existing retirement savings into it, and the plan buys stock in the corporation, which then uses the cash to buy the business. People use it to fund equity without a loan or early-withdrawal taxes.

Risks to name: the retirement savings ride on one small business; the arrangement has ongoing plan administration and compliance obligations; mistakes can have tax consequences; the corporation form and plan rules affect how the owner is paid and how other employees are treated. Whether a lender will count ROBS funds as equity is the lender's call (our sources don't cover it).

Stop there. A CPA and an ERISA attorney review before any step; don't describe how to set one up.

## "Little or no money down" on the user's numbers

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Total project cost (price + costs + working capital) | P |
| SBA minimum equity, initial acquisition = 10% × P | E |
| Most from limited sources (standby seller note, standby debt, minority equity) = 50% × E | L |
| Minimum from unlimited sources (unborrowed cash, gift, qualifying personal loan) = E − L | U |

Explain: U is at least 5% of P with an SBA loan on an initial acquisition, and the lender may require more. Without an SBA loan, a deal can be financed with more seller money or investor money, but the buyer usually pays for it with a higher price, less ownership, less control or more personal risk. Claims of "no money down" usually mean someone else's money is at risk in exchange for something.

## SBA, conventional, mezzanine (if asked)

| | SBA 7(a) | Conventional bank or cash-flow loan | Mezzanine or private credit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who it suits | Smaller deals, buyers with limited equity | Larger, steadier businesses; stronger buyers | Larger deals with a gap between senior debt and equity |
| Equity required | At least 10% of project cost on an initial acquisition (sourced) | The bank's policy; ask | Sits between debt and equity; investors still expect buyer equity |
| Term | Up to 10 years amortization for the business, no balloon (`sba-sop-50-10-8-1-change-of-ownership-term`) | Often shorter; ask | Often interest-only with a maturity date; ask |
| Guarantee | 20%+ owners give an unlimited guaranty (`sba-sop-50-10-8-1-personal-guaranty`) | Negotiable; ask | Varies; may include equity warrants rather than guarantees |
| Cost | Ask (rates and fees aren't in our sources) | Ask | Higher than senior debt; ask |
| Note | The lender certifies the borrower can't get the credit on reasonable terms without SBA help (`sba-sop-50-10-8-1-credit-elsewhere`) | Fewer program rules | Adds a lender with its own rights; a lawyer reviews the intercreditor terms |

## Questions for the lender

1. Exactly how much equity do you require for this deal, and on what base?
2. How do you treat a gift? A HELOC repaid from my spouse's salary? A family loan? ROBS funds? A 401(k) loan?
3. How long must cash be in my account, and what statements do you need?
4. Will you count a full-standby seller note, and minority investor money, up to the half limit?
5. Which diligence report costs count, and what proof do you need?

## Questions for a CPA and an ERISA attorney (retirement funds)

1. Is a ROBS or a 401(k) loan possible with my plans, and what are the risks and ongoing obligations?
2. What happens if the business fails, or if I make an administrative mistake?
3. How does the structure affect how I'm paid and how employees are treated?
4. What does it cost to set up and maintain?
