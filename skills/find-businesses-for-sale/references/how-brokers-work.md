# How business brokers work, and whose side they are on

## The basics

- A sell-side business broker is usually hired by the seller under a listing agreement and is usually paid a success fee when the deal closes, often with a minimum fee. Ask the broker how they're paid if it matters to the user; arrangements vary.
- That means the broker works for the seller. They want a buyer who will close, at a price the seller accepts, without surprises. They are not the buyer's advisor, even when friendly and helpful.
- Brokers usually value their listings themselves or with the seller. The asking price reflects the seller's hopes and the broker's judgment of what will sell, not an independent appraisal.
- Brokers typically control access: NDA, buyer profile, sometimes proof of funds, then the CIM (confidential information memorandum), then calls or a meeting with the owner.
- Licensing for business brokers varies by state; some states require a real estate license when real estate is involved. Not something to rely on as a quality signal either way.

## What a broker needs from a buyer

- Evidence the buyer can close: funds, financing path, a clear buy box.
- Speed and responsiveness: reading the CIM promptly, asking good questions, giving a clear yes or no.
- No time-wasting: not asking for everything before showing seriousness.

`business-broker-relations` covers how to show this and drafts the first note.

## Why many listings don't sell

Common reasons, each a question for the buyer rather than a verdict:

| Reason | What it tells the buyer |
|---|---|
| Asking price above what earnings can support with debt | Expect a price negotiation, seller financing, or an earnout question. See `small-business-valuation`. |
| Records too thin for a lender or buyer to rely on | Financing may be hard; proof of cash becomes central (`verify-seller-financials`). |
| Owner is the business (relationships, license, skills) | Transition risk; longer seller involvement needed. |
| Declining results | Ask for monthly data; why now? |
| Seller not truly ready (emotionally or financially) | Deals stall late. Ask about life after the sale (`business-owner-outreach`). |
| Industry hard to finance or hard to staff | Lender appetite and labor are the issue, not the business. |

A long-listed business can still be a good deal for the right buyer, often at different terms. Ask the broker directly: "What's held buyers back so far?"

## Buy-side advisors

Some buyers pay an advisor to find or negotiate deals for them. There is no recommendation here; questions to ask any candidate:

- How are you paid (retainer, monthly fee, success fee, from the buyer or the seller)? Could you be paid by both sides?
- Which deals in my size and industry have you sourced or closed in the past two years, and can I talk to those buyers?
- What will you do that I can't do myself with brokers and outreach?
- Who owns the relationships and leads if we stop working together?
- What are the exclusivity terms and how do I end the agreement?

A lawyer should review any advisor engagement letter before the user signs it.
