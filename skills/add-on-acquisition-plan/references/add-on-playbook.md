# Add-on playbook

## A. Readiness gates

| Gate | Pass looks like | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| 1. The first business runs without you | You've been away (or hands-off) for two or more weeks and the scorecard held | Scorecard, owner-dependence score (`professionalize-small-business`) |
| 2. Earnings are stable or rising | Last 12 months at or above the year you bought, explained if not | Monthly financials |
| 3. Debt is comfortable | Debt service covered with room to spare, as the lender measures it; no covenant near its limit | Loan documents, monthly covenant test |
| 4. Reporting is monthly and on time | Close by a set day each month; a cash forecast exists | Close calendar |
| 5. Cash for a second deal | A second down payment, closing costs and a reserve, without draining the first business's working capital | Cash forecast; personal balance sheet |
| 6. Consents are known | You know what the current lender, any investors and any seller note holder must approve | Loan documents, operating agreement (ask counsel) |
| 7. A leader for each company | Named person running daily operations of each business | Org chart |
| 8. Your own capacity | Family, health and time can take a second integration | The user's own judgment |

A fail is a reason to fix first or to wait, with a date to recheck.

## B. Roll-up logic and failure modes

### Where value can come from (each is a hypothesis to test)
- **Shared overhead:** one office, one bookkeeper, one dispatcher, one insurance program. Test: list each cost and who would actually do the combined work.
- **Density:** more customers in the same territory lowers drive time per job. Test: map both customer bases.
- **Cross-selling:** selling one company's services to the other's customers. Test: how many customers would plausibly buy, at what price.
- **Purchasing:** better supplier terms at higher volume. Test: ask suppliers for quotes at combined volume.
- **Management depth:** a stronger team than either company could afford alone.
- **Exit value:** a larger, less owner-dependent company may attract more buyers. This is the "multiple arbitrage" idea: buy small at lower multiples, sell larger at a higher one. It depends on future market conditions and on actually integrating; don't put it in a model as a certainty.

### How roll-ups go wrong
- Buying the next company before the last one is integrated.
- The owner spending all their time on deals while operations drift.
- Debt on top of debt, with coverage thin across the group.
- Paying up for add-ons because "the platform multiple will be higher".
- Integration that stays on paper: two systems, two price lists, two cultures, years later.
- Losing the add-on's key people or customers by changing too much, too fast.
- Rebranding before customers trust the new owner.
- Underestimating the cost of shared services (a controller, HR, IT).

## C. Integration plan (first 90 days after an add-on closes)

| Area | Days 1–30 | Days 31–60 | Days 61–90 |
|---|---|---|---|
| People | Announce with the seller; one-to-ones; name who leads the add-on | Align pay and benefits plan (with counsel and CPA) | Combined org chart in use |
| Customers | Top customers called; nothing visible changes | Cross-sell to a test group | Measure retention vs baseline |
| Finance | Add-on's bank and payroll under control; owner approvals | Combined monthly reporting; chart of accounts mapped | Shared bookkeeping if planned |
| Operations | Learn how they work; don't change what works | Pick one or two processes to align | Shared scheduling or purchasing if planned |
| Systems | Access and passwords under control | Decide which systems to keep | Migration plan with dates |
| Brand | Keep the add-on's name | Decide: keep, co-brand or rebrand, and when | Execute only after customers know the new owner |
| Thesis | Baseline each synergy measure | First readings | Report: holding, lagging or wrong |

Integration rule of thumb: control (money, payroll, approvals) early; customer-facing change late.

## D. Holding company operations

What a holdco usually does: allocates capital; hires and oversees each company's leader; runs shared finance, HR, insurance and sometimes IT; sets the reporting standard; manages debt and lender relationships.

What it usually doesn't do: run each company's daily operations.

Reporting from each company: monthly financials on one chart of accounts, a common scorecard, cash forecast, covenant tests.

Questions for a lawyer and a CPA: entity structure (holdco owning operating companies), management fees between companies, guarantees across companies, how existing loans and seller notes treat a new parent company, investor rights, tax filing structure.

## E. AI-enabled roll-ups

The idea: buy service businesses and lower costs or grow faster with software and AI, then combine them. Open questions to test for a specific target: which tasks software would take on, what that costs to implement, whether customers accept it, whether savings appear in the numbers within a year, and how the business itself is exposed to AI (route to `ai-disruption-risk-check`). Practitioners disagree on how much of the promised saving shows up in practice; present the views, don't endorse.
