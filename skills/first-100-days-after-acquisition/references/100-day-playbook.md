# 100-day playbook

## Phase 1: listen (days 1–30)

### Checklist
- [ ] Employee announcement done with the seller (`seller-transition-plan`).
- [ ] One-to-one with every employee in the first two to three weeks (smaller teams: everyone in week one).
- [ ] Work at least one full shift or ride-along in each core role.
- [ ] Visit or call the top customers with the seller, in revenue order.
- [ ] Call key suppliers: introduce yourself, confirm terms, ask what they see.
- [ ] Sit with the bookkeeper: how invoicing, collections, bills and payroll actually run.
- [ ] Read the last 12 months of bank statements, the aged receivables and payables, and the job or order records.
- [ ] Walk the facility, vehicles and equipment with whoever maintains them.
- [ ] Start the parked-ideas list; write ideas down instead of acting on them.

### Questions for one-to-ones
1. What's your job, in your words? What does a good week look like?
2. What should I definitely not change?
3. What slows you down or frustrates you most?
4. Which customers are easiest and hardest, and why?
5. If you owned the place, what's the first thing you'd fix?
6. What did [seller] do that you'll miss? What won't you miss?
7. Is there anything you were promised that I should know about?
8. How do you like to hear feedback?

Take notes; repeat back what you heard in a later team meeting so people see they were heard.

### Questions for top customers
1. Why did you start working with us, and why do you stay?
2. What do we do better or worse than others you've used?
3. Anything coming up we should know about (volume changes, new needs, bids)?
4. Who do you deal with day to day, and are they the right person?

## Phase 2: stabilize (days 31–60)

### Money controls (small-business basics)
- [ ] The owner, not the bookkeeper alone, has admin access to every bank account and the accounting system.
- [ ] Bank reconciliation monthly, reviewed by the owner.
- [ ] Two people involved in paying bills: one enters, the owner approves (or the owner reviews every payment weekly).
- [ ] Payroll changes (new hires, raises, terminations) approved by the owner in writing.
- [ ] Owner reviews the bank activity weekly for unknown payees.
- [ ] Cash handling: daily count and deposit log, two-person count where cash is material.
- [ ] Credit cards: who holds them, limits, monthly review of statements.
- [ ] Vendor master list cleaned: remove the seller's personal accounts and stale vendors; changes to vendor bank details verified by phone.
- [ ] Monthly close: P&L and balance sheet by a set day of the following month.
- [ ] Lender reporting calendar set from the loan documents.
- [ ] Cash forecast for the next 13 weeks, updated weekly, with loan payments and payroll on it.

### Weekly numbers sheet

Pick five to eight numbers. Each needs a definition, a source and an owner. Start with cash and leading indicators, not just last month's profit.

Always:
- Cash in the bank (and the 13-week low point in the forecast).
- Sales or revenue booked this week.
- Collections this week, and receivables over 60 days.
- Gross margin on completed jobs or orders (where measurable weekly).

By business type (choose what fits):
| Type | Useful weekly numbers |
|---|---|
| Home or trade services | Leads, booked jobs, close rate, average ticket, technician utilization, callbacks or rework, membership or maintenance agreements sold |
| Recurring service (cleaning, pest, pool, landscaping maintenance) | Active accounts, new and cancelled accounts, route density or revenue per crew-day, missed visits |
| Distribution or manufacturing | Orders, backlog, on-time delivery, inventory on hand vs target, scrap or returns |
| Professional practice (accounting, insurance, dental, vet) | Billable or production hours, new clients or patients, retention, collections rate, appointments booked ahead |
| Retail or food | Sales by day, transactions, average ticket, labor as share of sales, cost of goods, shrink |
| Online or software | New customers, churn, monthly recurring revenue, support tickets, refund rate |

## Phase 3: improve (days 61–100)

- Pick at most three improvements from the parked list. Prefer ones employees suggested, that are cheap to reverse, and that customers won't feel negatively.
- For each: the problem, the change, the measure, the owner, the date, and what would make you undo it.
- Share the plan with the team; credit the people whose idea it was.

## Pricing worksheet

1. Current price list, and the date each price last changed.
2. Cost increases since then (labor, materials, insurance, fuel), with sources.
3. Competitor prices, with where each came from and the date.
4. Customers on contracts or quotes with locked prices, and when they renew.
5. Break-even customer loss: with a price increase of p (as a fraction), revenue stays flat if no more than p ÷ (1 + p) of revenue leaves. Example: a 10% increase breaks even if no more than about 9.1% of revenue walks away. Gross profit stays flat if no more than p ÷ (m + p) of volume leaves, where m is the gross margin as a fraction of the old price (10% increase, 30% margin: about 25%). Show both if the user has margin data.
6. Options: new customers and new jobs first; existing customers at renewal with written notice; tiered or minimum charges; charging for things given away free.
7. Communication: say what's changing, from when, and why, in plain terms; give reasonable notice; brief employees first so they can answer questions.
8. The user decides. Record the decision, the date and the notice in `decisions.md`.

Practitioners differ on timing: some say never touch prices in the first six months; others raise early when the business is clearly under-priced and costs have risen. Show both; don't pick.

## Leadership habits for a new owner

- Be visible: in the field, on the floor, at the start of shifts.
- Make small promises and keep every one. Don't promise raises, roles or investments you haven't decided.
- Explain decisions, especially ones that change routines.
- Don't criticize the seller or how things were done; ask why instead.
- Recognize good work specifically and publicly.
- Handle the first hard people decision carefully and privately; the team watches how you treat the first person who struggles.
- Protect your own time and health: the first year is long. (`acquired-business-operating-system` covers peer groups and burnout.)

## Common ways the first 100 days go wrong

- Changing prices, people and systems at once, so nobody can tell which change caused what.
- Losing a key employee who wasn't asked what they needed.
- A top customer leaving because nobody called them.
- Cash running short because the working-capital reserve was too thin or a big receivable didn't come in.
- The owner doing every job personally and having no time to manage.
