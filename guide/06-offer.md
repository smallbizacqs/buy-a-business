You're here when you've sized up a business, you want to buy it, and it's time to put terms in writing. The letter of intent (LOI) is a short letter, mostly non-binding, that sets the price, how it's paid, the deal structure, the working capital the business must keep, and a period when the seller stops talking to other buyers. Everything you leave vague here gets negotiated later, after you've spent money on diligence, so this stage is where you also decide your structure and line up your advisors.

## The letter of intent

The LOI sets out the main terms and says which few clauses, such as exclusivity (also called a no-shop: the seller agrees not to negotiate with other buyers for a set time) and confidentiality, bind both sides. Settle every term that moves money, and size the exclusivity period to your real timeline for diligence and the loan. Some buyers start from a free template, such as the [letter of intent at openagreements.org](https://openagreements.org/templates/common-paper-letter-of-intent).

## Deal structure

Structure decides what you're buying (the company's assets, or its shares), which liabilities come with it, and how and when the seller is paid. It also drives taxes for both sides and what a lender will finance: an SBA 7(a) loan (a bank loan partly guaranteed by the U.S. Small Business Administration) doesn't allow seller earnouts, for example. Work out the structure with your CPA and lawyer before you sign the LOI.

## Working capital

Net working capital is the money tied up in day-to-day operations: mostly receivables and inventory, minus payables. The price assumes a normal amount stays in the business, so the LOI should say how the target (the peg) is set and how the price is trued up after closing. "Cash-free, debt-free" means the seller keeps the cash and pays off the debt, and the fight is over what counts as debt.

## Negotiation

Many small-business sellers are owners selling the one business they built, and it is often a big part of their life. Price is only one lever: how much is paid at closing, the seller's role, and the timeline often matter as much to them. Keeping the deal moving, with visible progress, is part of negotiating well.

## Your advisors

Buyers typically work with a lawyer on the purchase agreement, a CPA for the numbers and taxes, and sometimes a provider for a quality of earnings report (an accountant's independent check of the earnings). Some buyers handle small deals with templates; buyers who hire counsel can look for AI-using law firms with fixed fees that are lean and run by an attorney with big-law experience. Ask every advisor what the fee covers and how many deals like yours they've done.
